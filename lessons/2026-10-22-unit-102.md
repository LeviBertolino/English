### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** I sat through the vendor demo this morning.
> **Levi:** And? Were you convinced?
> **CEO:** Honestly, I was a bit **bored**. The tech is **impressive**, but the pitch was **confusing** — twenty slides and I still couldn't tell what we'd actually buy.
> **Levi:** I was **frustrated** too. It's a **promising** platform, but the team isn't ready to sell to a board. I'm **interested** in the capability, not **excited** about the partner.
> **CEO:** Agreed. A **disappointing** meeting for a genuinely **exciting** market.

Notice the pairs: the **pitch** was *confusing* → the CEO felt *bored* and *frustrated*. The **market** is *exciting* → Levi is *interested*, not *excited*. One form points at the thing; the other points at the person.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Many adjectives come in a pair built from the same verb (*to interest, to bore, to excite, to confuse*):

| Form | Points at | Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **-ing** | the **thing / situation / person that CAUSES the feeling** | *What is it like?* | The meeting was **boring**. · an **exciting** market |
| **-ed** | the **person who HAS the feeling** (their reaction) | *How do you feel?* | I was **bored**. · I'm **excited** about it |

Common pairs you'll use weekly:

| Causes it (*-ing*) | Feels it (*-ed*) |
|---|---|
| interesting | interested |
| exciting | excited |
| confusing | confused |
| frustrating | frustrated |
| disappointing | disappointed |
| surprising | surprised |
| tiring | tired |
| boring | bored |
| worrying | worried |
| impressive* / promising* | *(these have no everyday -ed partner)* |

- Both are **adjectives** — same positions as any adjective: before a noun (*a confusing roadmap*) or after *be / look / seem / feel* (*the roadmap is confusing · I feel confused*).
- The *-ed* one is usually followed by a preposition when you add what caused it: **interested in**, **excited about**, **worried about**, **surprised at/by**, **disappointed with/by**, **bored with/by**, **frustrated with/by**.

**Fast test:** ask *"Is it the cause, or the reaction?"* A report, a market, a decision, a person who creates the feeling → **-ing**. A human being reporting their own state → **-ed**.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this pair exist?**
English splits one idea into two words so you can separate the **source** of a feeling from the **experience** of it. *Boring* and *bored* both come from *bore*, but they look in opposite directions: *boring* looks **outward** at what causes the feeling; *bored* looks **inward** at the person having it. That lets you say two completely different things with the same root — *"The CFO is boring"* (he puts others to sleep) vs *"The CFO is bored"* (he's the one yawning). Choosing the wrong one doesn't just sound off; it can reverse your meaning.

**Decision rule — *-ing* vs *-ed* (the only question that matters):**
Ask: **is this word describing the cause, or the person who feels it?**
- **The thing that makes you feel something → -ing.** *an exciting opportunity · a confusing slide · a frustrating process · a disappointing quarter.* (The opportunity isn't itself excited — it excites *you*.)
- **A person (or group) reporting their own reaction → -ed.** *I'm excited · the board was confused · investors are disappointed · I'm frustrated.*

Side-by-side minimal pairs — same root, opposite directions:
- *The roadmap is **confusing**.* (the roadmap creates confusion) ⇆ *The team is **confused**.* (the team feels it)
- *It was an **exciting** pitch.* (the pitch generated excitement) ⇆ *I was **excited** by the pitch.* (I felt it)
- *A **disappointing** result.* (the result lets people down) ⇆ *The investors were **disappointed**.* (they feel let down)
- *She's a **tiring** colleague.* (she wears others out) ⇆ *She's **tired**.* (she needs sleep)

The trap sentence that catches everyone: **"I'm boring"** means *"I am a dull person"* — not *"I'm bored."* If you mean *the meeting put me to sleep*, you must say **"I'm bored."**

**PT→EN mapping:**
- Portuguese often uses **one** form — the participle — for both ideas, or marks the difference with a different word, so the two-way split has no clean twin and Brazilians reach for the *-ed* form by default.
- *"Estou entediado"* → ✅ *I'm **bored*** (feeling). But *"A reunião é entediante / chata"* → ✅ *The meeting is **boring*** (cause). The *-ante* ending in PT (*interessante, empolgante, confuso→confuso é ambíguo, cansativo, frustrante, decepcionante*) is your signal that English wants **-ing**: *interess**ante** → interest**ing** · empolg**ante** → excit**ing** · frustr**ante** → frustrat**ing** · cans**ativo** → tir**ing** · decepcion**ante** → disappoint**ing**.* When PT ends in *-ado/-ido* (*empolgado, interessado, confuso, cansado, frustrado, decepcionado*) → English wants **-ed**.
- The preposition also betrays Levi: PT says *"interessado **em**"* and *"preocupado **com**"* — English keeps *interested **in*** (good match) but switches to *worried **about*** (not *worried with*).

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. **Using the *-ed* form (or saying "I'm [feeling]") when you mean the cause — the classic reversal:**
   - ❌ *"The presentation was very **interested**."* · ❌ *"It's a **bored** meeting."* · ❌ *"I'm **boring** in this call."*
   - ✅ *"The presentation was very **interesting**."* · ✅ *"It's a **boring** meeting."* · ✅ *"I'm **bored** in this call."* *(cause → -ing; your own feeling → -ed)*
2. **Using the *-ing* form to describe your own reaction:**
   - ❌ *"I'm very **exciting** about the roadmap."* · ❌ *"The board was **confusing** by the numbers."*
   - ✅ *"I'm very **excited** about the roadmap."* · ✅ *"The board was **confused** by the numbers."* *(a person feeling it → -ed; if the board itself causes confusion in others, then "the board is confusing" — but that's a different meaning)*
3. **Wrong preposition after the *-ed* form (PT interference):**
   - ❌ *"interested **on** AI"* · ❌ *"worried **with** the timeline"* · ❌ *"surprised **with** the result"* (often)
   - ✅ *"interested **in** AI"* · ✅ *"worried **about** the timeline"* · ✅ *"surprised **by/at** the result"* *(fixed pairs — memorise: interested* in*, excited* about*, worried* about*, surprised* by/at*)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **The AI market is genuinely exciting, but I'm not excited about this particular vendor.**
   *— Executive. Both forms in one line: the market CAUSES excitement (**exciting**); Levi reports his own reaction (**excited about**). The contrast is the whole lesson.*
2. **The board was confused by the pricing model — three tiers and no clear logic.**
   *— Executive. A group reporting its reaction → **-ed**. If the model itself is the problem you'd also say "the pricing model is **confusing**," but here the focus is how the board felt.*
3. **It was a disappointing quarter for the innovation portfolio; two of the four bets stalled.**
   *— Executive. The quarter CAUSES the let-down → **-ing** before the noun. Nobody is "disappointing" here — the result is.*
4. **I'm interested in the capability, not in the partner's roadmap.**
   *— Executive. Personal stance → **-ed**, plus the fixed preposition **interested in** (never "interested on"). This is the exact phrase from the mini-context.*
5. **The migration timeline is worrying, and frankly the whole team is worried about it.**
   *— Executive. The timeline generates worry (**worrying**); the people feel it (**worried about**). Note "about," not "with."*
6. **We should position this as a promising, high-growth category to the C-level.**
   *— Executive. **promising** = "full of promise," a cause-type -ing adjective describing the category. There's no "I'm promised" reaction partner for this meaning.*
7. **I was exhausted after the strategy offsite — three days of back-to-back sessions is tiring.**
   *— Everyday + work. Levi's own state → **exhausted/tired** (-ed); the schedule that drains him → **tiring** (-ing). Same split, two words.*
8. **The film was so boring that half the audience left; I've never been that bored.**
   *— Everyday. Textbook pair: the film CAUSES boredom (**boring**); the viewer FEELS it (**bored**). Say "I'm boring" only if you mean you're a dull person.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 101):**
1. Natural version: **"the fund we invested in"** — *in* moves to the **end** of the clause, and the relative pronoun (*which*) **disappears**. (The formal twin keeps both: *in which we invested*.)
2. **"all of whom"** (people → *whom*) and **"most of which"** (things → *which*). The folded quantifier clause needs commas (non-defining) and uses *of whom* for people, *of which* for things.
3. The preposition *with* is used **twice** — once fronted (*with whom*) and once at the end. Use it **once**: formal *"the vendor with whom we signed"* OR natural *"the vendor we signed with."*

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 095:** *"If I **were** you, I **wouldn't** lock us into a single vendor."* The unreal conditional requires **were** for all persons in the fixed *If I were you* advice phrase — *was* is wrong here because this is an imagined/contrary-to-fact situation (you are not the other person).
- **Unit 073:** **Yes**, drop it → *"The platform we chose cleared every gate."* The deciding factor: the pronoun is droppable only when it's the **object** of the relative clause (a new subject — *we* — follows it). If a verb followed directly, the pronoun would be the subject and must stay.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **confusing** — the presentation is the **cause** of confusion → -ing. (The people would be *confused*.)
2. Error: **interesting → interested**. "I'm **interested in** leading…" — you're reporting your own feeling (-ed), and the fixed preposition is *in*, not *on*.
3. **exhausted** — you are the person **feeling** it → -ed. (The hour of grilling was *exhausting*.)
4. **worried about** — the CFO is a person reporting a feeling → -ed; the fixed preposition is *about*, never *with*. (The burn rate itself is *worrying*.)

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Muitos adjetivos vêm em par a partir do mesmo verbo (*interest → interesting / interested*). A forma **-ing** aponta para a **coisa que causa** o sentimento — a reunião, o mercado, o roadmap (*an exciting market, a confusing slide*) — e responde "como é isso?". A forma **-ed** aponta para a **pessoa que sente** — você, o board, os investidores (*I'm excited, the board was confused*) — e responde "como você se sente?". Teste rápido: é a causa ou é a reação? Causa → *-ing*; reação → *-ed*.

A armadilha clássica do brasileiro é inverter os dois, porque o português costuma usar só o particípio: *"I'm boring"* significa *"sou uma pessoa chata"*, não *"estou entediado"* (isso é *"I'm bored"*). Use a pista do português: terminações *-ante/-ativo* (interessante, frustrante, cansativo) viram **-ing**; terminações *-ado/-ido* (empolgado, frustrado, cansado) viram **-ed**. E cuidado com a preposição fixa depois da forma *-ed*: é *interested **in***, *worried **about***, *excited **about***, *surprised **by/at*** — nunca *interested on* nem *worried with*.
