### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Be honest — are we moving fast enough on AI?
> **You:** No. **If we went all-in now, we'd lead the category by 2027.** Today we're hedging.
> **CFO:** All-in is risky. What would that even cost?
> **You:** **If the board doubled the budget, I'd hire two senior ML leads next quarter.** We can't with what we have.
> **CEO:** And the vendor route?
> **You:** **If I were you, I wouldn't lock us into one vendor** — it would cost us flexibility later.
> **CFO:** So you're saying we're playing it too safe.
> **You:** **If we weren't, I wouldn't be raising it.**

Yesterday every *if* was a **real** plan — *if the board approves* (it might, and then we act). Today the *if* turns **imaginary**. Listen to the shape: *If we **went** all-in … we'**d** lead* — the *if*-part is in the **past** (*went*), yet nobody is talking about the past. That past tense is doing something strange: it marks the whole thing as **unreal** — a hypothesis, not a plan. And the other half isn't *will*, it's **would**. Same two-halves structure as yesterday, but shifted one step back into the imaginary.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

An unreal (hypothetical) condition also has **two halves**, each one step "back" from the real version:

| Half | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **if** + condition | **past simple** (never *would*) | *If we **went** all-in …* |
| result | **would / could / might** + base verb | *… we'**d** lead the market.* |

Compare the two conditionals side by side — the only change is one step back in each half:

| | if-part | result | meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **real** (Unit 94) | *If the board **approves*** | *we'**ll** move* | possible — it may really happen |
| **unreal** (today) | *If the board **approved*** | *we'**d** move* | imaginary — it probably won't / isn't so |

**Contractions:** *would* → *'d* (*we'd, I'd, they'd*); *would not* → *wouldn't*.

**The fixed phrase *If I were you*:** in unreal conditions, *be* usually becomes **were** for every subject — *if I **were**, if he **were**, if it **were***. *If I **were** you* is the standard way to give advice. (*If I was* is heard in casual speech, but *were* is the correct, professional form.)

Either half can come first; put a comma after the *if*-part only when it comes first.

The one unbreakable rule, exactly parallel to yesterday: **no *would* in the *if*-part.** ✅ *If we **went*** — ❌ *If we **would go***.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?**
It lets you talk about a situation that is **not real** — imaginary, unlikely, or simply contrary to the facts right now — and reason about its consequences. It's the language of **strategy under hypothesis**: war-gaming a bold move you're *not* making, pushing back with "what if," giving advice (*if I were you*), or naming a trade-off you've decided against. *If + past → would* quietly tells the listener: **this isn't a plan, it's a thought experiment.**

**Decision rule vs the rival — real (*if* + present → *will*) vs unreal (*if* + past → *would*):**
Both are *if*-sentences; the tense is the switch. Ask one question: **is this a real option on the table, or an imaginary / unlikely one?**

| | Form | Use when | Signal to the listener |
|---|---|---|---|
| **real** | *if* + **present**, **will** | the event is a genuine possibility | "this could actually happen — treat it as a live plan" |
| **unreal** | *if* + **past**, **would** | imaginary, unlikely, or contrary to fact now | "I'm hypothesizing — I'm *not* saying we'll do this" |

Side-by-side minimal pairs — same words, one step back changes everything:

- ***If the board approves** the budget, we'**ll** scale.* → scaling is a **live plan**; the vote is realistically coming.
- ***If the board approved** the budget, we'**d** scale.* → you're **imagining** it; maybe they won't, or you're war-gaming a scenario.

- *If we **hire** two ML leads, we **can** ship by Q2.* → a real, costed option.
- *If we **hired** two ML leads, we **could** ship by Q2.* → hypothetical — probably you can't hire them, so it's a "what if."

Choosing wrong **misleads your audience**: tell a CEO *"If we went all-in, we'd lead"* and they hear a bold hypothesis; tell them *"If we go all-in, we'll lead"* and they hear a recommendation you're ready to execute. The tense *is* the commitment level.

**PT→EN mapping:**
Portuguese builds this with **se + imperfeito do subjuntivo + condicional**: *"**Se** nós **fôssemos** all-in, nós **lideraríamos** o mercado."* The two mappings:
- The *if*-part *fôssemos / dobrasse / tivéssemos* (imperfect subjunctive) → English **past simple** (*went / doubled / had*).
- The result *lideraríamos / contrataria* (condicional, the *-ria* ending) → English **would + base verb** (*would lead / would hire*).

Where it betrays you: Portuguese marks the condition with a special subjunctive *form*, so your instinct is to mark the English *if*-part with *would* (the "conditional feeling"). English refuses — the *if*-part is a **plain past**, and *would* lives **only** in the result. This is the number-one error, and it's the same shape as yesterday's *"no will after if"* — just one tense deeper.

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. ***would* smuggled into the *if*-part** (the *-ria* echo):
   - ❌ *If we **would go** all-in, we'd lead the market.*
   - ✅ *If we **went** all-in, we'd lead the market.*
2. **using the *real* form for an imaginary / contrary-to-fact case:**
   - ❌ *If I **am** the CEO, I **will** kill that project.* (you are **not** the CEO — it's hypothetical)
   - ✅ *If I **were** the CEO, I **would** kill that project.*
3. ***if I was* where professional English wants *if I were*** (advice / hypothesis):
   - ❌ *If I **was** you, I wouldn't sign that contract.*
   - ✅ *If I **were** you, I wouldn't sign that contract.*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **If we went all-in on AI now, we'd lead the category by 2027.**
   *Executive. Pure hypothesis — you're war-gaming a move you're not making. Past (*went*) in the if-part, *would* ('d) in the result.*
2. **If the board doubled the AI budget, I'd hire two senior ML leads next quarter.**
   *Executive. Unlikely / not-yet-real, so unreal: compare the live-plan version "If the board doubles the budget, I'll hire…". The past *doubled* signals you don't expect it.*
3. **If I were you, I wouldn't lock us into a single vendor.**
   *Executive. The fixed advice phrase — *were*, not *was*. *wouldn't* carries the recommendation without ordering anyone.*
4. **If we weren't playing it safe, I wouldn't be raising this with the board.**
   *Executive. Contrary to fact: you *are* playing it safe. *weren't* (the *were* form, negative) states the opposite of reality.*
5. **If the market moved against us, we could pivot the portfolio within a quarter.**
   *Executive. *could* (not *would*) in the result = "we'd be able to"; the if-part stays past simple (*moved*). A capability under a hypothesis.*
6. **If I had more runway, I'd run three more pilots before committing.**
   *Executive. *had* (past) marks the shortage as the current reality you're reasoning against; *'d run* is the imagined consequence.*
7. **If I won the lottery, I'd take a year off and travel.**
   *Everyday. Classic unreal — winning is unlikely, so past (*won*) + *would*. Not a plan, a daydream.*
8. **If she asked me, I'd help without thinking twice.**
   *Everyday. Hypothetical request (she hasn't asked); *asked* + *'d help*. The real version "If she asks me, I'll help" would mean you expect her to ask.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Ask your tutor to run a **"war-game vs plan" switch drill**: they name a scenario and tag it *real* or *hypothetical* (*"the board votes next week — real"* / *"imagine we had triple the budget — hypothetical"*) and you must respond in the matching conditional on the spot — *"If they approve, we'll…"* vs *"If we had triple the budget, we'd…"* — until shifting the tense to match the commitment level is automatic.
2. Then an **"if I were you" advice round**: the tutor plays a peer executive bringing you a dilemma (vendor lock-in, a rushed hire, an over-ambitious roadmap) and you give advice using *"If I were you, I'd / I wouldn't…"*, forcing *were* (never *was*) and keeping *would* out of the *if*-part.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 094):**
1. *If the board **will approve*** → *If the board **approves***. The real-conditional *if*-part takes the **present simple**; *will* never goes after *if*.
2. ***If*** — *"**If** the round comes through, we'll double the team."* *If* signals the event is **uncertain** (funding isn't guaranteed); *when* would wrongly promise it's coming.
3. The present *approves* covers Portuguese *aprovar*; you must **not** add *will*. (*If the board **approves**…*)

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 088:** ✅ *"We discussed **the risk** openly with the CEO **at the office yesterday**."* Keep *discussed + the risk* glued (don't split with *openly*), and put **place before time** (*at the office* → *yesterday*), not time before place.
- **Unit 067:** ***a few*** — *"We have **a few** live pilots — only three."* *A few* = a small but **positive** amount ("some, enough"). Drop the *a* and *few pilots* flips to **negative** ("almost none, a shortage") — the opposite of your point.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ❌ *would double* → ✅ *If we **doubled** the budget, we'd ship…*. No *would* in the *if*-part; past simple only.
2. **Real:** *"If the board **approves**, we **will** start in Q1."* Sign-off is realistically expected, so the live-plan form (present + *will*), not the hypothetical past + *would*.
3. ***were*** — *"**If** I **were** in your position, I'd push harder."* Unreal condition → *were* for every subject; professional English avoids *was* here.
4. ✅ *"If I **were** the CFO, I **would** cut that line item tomorrow."* You're not the CFO, so it's unreal: *were* in the if-part, *would* (not *will*) in the result.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

A condicional **irreal** (hipotética) também tem duas metades, cada uma um passo "para trás" em relação à real: **if + passado simples** na condição (*If we **went** all-in…*) e **would / could / might + verbo** no resultado (*… we'**d** lead the market*). A regra inquebrável é a mesma de ontem, só que uma camada mais funda: **nunca use *would* depois de *if*** — o *if* leva passado puro, e o *would* mora **só** no resultado. Use essa forma quando a situação é **imaginária, improvável ou contrária ao fato agora** (um cenário de guerra, um "e se", um conselho). A real (*if + presente → will*) é para um plano que pode **de fato** acontecer; a irreal avisa o ouvinte: "estou hipotetizando, não estou propondo executar".

A armadilha principal vem direto do português: nós marcamos a condição com o **imperfeito do subjuntivo** (*se nós **fôssemos**, se o conselho **dobrasse***) e o resultado com o **condicional** (*nós **lideraríamos**, eu **contrataria***). O inglês mapeia o subjuntivo para **passado simples** (*went, doubled*) e o *-ria* para **would + verbo** — mas como o português usa uma forma especial na condição, a tentação é botar *would* no *if*-part (*"If we would go"*), que é o erro número um. Cuidado também com *If I **was** you*: a forma profissional e correta é *If I **were** you*, com *were* para todos os sujeitos.
