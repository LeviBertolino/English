### 1. 📘 Unit 105 — *so* and *such*: how to add emphasis without betraying your Portuguese

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Honest read — was the AI pivot worth it?
> **Levi:** It was **such a bold bet**, and the timing was **so tight**, that honestly I wasn't sure. But we hired **such talented people** that execution never slipped.
> **CFO:** The numbers moved **so fast** this quarter I almost didn't believe them.
> **Levi:** I know. It's **such a big swing** from last year that the board asked me to re-present it.
> **CEO:** Because it's **so important** we get the narrative right for investors.
> **Levi:** Exactly. It's **such an important story** that I'd rather over-prepare than wing it.

Look at what comes right after each **so** and each **such**. After **so** there's an **adjective or an adverb, alone** — *so tight*, *so fast*, *so important*. After **such** there's a **noun phrase** — *such a bold bet*, *such talented people*, *such an important story*. That single difference — *is there a noun, or just a describing word?* — decides everything in this unit. And notice the frequent tail: *… that we almost didn't believe them*. **so/such** often sets up a **result**.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Both **so** and **such** mean *very / to a high degree* and add emphasis. They are **not** interchangeable — the grammar after them is different.

| Pattern | What follows | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **so** + **adjective** | a describing word, **no noun** | *The timing was **so tight**.* |
| **so** + **adverb** | a describing word, **no noun** | *The numbers moved **so fast**.* |
| **so much / so many** + noun | a quantity word + noun | ***so much** pressure · **so many** risks* |
| **such (a/an)** + (adjective +) **noun** | a **noun phrase** | *It was **such a bold bet**.* · *They are **such talented people**.* |

**The one rule that settles it:** look at the word group right after.
- **just an adjective or adverb → `so`** ( *so important*, *so quickly* )
- **a noun is in there → `such`** ( *such an important decision* )

**The `a / an` detail (where Portuguese speakers slip):** with **such** + a *singular countable* noun you **must** keep *a/an*, and it sits **between** such and the adjective:
> **such** + **a/an** + (adjective) + **singular noun** → *such **a** bold bet*, *such **an** important story*
> **such** + (adjective) + **plural / uncountable noun** → *such **talented people**, **such good news*** (no *a*)

**The result pattern — `so / such … that`:** both can introduce a consequence.
> *It was **so tight that** I wasn't sure.* (so + adjective … that)
> *It was **such a big swing that** the board asked me to re-present.* (such + noun phrase … that)

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these two words exist — and why two of them?**
English splits the job of emphasis by **what you're emphasising**. If you're turning up a **quality** (an adjective or adverb standing alone), you use **so**. If you're turning up a **whole thing** (a noun, usually with an adjective attached), you use **such**. Portuguese collapses both into *tão* / *tamanho* / *que* and lets you emphasise freely — so the choice feels arbitrary to you, and the default *so* leaks into places that need *such*.

**Decision rule vs the rival (`so` ⇆ `such`):**
Ask one question — **is there a noun in the phrase I'm emphasising?**
- **No noun, just a describing word → `so`.** → *The decision was **so important**.* (important = adjective, alone)
- **Yes, there's a noun → `such`.** → *It was **such an important decision**.* (decision = noun)

Side-by-side minimal pairs — same meaning, the noun is the only thing that flips the word:
- *The bet was **so bold**.* (adjective alone) ⇆ *It was **such a bold bet**.* (adjective + **noun**)
- *The team is **so talented**.* ⇆ *They are **such a talented team** / **such talented people**.*
- *The story is **so important**.* ⇆ *It's **such an important story**.*
- *She presents **so well**.* (adverb — **always** *so*, never *such*) ⇆ *She's **such a strong presenter**.* (noun)

**Quantities — `so much` / `so many`, not `such`:** before a bare quantity + noun, English uses **so much** (uncountable) or **so many** (countable), not *such*:
- ✅ *There was **so much** pressure.* · ✅ *We faced **so many** risks.*
- (*such* reappears only with *a lot of*: *such a lot of pressure* — but *so much / so many* is the natural executive choice.)

**PT→EN mapping:**
- ***tão* + adjetivo/advérbio → `so`.** *tão importante → so important*, *tão rápido → so fast(ly)*. This direct line is safe.
- ***tão* / *um(a) … tão* + **substantivo** → `such`.** Here's the betrayal: *"foi uma decisão tão importante"* feels like *"it was a so important decision"* — but English **forbids** *so* before a noun phrase. It must be *"it was **such an** important decision."* The Portuguese *um(a) … tão …* word order (article, then noun, then *tão*) is exactly what produces the classic ❌ *"a so important decision."*
- ***tanto/tanta/tantos* + substantivo → `so much / so many`,** not *such*: *tanta pressão → so much pressure*, *tantos riscos → so many risks*.
- ***tão … que* / *um … tão … que* → `so … that` / `such … that`.** The *que* of result maps cleanly to *that* — the only trap is picking *so* vs *such* for the first half, same rule as always.

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. **`so` before a noun phrase (the #1 error):**
   - ❌ *"It was a so big decision."* · ❌ *"We have so good team."* · ❌ *"That's a so important point."*
   - ✅ *"It was **such a big** decision."* · ✅ *"We have **such a good** team."* · ✅ *"That's **such an important** point."* *(noun in the phrase → `such`, and keep the* a/an*)*
2. **`such` before a lone adjective/adverb:**
   - ❌ *"The timing was such tight."* · ❌ *"The results came in such fast."*
   - ✅ *"The timing was **so tight**."* · ✅ *"The results came in **so fast**."* *(no noun → `so`)*
3. **Dropping *a/an*, or using *such* for quantities:**
   - ❌ *"It was such good news for us."* is fine (uncountable, no *a*) — but ❌ *"such a good news"* and ❌ *"It was such important decision"* (singular countable, missing *an*) are wrong; and ❌ *"We had such many problems."*
   - ✅ *"It was **such important** news"* / *"**such an** important decision"*; ✅ *"We had **so many** problems."* *(singular countable → need* a/an*; bare quantity → so much/so many)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **It was such a bold bet that even the board hesitated at first.**
   *— Executive. *bet* is a noun → **such**, with *a* kept before the adjective *bold*. The *… that …* tail states the result. "A so bold bet" would be the classic error.*
2. **The market moved so fast this quarter that our forecast was outdated within weeks.**
   *— Executive. *fast* is an adverb standing alone → **so** (never *such fast*). Again *so … that …* sets up the consequence.*
3. **We've hired such talented people that execution has basically stopped being my worry.**
   *— Executive. *people* is a plural noun → **such**, and with a plural you drop the *a/an* (not "such a talented people").*
4. **Getting the investor narrative right is so important that I'd rather over-prepare.**
   *— Executive. *important* is an adjective alone → **so**. Compare example 8, where the same word rides a noun and flips to *such*.*
5. **There was so much pressure on the AI budget this year that we re-ran the model three times.**
   *— Executive. A bare quantity + uncountable noun (*pressure*) → **so much**, not *such*. For a countable noun it would be *so many risks*.*
6. **The quarterly results were so strong that the CFO double-checked the numbers.**
   *— Executive. *strong* is an adjective alone → **so**. "Such strong" is wrong because there's no noun; "such strong results" would be the *such* version.*
7. **It was such a long meeting that half the room had stopped taking notes by the end.**
   *— Everyday + work. *meeting* is a singular countable noun → **such a** long meeting. This is the most common place the missing *a* gets dropped.*
8. **It's such an important story for investors that I won't improvise a single slide.**
   *— Executive. Same idea as example 4, but now *important* is attached to a noun (*story*) → **such an** (note *an* before the vowel sound). The noun is the only thing that switched *so* to *such*.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Ask your tutor to run a **"flip it" drill**: they give you an emphasis in one form and you must flip it to the other, out loud and fast — *"so bold" → "such a bold bet"*, *"so talented" → "such a talented team"*, *"such a tight timeline" → "so tight"*. Doing this ten times trains the reflex that there's a **noun** present (→ *such*, keep the *a/an*) or not (→ *so*). This is the single highest-value fix for Portuguese speakers.
2. Practise the **result pattern under pressure**: role-play the CFO challenging one of your real bets, and defend it using *so … that* and *such … that* at least twice each — *"It was such a strategic move that passing on it would have cost us the market," "The window was so narrow that waiting a quarter wasn't an option."* It makes your emphasis sound native instead of translated.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 104):**
1. ❌ *fastly* → **fast** (it's already an adverb; "fastly" doesn't exist), and ❌ *worked hardly* → **worked hard**. *hardly* is a false friend meaning *quase não* / barely — *"worked hardly all quarter"* literally says they **barely** worked, the opposite of the intended "worked a lot." ✅ *"We need to move **fast** and the team worked **hard** all quarter."*
2. **completely** — to sharpen the adjective *different* you need an **adverb**, not another adjective. The word describes an adjective → adverb. *"a complete different model"* is wrong.
3. **"The rollout went well, even though the pilot started badly."** Both *go* and *start* are **action verbs**, so both take an **adverb**: *well* (never "went good") and *badly*. The rule is about the kind of verb, not whether the news is good or bad.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 098:** The version **with commas** — *"The directors, who opposed the plan, resigned"* — means **all** of them resigned (and, as an aside, they all opposed). The version **without commas** means **only** the ones who opposed resigned (the others stayed). The commas aren't decoration: they change *which directors* you're talking about — the facts, not just the style.
- **Unit 075:** **which** — *"They approved the full budget, which surprised everyone."* The key point: here *which* doesn't point back at a single noun; it refers to the **whole event/clause** (the fact that they approved the full budget). Non-defining, so it needs the comma and can never be *that*.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **such** — *"such a strategic move."* The phrase contains a **noun** (*move*), so it takes *such* (with *a* kept). "So a strategic move" is impossible — *so* can't sit before a noun phrase.
2. Error: **such → so**. *tense* is an adjective standing **alone** (no noun), so it must be *so*: *"The negotiation was **so tense** that nobody spoke."* (If you wanted *such*, you'd need a noun: *"such a tense negotiation."*)
3. ❌ *"a so risky investment"* → ✅ *"**such a risky** investment."* There's a noun (*investment*), so you need *such*, and *so* can never precede a noun phrase. This is the direct-translation trap from Portuguese *"um investimento tão arriscado."*
4. **"There were **so** many unknowns and the stakes were **so** high that we delayed the launch."** First gap: *so many* is the fixed quantity form before a countable noun (*unknowns*) — not *such*. Second gap: *high* is an adjective standing alone → *so*. Both are *so*; *such* would need a noun right after it.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra:** *so* e *such* dão ênfase ("muito / tão"), mas a gramática depois deles muda. Use **so** antes de um **adjetivo ou advérbio sozinho** (*so important*, *so fast*) e antes de quantidade (*so much* / *so many*). Use **such** quando houver um **substantivo** no grupo (*such a bold bet*, *such talented people*). A pergunta que resolve tudo: **tem um substantivo na frase que estou enfatizando?** Se sim → *such* (e, com substantivo contável no singular, **mantenha o *a/an*** : *such **a** bold bet*, *such **an** important story*); se é só uma palavra que descreve → *so*. Os dois montam o padrão de resultado *so/such … that* (*= tão … que*).

**As armadilhas (interferência do português):** (1) o erro nº 1 é usar *so* antes de substantivo — *"uma decisão tão importante"* vira ❌ *"a so important decision"*; o certo é ✅ *"**such an** important decision"*. (2) o inverso: *such* antes de adjetivo sozinho — ❌ *"such tight"* → ✅ *"**so** tight"*. (3) com contável singular, nunca esqueça o *a/an* (❌ *"such important decision"* → ✅ *"such **an** important decision"*), e para quantidade use *so much / so many*, não *such* (*tanta pressão → **so much** pressure*). Se a diferença advérbio × adjetivo ainda pesar na escolha (*so well* vs *such a strong presenter*), releia `lessons/2026-10-24-unit-104.md`.
