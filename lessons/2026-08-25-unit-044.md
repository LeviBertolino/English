### 1. 📘 Unit 044 — Verb + object + to… (I want you to / I told him to)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** I need someone to own the AI roadmap end to end.
> **You:** I **want you to** give me the mandate, and I'**ll ask** the CFO **to**
> ring-fence the budget. I'**ve already told** the team **to** pause the smaller
> pilots.
> **CEO:** Good. I **expect us to** show the board a single flagship bet.
> **You:** Agreed. I'd **advise the board to** fund one thing well, not five
> things halfway. And I **need you to** back me in the room on Thursday.
> **CEO:** You have it.

Notice the shape before any rule. Each verb here points at a **person** and then
tells you what you want that person **to do**: *want **you** to give*, *ask **the
CFO** to ring-fence*, *told **the team** to pause*, *expect **us** to show*. The
pattern is **verb + who + to + do**. Portuguese reaches for *"quero que você
faça"* — a *that*-clause — and translating that word-for-word is exactly the trap
this unit closes.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Many verbs take an **object** (a person) and then a **to-infinitive** saying what
that person does:

| Verb | + object | + to + base verb |
|---|---|---|
| I want | **you** | **to lead** the roadmap. |
| The CEO asked | **me** | **to present** to the board. |
| I told | **the team** | **to prioritize** the flagship. |
| The board expects | **us** | **to deliver** by Q3. |

**The core set (verb + object + to…):**

> **want · ask · tell · expect · would like · advise · encourage · persuade ·
> allow · enable · invite · remind · warn · get (someone to do) · need**

**Key facts:**
- The **object comes first**, then *to*: *"I want **you to** decide"* — never
  *"I want **to you** decide"* and never *"I want **that you** decide."*
- To make it **negative**, put *not* before *to*: *"I told them **not to**
  announce yet."*
- The object is an **object pronoun**: *me, you, him, her, us, them* —
  *"She asked **me** to lead"*, not *"She asked **I** to lead."*
- **Two verbs are exceptions — they drop *to* (bare infinitive):** *make* and
  *let*. *"The CEO **let me** run it"*, *"Pressure **made us** rethink"* — no
  *to*. (More on this in section 4.)
- **say** does **not** take this pattern: you **tell someone to do**, but you
  **say that…** — *"I **told him to** wait"* ✅ / *"I **said him to** wait"* ❌.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** Because leadership language is full of one
person directing, wanting, or enabling **another** person to act — and English
packs that into a tight frame: **verb + who + to do**. It's the grammar of
delegation, instruction, and expectation, which is most of an executive's speech:
*"I want **you** to own this", "The board expects **us** to deliver", "I'll ask
**Legal** to review", "I'd advise **the CFO** to hold."* Whenever the *doer* of
the second action is **different** from the subject, you need this pattern — one
verb reaching across to a second person.

**Decision rule #1 — verb + object + to… vs a *that*-clause (the whole game for a
Brazilian).** Portuguese expresses this with **que + subjuntivo**: *"quero **que
você** faça", "pedi **que ele** viesse", "espero **que a equipe** entregue."*
English **refuses** the literal *that* here. It restructures into **object + to**:

| Portuguese | ❌ Literal English | ✅ English |
|---|---|---|
| Quero que **você** lidere | I want **that you lead** | I want **you to lead** |
| Peço que **ele** revise | I ask **that he reviews** | I ask **him to review** |
| Espero que **a equipe** entregue | I expect **that the team delivers** | I expect **the team to deliver** |

Same meaning, completely different machinery: drop *that*, name the person as an
**object**, and put the second verb in the **to-infinitive**. This one swap kills
the most common Brazilian mistake in executive English.

**Decision rule #2 — *tell / ask someone to do* vs *say* + *that*.** *tell* and
*ask* take a **person + to**; *say* takes **no person** and a **that-clause**:
- *"I **told the board to** wait."* → instruction to a person. ✅
- *"I **said** (that) we should wait."* → reporting your words, no object. ✅
- ❌ *"I **said the board to** wait"* / ❌ *"I **told that** we should wait."*
Rule of thumb: if a **person** appears right after the verb, use *tell/ask*
(+ *to*), not *say*.

**Decision rule #3 — *want/ask/tell someone to do* vs *make/let someone do*.**
Most verbs in this family keep *to*. **make** (= force) and **let** (= allow)
**drop it**:
- *"The CFO **let me** present first."* (let + **do**, no *to*) ✅
- *"The deadline **made us** cut two pilots."* (make + **do**, no *to*) ✅
- but *"The CFO **allowed me to** present."* (allow keeps *to*) ✅
So *let/make* → bare verb; the near-synonyms *allow/enable/get* → *to*.

**PT→EN mapping — where Portuguese betrays Levi.** (1) The *que*-clause reflex
(*"want that you…"*) — the #1 trap, fixed above. (2) *pedir* → English splits:
*ask **someone** to do* (request an action) vs *ask **for** something* (request a
thing). (3) *dizer para alguém fazer* maps to *tell someone to do*, **not** *say*.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"I **want that the board approves** the budget."* → ✅ *"I **want the board
  to approve** the budget."* — English has no *want that…*; use **object + to**.
- ❌ *"The CEO **said me to** lead the roadmap."* → ✅ *"The CEO **told me to** lead
  the roadmap."* / *"The CEO **asked me to** lead…"* — a person after the verb
  needs *tell/ask*, never *say*.
- ❌ *"The board **let us to** hire a Head of AI."* → ✅ *"The board **let us hire**
  a Head of AI."* — *let* and *make* take the **bare** verb, no *to*.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"I **want you to own** the AI roadmap end to end, not just advise on it."*
   *— want + object (you) + to: the doer of "own" is a different person, so no that-clause — never "want that you own".*
2. *"The CEO **asked me to present** the investment case to the board."*
   *— ask + person + to = request an action of someone. "Asked to me" and "asked that I present" are both wrong.*
3. *"I'**ve already told the team to pause** the smaller pilots."*
   *— tell + person + to (instruction). A person follows the verb, so it's tell, not say ("said the team to" is impossible).*
4. *"The board **expects us to deliver** a single flagship bet by Q3."*
   *— expect + object + to = a demand/assumption about someone's future action; not "expects that we deliver".*
5. *"I'd **advise the CFO not to release** the numbers before the audit signs off."*
   *— negative form: "not" goes right before "to". advise + person + to is the diplomatic "recommend that someone…".*
6. *"The CFO **let me present** first, which set the framing for the whole session."*
   *— let = allow, and it drops "to": "let me present", never "let me to present".*
7. *"The tight deadline **made us cut** two pilots to protect the flagship."*
   *— make = force, also bare infinitive: "made us cut", not "made us to cut". Contrast with "forced us to cut" (force keeps to).*
8. *"I need you to text me when you leave, so I don't wait up."*
   *— everyday life: need + object + to. Same delegation frame off the clock — "I need that you text me" is the classic slip from "preciso que você me avise".*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 042 — Verb + -ing):**
1. ***suggest running*** — *suggest* is an *-ing* verb; the second verb wears
   *-ing* as a **fixed pattern**. *"suggest to run"* is ungrammatical (the
   *sugerir + infinitivo* reflex).
2. ***stop debating*** means **end the debate** (stop + *-ing* = quit the
   activity). *"stop to debate"* would mean **pause in order to** debate — the
   opposite event.
3. ***to launch*** — *"We **decided to launch** the pilot."* *decide* is a
   **to-infinitive** verb, not an *-ing* verb: the pattern is set by **which verb
   comes first**, not by meaning.

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 037)** ***"Would you like me to draft** the board story this week?"* —
  the polite **offer** form is *would like + object + to* (*would you like **me
  to** do…?*). *"Do you want that I draft…"* is wrong on two counts: no *want
  that…* in English, and the polite register uses *would like*.
- **(Unit 016)** ***"We **haven't** locked the risk section **yet**."*** —
  present perfect (*have + not + locked*) reports the past action **for its effect
  now** (still open); *yet* sits at the **end**, marking something expected but not
  done.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ***"I want the board to approve** the AI budget this quarter."* — English has
   no *want that…*; the *que*-clause is replaced by **object (the board) + to +
   base verb (approve)**.
2. ***told*** — *"The CEO **told me to** lead the roadmap."* A **person** right
   after the verb forces *tell/ask* (+ *to*); *say* takes **no object** and a
   *that*-clause (*"said that…"*), so *"said me to"* is impossible.
3. ***"The deadline **made us cut** two pilots."*** — *make* (= force) takes the
   **bare** infinitive: *made us **cut***, no *to*. Swap in *allow* and *to*
   returns: *"allowed us **to cut**."* (*let/make* drop *to*; *allow/enable/get*
   keep it.)
4. **Wrong word: *to* (after *asked*)** → ✅ *"I **asked the CFO to** ring-fence
   the budget."* *ask* takes the person as a **direct object** — no preposition
   before it. (*ask **for** something* is the only place *for* belongs.)

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** Muitos verbos pedem um **objeto** (uma pessoa) e depois
um **to-infinitivo** dizendo o que essa pessoa faz: *want, ask, tell, expect,
advise, encourage, persuade, allow, invite, remind, need* + **quem** + **to** +
verbo. Ex.: *"I want **you to lead**", "The CEO asked **me to present**", "The
board expects **us to deliver**."* Use sempre que o **agente** da segunda ação for
uma pessoa **diferente** do sujeito — é a gramática da delegação e da instrução, o
dia a dia de um líder. Para negar, *not* vem antes de *to* (*"told them **not to**
announce"*). Dois verbos fogem da regra e **largam o *to***: *make* (= forçar) e
*let* (= deixar) → *"let me **present**", "made us **cut**"* (sem *to*).

**As armadilhas.** (1) A cilada nº 1 é o **que + subjuntivo** do português:
*"quero que você lidere"* vira, por reflexo, *"I want **that you lead**"* —
**errado**; o inglês exige **objeto + to**: *"I want **you to lead**."* Vale para
*ask, expect, need* etc. (2) *dizer para alguém fazer* é *tell/ask **someone**
to do*, nunca *say* — *say* não leva pessoa, leva *that* (*"said that we should
wait"*). (3) *ask* leva a pessoa como **objeto direto**, sem preposição: *"asked
**the CFO** to…"*, não *"asked **to the** CFO"* (*ask **for** something* é o único
lugar do *for*). Se a mecânica de verbo + verbo ainda embolar, releia
`lessons/2026-08-23-unit-042.md` (Verb + -ing) — é o par direto desta unidade.
