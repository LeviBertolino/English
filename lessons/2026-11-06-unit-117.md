### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Who's running the AI audit?
> **Levi:** Marta — she's the consultant **who led** our cloud migration last year.
> **CFO:** And on the vendor side?
> **Levi:** We went with the one **that scored highest** on security. The other two had gaps **we couldn't close** in time.
> **CEO:** And that roadmap slide the board flagged?
> **Levi:** That's the decision **which reshaped** our whole 2027 plan — the bet **everyone remembers**.
> **CFO:** So the lead you want to own it is…?
> **Levi:** The AI specialist **we poached** from the challenger. She starts Monday.

Notice what these little clauses do: each one **identifies** a noun by telling you *which* one. *The consultant* — which consultant? — *who led our migration*. *The one* — which vendor? — *that scored highest*. The clause narrows the noun down to the exact one you mean. Now watch something subtle: in *the consultant **who led*** and *the one **that scored***, the pronoun (*who / that*) is the **subject** of the clause — it can't be removed. But in *the gaps **(that) we couldn't close***, *the decision **(which) everyone remembers***, and *the specialist **(who) we poached***, the pronoun is the **object** — and native speakers quietly **drop it**: *the gaps we couldn't close*, *the specialist we poached*. That drop is the half of this unit Portuguese never prepares you for.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

A **defining** relative clause identifies the noun — it answers *"which one?"* and is essential to the sentence. It sits right after the noun and starts with a relative pronoun:

| Pronoun | Use it for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | people | *the consultant **who** led the audit* |
| **which** | things, ideas, animals | *the decision **which** reshaped our plan* |
| **that** | people **or** things (the everyday default) | *the vendor **that** scored highest · the lead **that** we hired* |

**The one rule that unlocks the unit — subject vs object:**

Ask: inside the clause, is the pronoun the **subject** (doing the verb) or the **object** (receiving it)?

| Role of the pronoun | Example | Can you drop it? |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject** (verb comes right after it) | *the consultant **who led** the audit* | ❌ **No** — *the consultant led the audit* changes the meaning |
| **Object** (a new subject comes right after it) | *the proposal **(that) the board rejected*** | ✅ **Yes** — *the proposal the board rejected* |

Quick test: if the next word after the pronoun is a **verb**, the pronoun is the **subject → keep it**. If the next word is a **new noun/pronoun** (a fresh subject), the pronoun is the **object → you may drop it**.
- *the vendor **that scored** highest* → *scored* is a verb → subject → **keep**.
- *the vendor **(that) we chose*** → *we* is a new subject → object → **droppable**.

**Prepositions:** in everyday English the preposition stays at the **end** of the clause, and the pronoun is usually dropped: *the platform **(which) we migrated to*** → *the platform we migrated to*. (Formal written English can write *to which*, but that's not how you'd speak.)

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** A noun on its own is often too vague to act on — *"the consultant," "the vendor," "the decision."* Which one? A relative clause **pins it down** without starting a second sentence: not *"We hired a specialist. She came from the challenger,"* but *"the specialist **we poached from the challenger**"* — one clean noun phrase you can drop into any sentence. For a leader this is the grammar of precision: *the risk that keeps me up*, *the one metric the board actually watches*, *the hire who changed the team*. It lets you name exactly the thing you mean, in the flow of a sentence, with no second clause.

**Decision rule #1 — *who* vs *which* vs *that*:** *who* for people, *which* for things, and *that* for **either** (it's the common, slightly more informal default). So for a person you have a choice — *the consultant **who** led* = *the consultant **that** led* — but *which* for a person is simply **wrong**: ❌ *the consultant **which** led*. The Portuguese *que* covers all three, which is exactly why *which* leaks onto people.

**Decision rule #2 — keep the pronoun or drop it (THE core):**
This is the half Portuguese doesn't have, because *que* is **never** dropped in Portuguese. English drops the relative pronoun whenever it's the **object** of the clause — and native speakers do this constantly:
- *"The proposal **that** the board rejected came back stronger."* → more natural: *"The proposal **the board rejected** came back stronger."* (*that* = object → drop it.)
- *"The specialist **who** we poached starts Monday."* → *"The specialist **we poached** starts Monday."*

But you **cannot** drop a **subject** pronoun:
- ✅ *"Marta is the consultant **who led** our migration."* / ❌ *"Marta is the consultant **led** our migration."* (Without *who*, the sentence breaks — *who* is the subject of *led*.)

So the move is: **object → drop freely** (sounds native), **subject → always keep**. When in doubt, apply the next-word test from section 3.

**Decision rule #3 — one object only (no "echo" pronoun):**
In colloquial Portuguese you hear the object repeated — *"o fornecedor **que** nós escolhemos **ele**," "a plataforma **que** migramos **para ela**."* English allows the object **once**: either the relative pronoun carries it, or the end preposition does — never a second stray pronoun.
- ❌ *"the vendor **that** we chose **it**"* → ✅ *"the vendor (that) we chose."*
- ❌ *"the platform **that** we migrated **to it**"* → ✅ *"the platform (that) we migrated **to**."*

**PT→EN mapping (where translation betrays you):**
- *que* (pessoa) → **who / that**; *que* (coisa) → **which / that**. One Portuguese word, three English ones — and **never** *which* for a person.
- *o qual / a qual / os quais* (formal) → **which / who**, but in speech just use **that** or drop it.
- *quem* (depois de preposição) → **who** + end preposition: *"a pessoa **com quem** trabalho"* → *"the person **I work with**"* (drop *who*, strand *with*).
- ⚠️ The big one: Portuguese **keeps** *que* even as an object (*"o fornecedor **que** escolhemos"*), so your hand writes *that* every time. English prefers to **drop** the object pronoun (*"the vendor we chose"*). Keeping *that* isn't wrong — but dropping it is what sounds native.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"Marta is the consultant **which** led the audit."* → ✅ *"…the consultant **who** (or **that**) led the audit."* *which* is never for people; the neutral *que* is what pulls you into it.
2. ❌ *"The vendor **that we chose it** pulled out."* (echo pronoun) → ✅ *"The vendor **(that) we chose** pulled out."* The object appears **once** — don't repeat it with *it / him / them*.
3. ❌ *"Marta is the consultant **led** our migration."* (dropped a **subject** pronoun) → ✅ *"…the consultant **who led** our migration."* You can only drop the pronoun when it's the **object** — a subject pronoun must stay.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **Marta is the consultant who led our cloud migration last year.**
   *— subject relative (person → `who`); `who led` — the verb follows directly, so `who` is the subject and **cannot** be dropped. "the consultant led" would break the sentence.*
2. **We went with the vendor that scored highest on security.**
   *— subject relative (thing → `that`); `that scored` — verb follows, so keep it. `which` would also work; `that` is the everyday default.*
3. **The proposal the board rejected last quarter came back far stronger.**
   *— object relative with the pronoun **dropped**: "the proposal (that) **the board** rejected" — a new subject (`the board`) follows, so `that` is the object and vanishes. This is the native-sounding version.*
4. **Long term, it's the decision which reshaped our entire 2027 roadmap.**
   *— subject relative (thing → `which`), a touch more formal than `that`; `which reshaped` — verb follows, so it stays. Fits the weight of a board statement.*
5. **The AI lead we poached from the challenger starts on Monday.**
   *— object relative, pronoun dropped: "the lead (who/that) **we** poached." `we` is a new subject → the pronoun is the object → gone. Note the end of the clause runs straight into the main verb `starts`.*
6. **This is exactly the kind of bet that defines a company.**
   *— subject relative on an abstract noun (`bet` → `that`); `that defines` — verb follows, keep it. The clause pins down *which* kind of bet you mean.*
7. **The platform we migrated to last quarter is finally stable.** *(strategy/everyday blend)*
   *— object relative with a **stranded preposition**: "the platform (which) we migrated **to**." The pronoun drops and `to` sits at the end — never "to which we migrated" in speech, and never "migrated to it."*
8. **The colleague who sits next to me just made partner.** *(everyday)*
   *— subject relative (person → `who`); `who sits` — verb follows, so it can't be dropped. Identifies *which* colleague.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Run a **"which one?" round** with your tutor: they name a bare noun from your world (*the consultant, the vendor, the decision, the hire, the metric*) and you must pin it down with a defining clause — *"the consultant **who** led the migration," "the vendor **that** scored highest," "the decision **which** reshaped the roadmap."* Rule to enforce out loud: **verb right after the pronoun → keep it; a new subject right after → drop it.** Ask them to buzz you the instant you say *which* about a **person**, or leave an echo pronoun (*"the vendor that we chose it"*).

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 116 — *unless / as long as / provided*):**
1. **objects** — *"**Unless** the board **objects**, we commit by Friday."* *unless* already means *if … not*, so the verb after it stays **positive**. *"unless the board doesn't object"* is a double negative — it reverses the meaning (it would say you commit only if they *do* block it).
2. **stays** — *"**As long as** adoption **stays** above sixty percent…"* After *as long as* (and *provided / providing / unless*, exactly like *if*) the future is carried by the **present tense** — *will* never appears in the condition.
3. **in case** — *"Keep a cash reserve **in case** the funding round slips."* It's a **precaution**: you act **now** because the round **might** slip. *"as long as the round slips"* would make the slip a *condition* for keeping the reserve — the opposite, nonsensical here.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 110 (*the same as*):** **as** — *"Our run costs are **the same as** last year."* *same* is **always** followed by *as*, never *than* (nor *of* / *that*); the Portuguese *mesmo **que*** is what pulls you toward *than*.
- **Unit 087 (superlatives):** **in** — *"the biggest AI player **in** the market."* Use **in** for a place or group (*in the world, in the industry*); save **of** for a time span or an explicit set (*the best **of** the year*). The Portuguese *do mercado* tempts *of*, but here it's *in*.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **who** — *"the specialist **who** ran the audit."* A specialist is a **person**, and *which* is only for things; *that* would also be fine, but *which* for a person is impossible. (The neutral *que* is what makes *which* feel safe — it isn't.)
2. **Wrong — a subject pronoun was dropped.** Put it back: *"The vendor **that** (or **who/which**) **scored** highest on security won the contract."* *scored* is a verb sitting right after the noun, so the pronoun is the **subject** — and a subject relative pronoun can **never** be dropped. (You can only drop the *object* one.)
3. **The extra word is *it*.** → ✅ *"The platform **(that) we migrated to** is finally stable."* English names the object **once**: the relative pronoun (or the dropped slot) already is the object, so repeating it with *it* is the colloquial-Portuguese echo (*que migramos para **ela***). The preposition *to* stays stranded at the end; no *it*.
4. **(b)** — *"the budget **(that) the board approved."*** In (b) a new subject (*the board*) follows the pronoun, so *that* is the **object** → droppable. In (a) *that* is immediately followed by the verb *approved*, so it's the **subject** → it must stay. Next-word test: verb → keep; new subject → drop.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra:** uma oração relativa **restritiva** identifica o substantivo — responde *"qual?"* e gruda logo depois dele. Escolha do pronome: **who** para pessoas, **which** para coisas, e **that** para os dois (o padrão coloquial do dia a dia). O português usa *que* para tudo, então o perigo é soltar *which* sobre pessoa — nunca faça isso. A outra metade, que o português **não** tem, é **dropar** o pronome: quando ele é o **objeto** da oração (vem um **novo sujeito** logo depois — *"the vendor **we** chose"*), o inglês simplesmente o **apaga**, e é isso que soa nativo. Quando ele é o **sujeito** (vem um **verbo** logo depois — *"the consultant **who led**"*), ele é **obrigatório**. Teste rápido: verbo depois do pronome → mantenha; sujeito novo depois → pode apagar.

**As armadilhas (interferência do português):** (1) *que* vira *who/which/that* conforme o caso — e **nunca** *which* para gente: *"the consultant **who** led"*, não *"which led"*. (2) O pronome-eco: o português falado repete o objeto (*"o fornecedor **que** escolhemos **ele**"*), mas o inglês nomeia o objeto **uma vez só** — ❌ *"the vendor that we chose **it**"* → ✅ *"the vendor we chose"*; e a preposição fica no **fim** (*"the platform we migrated **to**"*, sem *it*). (3) Não apague o pronome **sujeito**: ❌ *"the consultant **led** our migration"* → ✅ *"the consultant **who led**…"*. Como *que* nunca cai no português, treine de propósito o **drop** do objeto — é o detalhe que mais aproxima seu inglês do de um nativo.
