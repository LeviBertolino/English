### 1. 📘 Unit 083 — *enough* and *too*

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Can we green-light the AI platform this quarter?
> **You:** Not yet — it's **too risky to** approve without a pilot. The team isn't **senior enough to**
> run it alone.
> **CFO:** We also have **too much** spend committed elsewhere.
> **You:** Right. But if we scope it down, we'd have **enough runway to** fund one flagship bet.
> **CEO:** Is the prototype ready for the board?
> **You:** It's **good enough to** show them the direction — not polished, but the signal is **strong
> enough**. Pushing for perfect now would cost us **too much** time.

Watch the two shapes before any rule. One says *more than we want* — **too risky, too much spend, too
much time** — a problem, a brake. The other says *the right amount* — **senior enough, enough runway,
good enough, strong enough** — a sufficiency check. And notice the positions: *enough* lands **before a
noun** (*enough runway*) but **after an adjective** (*senior enough, good enough*), while *too* always
sits **in front** (*too risky, too much*). Both of them love a *to*-verb that says what the amount is
enough — or too much — **for**: *too risky **to** approve*, *senior enough **to** run it*.

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Two intensifiers with **opposite jobs** and **different positions**.

| Meaning | Word | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| more than wanted (excess, a problem) | **too** | **before** adj/adv | *too **risky**, too **slowly*** |
| more than wanted, with a noun | **too much / too many** | before the noun | *too **much** spend, too **many** bets* |
| the right / sufficient amount | **enough** | **after** adj/adv | ***senior** enough, **fast** enough* |
| sufficient amount, with a noun | **enough** | **before** the noun | ***enough** runway, **enough** time* |

**The "… to do" frames (this is where they pay off):**

| Frame | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **too** + adj + **to** + verb | so much that you *can't* | *too risky **to** approve* (= we can't approve it) |
| adj + **enough** + **to** + verb | sufficient so that you *can* | *senior enough **to** run it* (= they can run it) |
| **too much / many** + noun + **to** | excess blocks the action | *too much work **to** finish today* |

**Key facts:**
- ***enough* goes AFTER an adjective/adverb but BEFORE a noun.** *old **enough*** ✅ · *~~enough old~~* ❌;
  *~~time enough~~* ❌ · ***enough** time* ✅. The word order literally flips depending on what follows.
- ***too* always goes BEFORE the adjective/adverb.** *too expensive, too quickly* — never *~~expensive too~~*.
- **With nouns, too splits by countable:** uncountable → *too **much*** (*too much pressure*); countable
  plural → *too **many*** (*too many options*).
- **The *for someone* add-on:** *too risky **for the board***, *senior enough **for the role***. The *for*
  phrase can sit before the *to*-verb: *too complex **for us to** ship this quarter*.
- **The *to*-verb is optional** — you can stop early: *"It's too risky."* / *"They're senior enough."*
  The *to*-clause just spells out *too risky to do what / enough for what*.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (the core section)

**Why do these two words exist?** They measure an amount against a **threshold**, in opposite directions.
*enough* says *we've hit the line — we can proceed*. *too* says *we've crossed the line — it's now a
problem*. Neither one just rates intensity the way *very* does; each carries a **verdict**. "The budget
is **very** large" describes it. "The budget is **too** large (to approve)" condemns it. "The team is
**experienced enough** (to run it)" clears it. In executive talk this is pure decision language — *go /
no-go, sufficient / excessive* — which is why these words show up every time you're gatekeeping a bet.

**Decision rule #1 — too vs very (the trap that matters most):**

| | *very* | *too* |
|---|---|---|
| job | intensifies, **neutral** | signals **excess — a problem** |
| *"The timeline is ___ aggressive."* | just emphatic: it's very aggressive | **negative**: so aggressive we *can't* hit it |
| can it be positive? | yes (*very good, very fast*) | **no** — *too good* means a problem, not praise |

- ✅ *"The prototype is **very** impressive."* (praise)
- ❌ *"The prototype is **too** impressive."* (sounds like *impressive is a problem* — not what you mean)

**Decision rule #2 — too vs enough (direction of the threshold):**
- *"The team isn't experienced **enough**."* → below the line (needs **more**).
- *"The project is **too** complex."* → above the line (needs **less**).
- Same situation, two angles: *"not fast **enough**"* = *"**too** slow."* Minimal pair, identical meaning,
  opposite word — pick whichever noun/adjective is cleaner.

**PT→EN mapping — three interference points:**
- **Word order of *enough*.** Portuguese *"o suficiente"* comes **after**: *"velho o suficiente"*,
  *"dinheiro suficiente"*. English keeps *enough* **after the adjective** (*old enough* ✅ matches) but
  moves it **before the noun** (*enough money*, not *~~money enough~~*). The noun case is where the direct
  translation betrays you.
- **"demais / muito" → too.** *"caro demais"* / *"arriscado demais"* → *"too expensive / too risky."*
  Clean. But the Portuguese *"muito"* covers **both** *very* and *too*, so Levi sometimes uses *too* where
  he means *very* — the #1 slip below.
- **"muito/muitos" → too much / too many.** *"pressão demais"* → *"too much pressure"*; *"opções demais"*
  → *"too many options."* Split by countable, same as *so much / so many*.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"The meeting was too productive."* / *"She's too smart — I'm impressed."* → ✅ *"The meeting was
   **very** productive." · "She's **very** smart."* (*too* = a problem; use *very* for praise.)
2. ❌ *"We don't have enough budget? No — we have budget enough."* / *"He's enough experienced."* → ✅
   *"We have **enough budget**." · "He's **experienced enough**."* (*enough* **before** a noun, **after**
   an adjective.)
3. ❌ *"There's too many pressure."* / *"too much risks on the table."* → ✅ *"too **much** pressure" · "too
   **many** risks."* (uncountable → *much*; countable plural → *many*.)

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **The AI budget is too risky to approve without a pilot.**
   *— `too` + adjective (`risky`) = excess → we **can't** approve; the `to`-verb says the blocked action.*
2. **The team isn't senior enough to run this programme alone.**
   *— `enough` sits **after** the adjective (`senior`); negative frame = below the threshold, needs more.*
3. **If we scope it down, we'd have enough runway to fund one flagship bet.**
   *— `enough` sits **before** the noun (`runway`); sufficiency = we **can** proceed.*
4. **There's too much pressure on Q4 to add scope now.**
   *— `too much` + uncountable noun (`pressure`); the excess blocks the action in the `to`-clause.*
5. **The board had too many competing priorities to decide quickly.**
   *— `too many` + countable plural (`priorities`); contrast with #4's uncountable `much`.*
6. **The prototype is good enough to show the board — not polished, but directional.**
   *— `good enough` (adj + enough) = clears the bar; `too good` here would wrongly mean a problem.*
7. **The coffee's too hot to drink — give it a minute.** *(everyday)*
   *— classic `too + adj + to` = so hot you **can't** drink it yet; the arrow to the blocked action.*
8. **I didn't sleep enough to feel sharp this morning.** *(everyday)*
   *— `enough` **after** the verb `sleep`; below the threshold → the `to`-verb names what you can't do.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **go / no-go drill**: they name a bet with a condition (*"the platform — not
  mature," "the team — small," "the timeline — tight," "the data — messy"*) and you must deliver the
  verdict **both ways** — once with *too* + adjective (*"it's too immature to scale"*) and once with
  *enough* (*"it's not mature enough to scale"*). Saying the same no-go through both words burns in the
  *not … enough = too …* equivalence and the two positions.
- Then have the tutor **bait the too/very trap**: they describe something genuinely good (*"the quarter
  was great," "the hire is brilliant"*) and you must praise it **without** sliding into *too* — forcing
  *very / really / so* for praise and reserving *too* strictly for *this is a problem*. This kills the
  *"it was too good"* slip that makes a compliment sound like a complaint.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 082):**
1. **so** / **such** — *"so aggressive"* (adjective standing alone) vs *"such an aggressive timeline"*
   (a **noun**, *timeline*, now follows). The single clue: **is there a noun right after?** Noun → *such*;
   bare adjective → *so*. The article *a/an* is the giveaway.
2. **so much** data / **so many** teams. *data* is **uncountable** → *so much*; *teams* is **countable
   plural** → *so many*. Countability decides.
3. They point to a **consequence** — *very* only rates, *so/such* rates **and** sets up a *that*-result
   (*so expensive **that** we paused*). *so/such* = *very* with a "therefore" attached.

**Spaced review:**
- **~1 week (Unit 076):** **approved** — *"the budget **approved** by the board."* The one question:
   *does the noun DO the action or RECEIVE it?* The budget **receives** approval → passive → **-ed**
   (= *that was approved*). *approving* would wrongly say the budget is doing something.
- **~1 month (Unit 051):** **before deciding** — after a preposition English uses **-ing**, never a plain
   infinitive. Portuguese *"antes de **decidir**"* (preposition + infinitive) pushes you to ❌ *"before
   to decide"*; English must be *"before **deciding**."*

**Recognition drill:**
1. **very** — *"very impressive"* = praise, which matches *"the board loved it."* *too impressive* would
   accidentally mean *impressive to the point of being a problem* — the opposite of a compliment.
2. **enough + noun goes before the noun:** *"We don't have **enough budget** for a second bet."*
   (*budget enough* copies the Portuguese order *"orçamento suficiente"* and is wrong.)
3. **too many** — *questions* is countable plural → *many*, not *much*.
4. **"The team is too slow."** — *not fast enough* = *too slow*: flip the direction and the adjective to
   its opposite. Same no-go verdict, rival structure.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

*too* e *enough* medem uma quantidade contra um **limite**, em direções opostas. *enough* = *o
suficiente, dá pra seguir* (positivo/neutro); *too* = *demais, virou problema* (negativo). Nenhum dos
dois só intensifica como o *very* — cada um carrega um **veredito** de decisão (*go / no-go*). Posição é
meia batalha: *enough* vem **depois** do adjetivo/advérbio (*senior **enough**, fast **enough***) mas
**antes** do substantivo (***enough** runway, **enough** time*); *too* vem sempre **antes** (*too risky,
too much*). Com substantivo, *too* se divide: incontável → *too **much*** (*too much pressure*), contável
no plural → *too **many*** (*too many options*). E as duas frases-chave: *too + adj + **to** + verbo* =
*tão… que não dá* (*too risky to approve*); *adj + **enough** + **to** + verbo* = *suficiente para poder*
(*senior enough to run it*).

As ciladas do português: (1) *muito* cobre *very* **e** *too*, então você às vezes diz *"it was too
good"* querendo elogiar — mas *too* significa **problema**; para elogio use *very/really/so*. (2) *"o
suficiente"* vem depois em português (*"dinheiro suficiente"*), mas em inglês *enough* vai **antes do
substantivo** (*enough money*, nunca *money enough*) — só fica depois quando é adjetivo (*old enough*).
(3) *demais* com substantivo se divide em *too much* (incontável) × *too many* (contável). Lembre:
*not … enough* = *too …* (não rápido o bastante = lento demais). Se a dupla *so/such* ainda embolar com
esta, releia `lessons/2026-10-02-unit-082.md`.
