### 1. 📘 Unit 065 — countable & uncountable nouns (some / any)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Where do we stand on the AI investment case?
> **You:** The board needs **more information** before they approve **any budget**. We got
> **some useful feedback** from the CFO, but not **enough data** to decide.
> **CEO:** How much **progress** have we made this quarter?
> **You:** Honestly, not much. There isn't **any equipment** left in the innovation lab, and
> the team is short on **people**. I'd like **some advice** on how to frame that gap for the board.
> **CEO:** Fair. Give me **three options** and **one recommendation** — I'll take it from there.

Notice two different kinds of noun living side by side. Some you can **count** and put a number
on: *any **budget** → three **options** → one **recommendation** → three **people**.* Others you
**can't** count — no number, no plural *-s*: *information, feedback, data, progress, equipment,
advice.* You'd never say *"~~three informations~~"* or *"~~an advice~~."* That split — and the
little words *some* and *any* that ride on top of it — is the whole lesson.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Every noun is either **countable** (you can count it: *one, two, three*) or **uncountable**
(you see it as a **mass**, not separate units).

| | **Countable** | **Uncountable** |
|---|---|---|
| Plural? | yes — *budget → budgets* | **no** — *information* (never *informations*) |
| *a / an*? | yes in singular — *a report, an idea* | **no** — *~~an advice~~* → *some advice* |
| "how many/much?" | **how many** (*how many reports?*) | **how much** (*how much progress?*) |
| small quantity word | *a few* (*a few risks*) | *a little* (*a little time*) |
| large quantity word | *many* (*many options*) | *much* (*much data*) — or *a lot of* for both |

**some / any — the quantity words that go with plurals *and* uncountables:**

| | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ✅ **affirmative** statement | **some** | *"We have **some** data."* · *"There are **some** risks."* |
| ❌ **negative** | **any** | *"We don't have **any** data."* · *"There aren't **any** risks."* |
| ❓ **question** | **any** | *"Do we have **any** data?"* · *"Are there **any** risks?"* |
| 🤝 **offer / request** (special!) | **some** | *"Would you like **some** coffee?"* · *"Can I have **some** advice?"* |

**Key facts:**
- *some* and *any* work with **both** uncountable nouns (*some information*) and **plural**
  countable nouns (*some options*). They **never** go with a **singular** countable noun — that
  one still takes *a/an* (*a report*, not *~~some report~~*).
- Uncountable nouns take a **singular** verb: *"The news **is** good," "The data **is** clear,"
  "The equipment **is** here."* — never *are*.
- To count an uncountable, borrow a **counting frame**: *a **piece** of advice, a **piece** of
  information, two **pieces** of equipment, a **bit** of feedback.*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this split exist?** English decides, per noun, whether it pictures the thing as
**separate units you can line up and count** (chairs, reports, ideas) or as an **undivided
mass** you measure but don't count (water, information, progress). The grammar then follows
automatically: countable things get *a/an* and a plural; mass things get *some/much* and stay
singular. Get the category wrong and **everything downstream breaks** — the article, the plural,
the verb, the quantity word all go wrong at once. That's why this is worth ten minutes.

**Decision rule — the one test.** Ask: *"Can I put a number in front and add -s?"*
- *"three **reports**"* ✅ → **countable** → *a report / reports / how many / a few / many.*
- *"~~three informations~~"* ❌ → **uncountable** → *information / some / how much / a little / much.*

Minimal pairs — same idea, different packaging, different grammar:

| Countable | Uncountable |
|---|---|
| *"We ran three **experiments**."* (units) | *"The **research** is inconclusive."* (mass) |
| *"I have a few **suggestions**."* | *"I have a little **advice**."* |
| *"There were **many delays**."* | *"There wasn't **much progress**."* |
| *"We bought two **laptops**."* (a laptop) | *"We bought **some software**."* (no *~~a software~~*) |

**some vs any — the meaning, not just "positive/negative."** *some* claims the thing **exists /
is available** ("we do have data"); *any* is **open, unspecified** — it fits where existence is
in doubt (questions) or denied (negatives): *"We don't have **any** data" = zero.* The one twist:
in **offers and requests** you use *some*, because you're not questioning existence — you're
assuming it and offering/asking: *"Would you like **some** help?" "Can I get **some** feedback?"*
Using *any* there (*"Would you like any help?"*) sounds cold, like you doubt they want it.

**PT→EN mapping — where this quietly betrays Levi.** Portuguese counts several nouns that English
refuses to count. In Portuguese you say *"uma informação," "informações," "um conselho," "os
feedbacks," "as notícias," "um equipamento," "uma pesquisa."* Translated straight, every one of
those becomes an error, because in English they are **uncountable**: *information, advice,
feedback, news, equipment, research, knowledge, progress, software, staff.* This is the single
most common noun mistake Brazilian executives make in English.

- *"Preciso de **mais informações**."* → ✅ *"I need **more information**."* — **not**
  *"~~more informations~~."*
- *"Me dá **um conselho**."* → ✅ *"Give me **some advice** / **a piece of advice**."* — **not**
  *"~~an advice~~."*
- *"**As notícias** são boas."* → ✅ *"The news **is** good."* — singular verb; *news* is
  uncountable despite the *-s*.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. ❌ *"The CFO gave me some good **advices** about the **informations** in the deck."* → ✅
   *"…some good **advice** about the **information** in the deck."* — *advice* and *information*
   are **uncountable**: no plural, no *a/an*. Count them with *a piece of* if you must.
2. ❌ *"We got **a feedback** and **an important news** from the market."* → ✅ *"We got **some
   feedback** and **some important news** from the market."* — no *a/an* on uncountables; reach
   for *some* (or *a piece of feedback*).
3. ❌ *"The data **are** ready and we don't need **some** more equipment."* → ✅ *"The data **is**
   ready and we don't need **any** more equipment."* — uncountable → **singular verb**; and in a
   **negative**, *some* becomes **any**.

---

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"The board hasn't given us **any feedback** on the AI strategy yet."*
   *— negative → "any", not "some"; feedback is uncountable, so no "a feedback" and no plural.*
2. *"We need **some more information** before we approve **the budget**."*
   *— affirmative statement → "some"; information is a mass (no plural). "the budget" is the one specific budget — that's the article from Unit 063 at work.*
3. *"How **much progress** have we made on the roadmap this quarter?"*
   *— progress is uncountable → "how much", never "how many"; you can't say "three progresses".*
4. *"I'd like **some advice** on how to position this for the CFO."*
   *— a request → "some" (not "any"), because you're assuming the advice exists and asking for it; "advice" stays uncountable.*
5. *"There isn't **any equipment** left in the innovation lab."*
   *— negative → "any"; equipment is uncountable (→ singular "isn't"), so not "aren't any equipments".*
6. *"We ran **three experiments**, but the **research** is still inconclusive."*
   *— "experiments" are countable units (a number + plural); "research" is the mass they add up to → singular "is". Both logics in one line.*
7. *"The **news** from the market **is** good, so we have **some room** to invest."*
   *— "news" takes a singular verb despite the -s; "room" (= space/margin) is uncountable here → "some", not "a room".*
8. *"Is there **any milk** in the fridge? — Yes, there's **some**, but not **much**."*
   *— everyday: question → "any"; affirmative answer → "some"; uncountable "milk" → "much", not "many".*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Ask your tutor to run a **"kill the plural" drill** on the nouns that trip up brasileiros:
   they say a Portuguese sentence loaded with the traps (*"preciso de mais informações e uns
   feedbacks," "as notícias são boas," "compramos uns equipamentos e uns softwares"*) and you snap
   the English back with the **correct uncountable forms** — *"I need more information and some
   feedback," "the news is good," "we bought some equipment and some software."* The goal is to
   kill the reflex of adding *-s* and *a/an* to these words.
2. Then a **some/any live switch**: the tutor throws the same idea as a statement, a negative, and
   a question, and you supply the right word each time — *"We have **some** data" → "We don't have
   **any** data" → "Do we have **any** data?"* — plus one **offer** round (*"Would you like **some**
   help with the board deck?"*) so the offer-exception becomes automatic under pressure.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 063 — the / no article):**
1. **AI → no article; business → no article** → ✅ *"**AI** is changing **business** faster than
   most leaders expect."* Both are **general** ideas (the field, business at large) → drop the
   article. Portuguese keeps *a/o*; English doesn't. (*the* would only appear for a **specific**
   AI or a **specific** business.)
2. **a pricing model** (first mention, not yet identified) → **the model** (now the specific,
   known one you just introduced). New → *a*; shared/known → *the*.
3. Drop both *the*s → ✅ *"**Brazil** is a key market and **Microsoft** is our AI partner."* Most
   **proper names** (countries, companies, people) take **no** *the*; Portuguese says *"o Brasil,
   a Microsoft,"* so the reflex is to carry the article across — English doesn't.

**Spaced review:**
- *(Unit 058 — possessive pronouns)* **yours** — *"It's **yours**."* A possessive pronoun stands
   **alone**, with no noun after it. *your* is the **adjective** and must sit **before a noun**
   (*your deck*); alone, it becomes *yours*.
- *(Unit 037 — would like)* **Would … like** — *"**Would** you **like** me to draft the board
   narrative?"* It's the **polite, diplomatic** offer — softer and more appropriate with a CEO
   than the blunt *"Do you want me to…?"*, which is fine among peers but can sound abrupt upward.

**Recognition drill:**
1. Both wrong → ✅ *"The CFO gave me **some good advice** about the **information** in the deck."*
   *advice* and *information* are **uncountable**: no plural *-s*, no *a/an*. (Count them as *a
   piece of advice / a piece of information* if you need to.)
2. **any / some** → ✅ *"We don't have **any** data yet, but I found **some** useful research
   online."* First blank is a **negative** → *any*; second is an **affirmative** statement →
   *some*. (Both nouns are uncountable, which is why neither takes a number or plural.)
3. **a new laptop / some new software** → ✅ *"We need **a** new laptop and **some** new software."*
   *laptop* is **singular countable** → *a*; *software* is **uncountable** → *some* (never *"a
   software"* or *"softwares"*). This is the core rival choice: *a/an* vs *some*.
4. Two fixes → ✅ *"The news **is** good and we don't have **many** open questions."* *news* is
   **uncountable** → **singular verb** *is* (not *are*); *questions* is **countable plural** →
   **many** (not *much*). Match the quantity word to the count type.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Todo substantivo em inglês é **contável** (você conta: *one report, two reports*) ou **incontável**
(uma **massa**, sem plural: *information, advice, feedback*). O teste é um só: *dá pra pôr um número
na frente e um -s no fim?* Se dá, é **contável** → usa *a/an*, plural, *how many, a few, many*. Se
não dá, é **incontável** → **sem** *a/an*, **sem** plural, verbo no **singular** (*the news is,
the data is*), e usa *some, how much, a little, much*. E lembra: *some* e *any* vão com plurais e
incontáveis (nunca com contável no singular — esse fica com *a/an*). Regra do *some/any*: **some**
em frases afirmativas e em **ofertas/pedidos** (*"Would you like some help?"*); **any** em
**negativas** e **perguntas** (*"We don't have any data," "Do we have any data?"*).

A grande armadilha para o brasileiro é que o português **conta** vários substantivos que o inglês
**não** conta. Nada de *"~~an information / informations~~", "~~an advice / advices~~", "~~a
feedback~~", "~~an equipment~~", "~~a software~~", "~~the news are~~"*. O certo é *some information
/ a piece of information, some advice, some feedback, some equipment, some software, the news is*.
Esses incontáveis — *information, advice, feedback, news, equipment, research, knowledge, progress,
software, staff, data* — são os que mais derrubam executivo brasileiro em inglês. Se a diferença
*contável × incontável* ou *some × any* ainda embolar, releia `lessons/2026-09-12-unit-062.md`
(a/an) e `lessons/2026-09-13-unit-063.md` (the) junto com este arquivo — os três formam um bloco só.
