### 1. 📘 Unit 058 — Whose is this? It's mine / yours / hers (possessive pronouns)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Two AI strategy decks landed on my desk overnight. **Whose** is this one?
> **You:** That one's **mine** — the three-year roadmap. The shorter one is **Sofia's**.
> **CEO:** And the budget model — is it **yours** or **hers**?
> **You:** The model is **hers**. The assumptions behind it are **ours** — we built them
> together in the offsite.
> **CFO:** Good. The board will ask whose numbers these are. Make sure the answer is clear:
> the recommendation is **ours**, not just one team's.

Notice what's happening before any rule. Nobody says *"that's my"* or *"it's her"*. When the
word stands **alone** — with no noun after it — English switches to a special form: **mine,
yours, hers, ours**. And to ask *who owns this?* it uses one word: **whose**.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Two families of words, easy to confuse because Portuguese uses **one** word for both.

| Owner | Possessive **adjective** (Unit 057) — comes **before a noun** | Possessive **pronoun** (Unit 058) — stands **alone** |
|---|---|---|
| I | **my** deck | **mine** |
| you | **your** deck | **yours** |
| he | **his** deck | **his** |
| she | **her** deck | **hers** |
| it | **its** value | *(no pronoun form — see trap below)* |
| we | **our** deck | **ours** |
| they | **their** deck | **theirs** |

**The rule in one line:** if a **noun follows**, use the adjective (*my deck*). If the word
**ends the phrase** and the noun is understood, use the pronoun (*It's **mine**.*).

- **Question of ownership:** **Whose** + noun → *"**Whose** deck is this?"* or **Whose** alone
  → *"**Whose** is this?"*
- **Answer:** *"It's **mine** / **yours** / **hers** / **ours** / **theirs**."*
- **Names** work like the pronoun: *"It's **Sofia's**."* (with *-'s*, Unit 061 territory).

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** So you can say *who owns it* **without repeating the
noun**. Instead of *"That's my deck and this is your deck,"* English compresses the second
one: *"That's my deck and this is **yours**."* The pronoun exists purely to avoid saying the
noun twice. It answers *whose?* and it closes a sentence cleanly.

**Decision rule vs the rival (adjective vs pronoun).** Same idea "belongs to me," two forms
— and the deciding signal is **what comes next**:

| Test | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A **noun** comes right after | adjective (*my, your, her, our*) | *"**My** roadmap** is ready."* |
| **Nothing** comes after (noun understood) | pronoun (*mine, yours, hers, ours*) | *"The roadmap is **mine**."* |

Minimal pair — watch the meaning stay identical while the form flips:

- *"This is **my** decision."* (noun *decision* follows → adjective)
- *"This decision is **mine**."* (nothing follows → pronoun)

**Second rival: `whose` vs `who's`.** They sound identical, so ears don't help — only the
meaning does.

- **whose** = *de quem / cujo* (ownership): *"**Whose** budget is this?"*
- **who's** = **who is** / **who has** (a shortened verb): *"**Who's** presenting today?"*
- Test: expand it to *who is*. If the sentence still makes sense → *who's*. If it breaks →
  *whose*. *"Whose is this?"* → *"Who is is this?"* ❌ → so it's **whose**.

**PT→EN mapping.** Portuguese uses the **same** word for both jobs: *"É o **meu** relatório"*
(adjective) and *"O relatório é **meu**"* (pronoun) — *meu* both times. English forces a
switch to **mine** in the second case. That single-word habit is exactly what trips Brazilian
speakers: they carry *meu* straight over and say *"it's my"*, which is incomplete in English.
Also, Portuguese *de quem* becomes one word, **whose** — not *"of who"*.

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. ❌ *"The deck is **my**."* → ✅ *"The deck is **mine**."*
   *(No noun after it, so the adjective can't stand there — you need the pronoun.)*
2. ❌ *"It's **her**."* (meaning *it belongs to her*) → ✅ *"It's **hers**."*
   *("It's her" is a different sentence — pointing at a person. For ownership: **hers**.)*
3. ❌ *"**Who's** budget is this?"* / ❌ *"**Her's** / **your's**"* → ✅ *"**Whose** budget…"*
   / ✅ *"**hers** / **yours**"*. *(Possessive pronouns take **no apostrophe** — ever.)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"The board will ask **whose** numbers these are — the answer is **ours**."*
   *— whose asks the owner; ours closes the sentence with no noun repeated. "Are our" would be wrong.*
2. *"That risk assessment is **hers**; the investment thesis is **mine**."*
   *— both stand alone after* is *→ pronouns. Note* hers *has no apostrophe.*
3. *"Is the final call **yours** or the CEO's?"*
   *— yours (pronoun, noun understood) parallel to the name form* CEO's*; you'd never say "is the final call your".*
4. *"**Whose** is this?" — "It's **theirs** — the data team owns that model."*
   *— whose alone (noun dropped) + theirs alone; the cleanest way to ask and answer ownership.*
5. *"We should position the roadmap as a company bet, not just **mine**."*
   *— mine ends the contrast cleanly; "not just my bet" also works, but the pronoun avoids repeating* bet.
6. *"The AI budget isn't **ours** to approve — that's the board's."*
   *— ours (no following noun) vs the name form* board's*; interference tempts "isn't our to approve".*
7. *"Whose laptop is this on the table? — I think it's **his**."*
   *— everyday life; note* his *is the same as adjective and pronoun, the one form that doesn't change.*
8. *"These aren't your keys — **yours** are the ones with the red tag."*
   *— everyday; first* your *is an adjective (before* keys*), second* yours *stands alone. Same idea, two forms.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Review Week 008):**
1. *"We **made** a decision after we **did** the analysis."* — *make* = you end with a new
   thing (a decision); *do* = an activity performed (analysis). Test: is there a product at
   the end? → *make*.
2. *"Let's **get aligned**."* — *get* = **become** / cross into a state we're not in yet;
   *be aligned* would claim the agreement already exists.
3. *"…we had to **put it off**."* — with a **pronoun** object the phrasal verb **must**
   split: *put **it** off*, never *"put off it"*.

**Spaced review:**
- *(Unit 051)* *"I look forward to **showing** the board…"* — *look forward to* ends in the
  **preposition** *to* (test: *"look forward to **it**"* works), so the verb takes **-ing**,
  not the base form.
- *(Unit 028)* *"**Shall** I present it?"* — *Shall I…?* is the polite offer/suggestion with
  *I* (*"quer que eu…?"*); it puts the decision in the listener's hands more naturally than a
  flat *"Do you want me to…?"*.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ✅ *"…is **ours**, not one team's."* — nothing follows, so the pronoun *ours* replaces the
   adjective *our*.
2. **Whose** — it asks about ownership of the deck. (*Who's* = *who is*, which breaks here:
   *"Who is deck…"* ❌.)
3. *"That's **her** call to make."* (noun *call* follows → adjective) / *"The call is
   **hers**."* (nothing follows → pronoun).
4. ✅ *"Is this budget **yours** or **hers**?"* — possessive pronouns take **no apostrophe**;
   *your's / her's* don't exist.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Inglês separa em **duas palavras** o que o português resolve com uma só. Antes de um
substantivo, use o **adjetivo** (*my* deck, *her* budget); quando a palavra fica **sozinha**,
sem substantivo depois, o inglês troca para o **pronome possessivo**: *mine, yours, his,
**hers**, ours, theirs*. A regra prática é olhar o que vem **depois**: tem substantivo →
adjetivo; não tem → pronome (*"The deck is **mine**"*). Para perguntar de quem é algo, uma
palavra só: **whose** (*"**Whose** is this?"*).

As armadilhas nascem do *meu/minha*, que em português serve para os dois casos. Isso faz o
brasileiro dizer *"it's my"* (❌) em vez de *"it's **mine**"*, e *"it's her"* (❌, aponta a
pessoa) em vez de *"it's **hers**"* (posse). Cuidado também com **whose** vs **who's**
(*who's* = *who is*; teste expandindo para *who is*) e lembre: pronome possessivo **nunca**
leva apóstrofo — é *hers*, *yours*, *theirs*, jamais *her's* ou *your's*.
