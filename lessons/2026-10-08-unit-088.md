### 1. 📘 Unit 088 — word order 1: keep *verb + object* together · *place before time*

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** How did the European launch go?
> **You:** Well. We **launched the product in Europe last quarter**, and adoption beat the forecast.
> **CFO:** And the budget — did you check the numbers?
> **You:** Yes. We **reviewed the budget carefully** before the board saw it. The team **presented the roadmap
> to the C-level yesterday**, and nobody pushed back.
> **CEO:** Good. I **read your memo this morning** — clear and tight.
> **You:** Thank you. I **know this market well**, so the positioning wasn't hard to land.

Listen to the *shape* of these sentences before any rule. Two quiet patterns are doing the work. First, the
**verb and its object stay glued together** — *reviewed **the budget** carefully*, *know **this market** well*,
*read **your memo** this morning*: nothing slips **between** the verb and what it acts on. Second, when both a
**place** and a **time** appear, the **place comes first**: *the product **in Europe** (where) **last quarter**
(when)*, *the roadmap **to the C-level** (where) **yesterday** (when)*. English is strict about this; Portuguese
is relaxed — and that's exactly where the interference lives.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Two simple ordering rules that carry most of the weight in a clear English sentence:

| Rule | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Verb + object stay together** | *subject* + **verb** + **object** + (rest) | *We reviewed **the budget** carefully.* |
| **2. Place before time** | … **where** → **when** | *We launched it **in Europe** **last quarter**.* |

**Rule 1 — don't split verb and object.** Whatever the object is, put it **right after the verb**. An adverb of
manner (*well, carefully, quickly*) or a degree phrase (*very much, a lot*) goes **after** the object, not
between verb and object.

- ✅ *I like **my job** very much.* — ❌ *I like very much my job.*
- ✅ *We reviewed **the proposal** carefully.* — ❌ *We reviewed carefully the proposal.*
- ✅ *She speaks **English** fluently.* — ❌ *She speaks fluently English.*

**Rule 2 — place before time.** When a sentence ends with both a **where** and a **when**, the **where** comes
first and the **when** comes last.

| Subject + verb (+ object) | **Place (where)** | **Time (when)** |
|---|---|---|
| We launched the product | **in Europe** | **last quarter.** |
| The team presented the roadmap | **to the C-level** | **yesterday.** |
| I'll see you | **at the office** | **tomorrow.** |

- A **time** phrase can also jump to the **front** for emphasis: *Last quarter, we launched the product in
  Europe.* But it can **not** sit in the middle, between verb and object: ❌ *We launched last quarter the
  product.*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these rules exist?** English has almost no case endings, so **position carries meaning**. The listener
knows *the budget* is the thing reviewed **only because** it sits right after *reviewed*. Break the glue and the
sentence stops sounding native instantly — it's the single most common "accent in the grammar" tell for a
Portuguese speaker, even when every word is correct. These rules are what make strategic English sound
**crisp** rather than translated.

**Decision rule vs the rival (Portuguese word order).** The rival here isn't another English structure — it's
the **word order your first language hands you**. Portuguese lets the adverb or a time phrase slide in right
after the verb; English does not. So the decision is a reflex to build:

| What you want to say | ❌ Portuguese-ordered English | ✅ English order |
|---|---|---|
| *Gosto muito do meu trabalho* | *I like very much my job.* | *I like **my job** very much.* |
| *Revisamos cuidadosamente o orçamento* | *We reviewed carefully the budget.* | *We reviewed **the budget** carefully.* |
| *Lançamos no ano passado o produto na Europa* | *We launched last quarter the product in Europe.* | *We launched **the product** in Europe last quarter.* |

Minimal pairs — same words, only the order moves:
- *We discussed **the risk** openly in the meeting.* ✅ (object glued, then manner, then place)
- *We discussed openly **the risk** in the meeting.* ❌ (adverb split the verb from its object)

**PT→EN mapping.** Portuguese word order is flexible precisely where English is rigid:
- *muito / bastante* after the verb → in English the degree word goes **after the object**: *"gosto muito
  **disso**"* → *"I like **it** a lot"*, never *"I like a lot it."*
- adverb of manner right after the verb in PT (*"analisamos rapidamente os dados"*) → English puts it **after
  the object** or at the end: *"we analysed **the data** quickly."*
- PT happily puts time before place (*"ontem no escritório"*) → English prefers **place before time**: *"at the
  office **yesterday**."* (Fronting the time for emphasis — *"Yesterday, at the office…"* — is the one allowed
  exception.)

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"I like very much this strategy."* → ✅ *"I like **this strategy** very much."* The object (*this
   strategy*) must sit **next to** the verb; *very much* waits until after it.
2. ❌ *"We reviewed carefully every assumption in the model."* → ✅ *"We reviewed **every assumption** in the
   model carefully."* Don't wedge the manner adverb between *reviewed* and its object.
3. ❌ *"The team presented yesterday the roadmap to the board."* → ✅ *"The team presented **the roadmap** to the
   board yesterday."* Keep verb + object together **and** send the time *(yesterday)* to the end — place
   *(to the board)* before time.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **We reviewed the budget carefully before the board meeting.**
   *— verb + object (reviewed the budget) stay glued; the manner adverb "carefully" waits until after the
   object. "reviewed carefully the budget" is the classic split.*
2. **The team presented the AI roadmap to the C-level yesterday.**
   *— place before time: "to the C-level" (where) comes before "yesterday" (when); and the object "the AI
   roadmap" sits right after "presented."*
3. **I know this market well, so the positioning was easy to defend.**
   *— "know this market" (verb + object) together; "well" (manner) lands after the object, never "know well
   this market."*
4. **The board approved the AI budget in the October session.**
   *— object "the AI budget" glued to "approved"; "in the October session" is a time/occasion phrase at the
   end — nothing splits the verb from its object.*
5. **We'll launch the new platform in Latin America next year.**
   *— place ("in Latin America") before time ("next year"); the object ("the new platform") sits directly after
   the verb.*
6. **Next quarter, we'll scale the pilot across all three regions.** *(time fronted for emphasis)*
   *— the one allowed move: the time phrase jumps to the front. It still can't go in the middle — "we'll scale
   next quarter the pilot" is wrong.*
7. **She explained the investment case clearly to the whole room.** *(strategy / everyday blend)*
   *— verb + object ("explained the investment case") together; "clearly" (manner) after the object, then the
   place ("to the whole room").*
8. **I read your email this morning and I really liked your proposal.** *(everyday)*
   *— "read your email" and "liked your proposal" each keep verb + object glued; "really" sits before the verb,
   which is fine — it's "very much it" / splitting an object that English forbids.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **"don't split it" drill**: they give you a verb + object + an adverb (*review / the
  budget / carefully*; *present / the vision / clearly*; *know / this market / well*) and you must say the
  sentence with the verb and object glued and the adverb at the end — fast, back to back — until *"I like very
  much it"* feels physically wrong in your mouth. This is the single tell that marks translated English.
- Then a **place-before-time round** anchored in your world: the tutor names a *where* and a *when* (*in Europe
  / last quarter*; *to the board / tomorrow*; *across the region / next year*) and you fold them into a project
  update in the right order — *"We'll present the strategy **to the board tomorrow**."* Doing it out loud trains
  the reflex so it survives a live executive meeting.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 087):**
1. **the** — *"**the** biggest risk in the portfolio."* A superlative **always** carries *the* (it's
   obligatory, even though spoken Portuguese can drop *o/a*). The comparative *"a bigger risk than budget"*
   only ranks talent against **one** rival (budget); here you're ranking it against the **whole set**, which is
   the superlative's job.
2. **"the most biggest" → "the biggest."** Never combine *-est* and *most*. Short adjective *big* takes *-est*
   **alone**; *most* is reserved for long adjectives (*the most expensive*) — one route only, never both.
3. **in** — *"the biggest AI player **in** the market."* Use *in* for a **place or group** (*in the world, in
   the industry*). *Of* would be right only for a **time span or explicit set** (*the best **of** the year, the
   best **of** the three*). Portuguese *do mercado* tempts *of*, but it's *in* here.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 081:** ✅ *"The team worked **hard** all quarter."* You mean *with effort* → **hard** (no *-ly*).
   *Hardly* is a different word meaning *almost not*, so *"worked hardly"* reverses your message to "the team
   barely worked" — the opposite of praise.
- **Unit 058:** ✅ *"It's **hers**."* When the possessive word **stands alone** (no noun after it), English uses
   the **pronoun** form *hers*, not the adjective *her*. *"It's her"* is ungrammatical — *her* must be followed
   by a noun (*her model*); alone, it becomes *hers*.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **"discussed openly the risk" → "discussed the risk openly."** The manner adverb *openly* split the verb
   from its object; keep *discussed + the risk* together, then the adverb.
2. **(b)** *I like **the new strategy** very much.* The object must sit next to the verb; *very much* waits
   until after it. *(a)* follows Portuguese order (*gosto muito de…*) and is the classic interference error.
3. **in Berlin, last month** — *"We opened a new office **in Berlin last month**."* **Place before time**:
   *where* (in Berlin) first, *when* (last month) last.
4. ✅ *"The team delivered **the final model** to the board **yesterday**."* Two fixes: keep *delivered + the
   final model* together (don't wedge *yesterday* between them), and send the **time** (*yesterday*) to the
   **end**, after the **place** (*to the board*).

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Esta unidade é sobre **ordem das palavras** — a área onde o português mais atrapalha, mesmo quando todas as
palavras estão certas. Duas regras carregam quase tudo. **(1) Verbo e objeto ficam colados:** nada entra
**entre** o verbo e o que ele atinge. O advérbio de modo (*carefully, well*) ou o grau (*very much, a lot*) vem
**depois do objeto** — *"I like **my job** very much"*, nunca *"I like very much my job."* **(2) Lugar antes de
tempo:** quando a frase termina com *onde* e *quando*, o **lugar** vem primeiro e o **tempo** por último —
*"We launched the product **in Europe** (onde) **last quarter** (quando)."* O tempo pode ir para o **começo**
por ênfase (*"Last quarter, we launched…"*), mas **nunca** para o meio.

As armadilhas são todas de interferência do português, que é flexível justamente onde o inglês é rígido. (1) O
*"muito"* depois do verbo em português vira, em inglês, *very much / a lot* **depois do objeto**: *"gosto muito
disso"* → *"I like **it** a lot"*, jamais *"I like a lot it."* (2) O advérbio de modo logo após o verbo
(*"analisamos rapidamente os dados"*) em inglês vai **depois do objeto**: *"we analysed **the data** quickly."*
(3) Português costuma pôr tempo antes de lugar; o inglês prefere **lugar antes de tempo**. Treine o reflexo em
bloco — *verbo + objeto + (modo) + lugar + tempo* — porque é essa ordem que faz seu inglês soar **nativo e
afiado** numa reunião de board, e não traduzido.
