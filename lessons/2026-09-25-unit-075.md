### 1. 📘 Unit 075 — relative clauses 4: extra-information (non-defining) clauses

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Where do we stand on the AI budget?
> **You:** Our CFO, **who joined in May**, already backs it. Phase one, **which the board approved last
> week**, starts Monday.
> **CEO:** And the team behind it?
> **You:** Priya, **whose team owns delivery**, will present the roadmap. We're moving the offsite to
> Lisbon, **where the whole leadership team can finally meet in one room**.
> **CFO:** Good. One more thing — the board approved the *full* number, not the reduced one.
> **You:** They approved the full budget, **which surprised everyone**. That changes our whole hiring plan.

Look at the commas. Each clause here is **extra information** you could remove and the sentence still
makes sense: *Our CFO already backs it* is complete on its own — *who joined in May* just adds colour.
That's the whole unit: some relative clauses **identify** which noun you mean (no commas), and some
just **add a fact** about a noun you've already identified (commas). And notice the last one — *which
surprised everyone* — doesn't point at a noun at all; it comments on **the whole event**.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

There are two kinds of relative clause. Everything in Units 072–074 was the **first** kind. Today is
the **second**.

| | **Defining** (Units 72–74) | **Extra-information** (today) |
|---|---|---|
| Job | tells you **which one** | just **adds a fact** about a known one |
| Commas | **no** commas | **commas** around the clause |
| *that* allowed? | ✅ yes (*the deal **that** she closed*) | ❌ **never** — use *who / which / whose / where* |
| Drop the pronoun? | ✅ often (*the deal she closed*) | ❌ **never** — the pronoun must stay |
| Remove the clause? | meaning breaks (which one?) | sentence still works |

**Key facts:**
- **Two commas, not one.** The clause is fenced off on **both** sides: *"Priya**,** whose team owns
  delivery**,** will present."* (If the clause ends the sentence, the second comma is the full stop:
  *"…starts Monday, **which surprised everyone.**"*)
- **In extra-information clauses you cannot use *that*, and cannot drop the pronoun.** *"Our CFO, ~~that~~
  joined in May"* and *"Our CFO, joined in May"* are both wrong → *"Our CFO, **who** joined in May."*
- **which for a whole idea.** When the relative clause comments on the **entire preceding sentence**
  (not one noun), use ***which*** — never *what*: *"They approved the full budget, **which** surprised
  everyone."* (= and **that fact** surprised everyone.)
- All the words from Unit 074 still apply here (*who, whom, whose, where, which*) — you just add commas
  and lose *that* / the dropping option.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (a seção central)

**Why does this structure exist?** Because there are two completely different reasons to attach a
clause to a noun. Sometimes you need it to **pick out the right one** from several: *"The vendor **that
scored highest** won"* — without the clause, *which vendor?* is unanswered. Other times the noun is
**already identified** and you're just layering in a fact: *"Our CFO, **who joined in May**, backs it"*
— you have exactly one CFO; the clause doesn't narrow anything, it just tells you more. English marks
this difference with **commas**, and the commas change the meaning. Getting them right is what makes
your written English read as precise rather than approximate — exactly what a board deck or a strategy
memo needs.

**Decision rule — the comma test (the whole game):**
Ask: *if I delete the clause, do I still know which noun we're talking about?*

| Situation | Commas? | Word | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| the clause **identifies** which one (drop it → *which one?*) | **no** | who / that / which / — | *the exec **who owns delivery** presented* |
| the noun is **already unique/named**, clause just **adds** info | **yes** | who / which / whose / where (**never** *that*) | *Priya**,** who owns delivery**,** presented* |
| the clause comments on the **whole sentence** | **yes** (one comma) | **which** | *the deal closed early**,** which impressed the board* |

The comma quietly changes meaning. Compare:
- *"My colleagues **who work in AI** back the plan."* (no commas) → **only** the AI ones back it;
  others may not. The clause **selects a subset**.
- *"My colleagues**,** who work in AI**,** back the plan."* (commas) → **all** my colleagues back it,
  **and** they all work in AI. The clause is just extra info.

Same words, opposite claim. That comma is doing strategic work.

**PT→EN mapping — where this helps Levi and where it trips him.**
- Good news: **Portuguese has the exact same distinction** — *oração adjetiva **restritiva*** (no
  commas) vs ***explicativa*** (with commas): *"os líderes **que investiram**…"* (restritiva) vs
  *"o CFO**,** que entrou em maio**,**…"* (explicativa). So the **concept** transfers directly — trust
  your Portuguese instinct about the commas here.
- The trap: in the **explicativa** (comma) version, Portuguese keeps using ***que*** — *"o CFO, **que**
  entrou em maio."* Levi then copies *que* → *that*: ❌ *"our CFO, **that** joined in May."* In English,
  *that* is **banned** after a comma; it must become **who / which**.
- The second trap: *"o que"* commenting on a whole idea (*"aprovaram o orçamento, **o que** surpreendeu
  todos"*) becomes ***which***, **not** *what*: ❌ *"…, **what** surprised everyone"* → ✅ *"…, **which**
  surprised everyone."*

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"Our CFO, **that** joined in May, backs the budget."* → ✅ *"Our CFO, **who** joined in May, backs
  the budget."* *(after a comma, never* that *— use* who/which*.)*
- ❌ *"The board approved the budget, **what** surprised everyone."* → ✅ *"…the budget, **which**
  surprised everyone."* *(a clause commenting on the whole sentence takes* which*, not* what*.)*
- ❌ *"Priya**,** who leads delivery will present."* (missing the **second** comma) → ✅ *"Priya**,** who
  leads delivery**,** will present."* *(extra-info clauses need commas on* both *sides.)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"Our CFO, **who joined in May**, already backs the AI budget."*
   *— one CFO, already identified → extra info, commas, "who" (never "that" after a comma).*
2. *"Phase one, **which the board approved last week**, starts Monday."*
   *— "phase one" is unique, so the clause just adds a fact → commas + "which"; you can't drop the pronoun here.*
3. *"They approved the full budget, **which surprised everyone**."*
   *— "which" points at the whole event (the approval), not at "budget"; this is the whole-sentence "which", never "what".*
4. *"Priya, **whose team owns delivery**, will present the roadmap."*
   *— non-defining "whose": Priya is named, the clause adds who she is; note the comma on BOTH sides.*
5. *"We're moving the offsite to Lisbon, **where the whole team can finally meet**."*
   *— "Lisbon" is a specific place already named → extra info about it → comma + "where".*
6. *"The exec **who owns delivery** presented first."*
   *— CONTRAST with #4: no commas here, because the clause identifies WHICH exec — drop it and you don't know who. Defining, so "who/that" both fine.*
7. *"Our new AI lead, **whom the board interviewed twice**, starts next month."*
   *— non-defining + object + formal → "whom"; because there are commas, you cannot drop it or use "that".*
8. *"She missed the deadline, **which is not like her at all**."*
   *— everyday life: "which" comments on the whole fact (missing the deadline), the classic whole-sentence use.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Ask your tutor to run a **comma-flip drill**: they give you a noun plus a fact (*"our CFO — joined
   in May," "the vendor — scored highest," "my colleagues — work in AI"*) and you must say **both**
   versions aloud — the defining one (no commas, *identifies which*) and the non-defining one (commas,
   *just adds a fact*) — and explain how the meaning changes. This locks in the one decision that
   matters and kills the *"that"*-after-comma reflex.
2. Role-play a **board update on the AI portfolio**: describe three or four bets, deliberately using an
   extra-information clause on each named item (*"Phase one, **which** the board approved, …," "Priya,
   **whose** team owns delivery, …," "Lisbon, **where** we'll run the offsite, …"*) — and end at least
   one sentence with the whole-sentence *which* (*"We beat every target, **which** changes our 2027
   plan"*). Have the tutor stop you the moment you say *that* or *what* where *who / which* belongs.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 074):**
1. **whose** — *"The CEO **whose** vision set the year."* Test: expand to *who is* → "who is vision set
   the year" is nonsense, so it's the possessive **whose**, not *who's* (= who is / who has).
2. **Not *where*** — a *decision* is an **abstract** noun, not a place you can physically stand in, so
   use *"a decision **in which** we underestimated the risk"* (or reword: *"a decision we got wrong"*).
   *where* only fits a **real place**; this is the *onde* over-reach.
3. **whom / who** → *"the candidate **whom** we hired"* (a new subject *we* follows → **object** → whom,
   or drop it in speech) vs *"the candidate **who** built the model"* (a **verb** *built* follows →
   **subject** → who). The clue: verb after the gap → *who*; new subject after the gap → *whom*.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 068:** *"**Most of our** flagship projects are over budget."* You mean **your** specific three,
   so *of our* localises it to your team. *"Most projects are over budget"* is a claim about projects
   **everywhere** — it hides your problem and sounds like an industry excuse. The *of* is the switch
   from *the whole category* to *this defined group*.
- **Unit 045:** **to align** → *"I called the CFO **to align** on the number."* Purpose of an **action**
   is *to* + base verb; *para* + verbo → *to* + verb, **never** *for* + verb. *"for align"* is the
   classic *para* interference.

**Recognition drill:**
1. *"My colleagues **who work in AI** back the plan"* (no commas) = **only** the AI colleagues back it —
   the clause **selects a subset**, so others may not. *"My colleagues**,** who work in AI**,** back the
   plan"* (commas) = **all** of them back it, and by the way they all work in AI. The commas turn
   *"which ones"* into *"extra fact."*
2. Wrong: **that** after a comma. It's an extra-information clause (the CEO is already identified — you
   have one CEO), so *that* is banned → *"Our CEO, **who** set the vision, is stepping down."* Rule:
   after a comma, only *who / which / whose / where*, never *that*, and you can't drop the pronoun.
3. **which** — *"…beat every target, **which** nobody expected."* *which* points at the **whole event**
   (the pilot beating every target), not at a single noun. A clause commenting on the whole sentence
   takes *which*, never *what*.
4. **Two commas** — *"Priya**,** whose team owns delivery**,** will present the roadmap."* Priya is
   named (already identified), so the clause is extra information and must be fenced on **both** sides;
   one comma alone is a common slip.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Existem **duas** orações relativas. As das Units 72–74 eram **restritivas** (sem vírgula): dizem **qual**
substantivo você quer — tire a oração e você não sabe mais de quem se fala (*"the vendor **that scored
highest**"*). A de hoje é a **explicativa** (com vírgula): o substantivo **já está identificado** e a
oração só **acrescenta um fato** (*"our CFO**,** **who joined in May**, backs it"* — você só tem um
CFO). A vírgula muda o sentido: *"my colleagues **who work in AI** back it"* = só os de IA apoiam;
*"my colleagues**,** who work in AI**,** back it"* = **todos** apoiam. Use vírgula dos **dois** lados,
e para comentar a **frase inteira** use ***which*** (*"they approved the budget, **which** surprised
everyone"*).

As ciladas do português: (1) na explicativa o português mantém *que* (*"o CFO, **que** entrou em maio"*),
e você copia para *that* — mas depois de vírgula o inglês **proíbe** *that*; use **who / which**. (2) o
*"o que"* que resume uma ideia inteira vira ***which***, nunca *what* (*"…, **which** surprised everyone"*).
(3) não esqueça a **segunda** vírgula. A boa notícia: o português **tem** essa distinção (restritiva ×
explicativa), então confie no seu ouvido para a vírgula — só troque *que* por *who/which* e evite *that*
e *what*. Se a base de whose/where/whom ainda embolar, releia `lessons/2026-09-24-unit-074.md`.
