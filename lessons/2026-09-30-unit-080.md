### 1. 📘 Unit 080 — adjectives & adverbs 1: *quick* vs *quickly*

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** We lost the deal because we moved too slowly. Talk to me.
> **You:** Fair. We made a **quick** decision on paper, but we executed it **slowly** — legal sat on
> it for three weeks.
> **CEO:** So next time?
> **You:** Same speed, tighter process. We decide **fast**, then we move **decisively** — no gap.
> **CFO:** The last rollout went **well**, though. Adoption climbed **steadily**.
> **You:** It did. The team responded **quickly** once the incentives were **clear**.

Watch two shapes doing two jobs. **Quick, clear, well** sit next to *decision*, and after *were* —
they describe **things**. But **slowly, fast, decisively, steadily, quickly** sit next to *executed,
decide, move, climbed, responded* — they describe **how the action happened**. Same idea, different
job: *a **quick** decision* (the decision) vs *decide **quickly*** (the deciding).

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

**An adjective describes a noun. An adverb describes a verb** (and also an adjective or another
adverb — it tells you *how / how much*).

| | describes… | example |
|---|---|---|
| **adjective** | a noun / pronoun | *a **quick** decision · the plan is **clear*** |
| **adverb** | a verb | *decide **quickly** · she explained it **clearly*** |
| **adverb** | an adjective | *a **genuinely** critical call · **surprisingly** bold* |
| **adverb** | another adverb | *she reacted **incredibly** fast* |

**Making the adverb:** usually **adjective + -ly** — *careful → carefully*, *clear → clearly*,
*serious → seriously*. Small spelling shifts: *easy → easily*, *automatic → automatically*,
*true → truly*.

**Two you must memorise:**
- **good** (adjective) → **well** (adverb): *a **good** plan · the plan went **well***. There is no
  *"goodly"* and no *"went good."*
- **fast**, **hard**, **early**, **late** look the **same** as adjective and adverb — no *-ly*:
  *a **fast** decision · decide **fast*** (never *"fastly"*).

**The exception you learned yesterday still holds:** after *be, seem, look, feel, sound, taste,
smell, stay, get* → use the **adjective**, because it describes the subject, not the action:
*the numbers **look good*** (not *well*), *the room **stayed calm*** (not *calmly*).

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (the core section)

**Why does the adverb exist?** Because English refuses to let one word do two jobs. The adjective
locks onto a *thing*; the adverb locks onto an *action* (or a quality). *A **clear** strategy* is a
comment about the strategy. *She explained the strategy **clearly*** is a comment about her
explaining. Drop the *-ly* and you accidentally re-point the word at the wrong target.

**Decision rule vs the rival:** ask **one question — what is the word about?**
- About a **noun** → adjective: *a **rapid** rollout*, *the response was **weak***.
- About a **verb / adjective / adverb** → adverb: *rolled out **rapidly***, *responded **weakly***,
  *a **remarkably** weak response*.

Minimal pairs — the meaning is the same idea, but the grammar target moves:

| adjective (about the thing) | adverb (about the action) |
|---|---|
| *We made a **quick** decision.* | *We decided **quickly**.* |
| *It was a **clear** answer.* | *She answered **clearly**.* |
| *a **good** result* | *it worked out **well*** |
| *a **serious** risk* | *we take it **seriously*** |

**And the trap that reverses everything — the linking verb (yesterday's rule):**

| ✅ *The market reacted **badly**.* | *reacted* = real action → **adverb** |
| ✅ *The forecast **looks bad**.* | *looks* = linking verb → **adjective** (describes the forecast) |

So the same word choice flips depending on the verb: **action verb → adverb; linking verb →
adjective.** That single distinction resolves most "good vs well" confusion at the board table.

**PT→EN mapping.** Portuguese also builds adverbs from adjectives (*rápido → rapidamente*,
*claro → claramente*), so the *idea* transfers cleanly. But Brazilian colloquial speech constantly
uses the **bare adjective as an adverb** — *"ela fala rápido"*, *"respondeu claro"*, *"foi bom"* —
and that habit crosses straight into English, where it's wrong. *"Fala rápido"* → *"speaks fast"* is
fine (fast has no *-ly*), but *"respondeu claro"* → **"responded clearly"**, not *"responded clear."*
And *"deu bom / foi bom"* → **"went well"**, never *"went good."*

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"The team responded quick and the launch went good."* → ✅ *"…responded **quickly** and the
   launch went **well**."* (dropped *-ly*; *good→well* after an action verb.)
2. ❌ *"She explained the strategy very clear."* → ✅ *"…explained the strategy very **clearly**."*
   (*clear* points at the strategy; you mean **how** she explained.)
3. ❌ *"It was a real important decision."* → ✅ *"It was a **really** important decision."* (You're
   sharpening the adjective *important*, so you need the adverb *really*, not the adjective *real*.)

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **The board approved the AI budget surprisingly quickly.**
   *— `quickly` describes **how** they approved (the verb); `surprisingly` sharpens `quickly`. The
   noun `budget` would take an adjective, but neither word is about the budget here.*
2. **We make quick decisions, but we implement them carefully.**
   *— both shapes in one line: `quick` describes the noun `decisions`; `carefully` describes the verb
   `implement`. Same lesson, opposite jobs.*
3. **The pilot went well, so the CFO released the next tranche.**
   *— after the action verb `went` you need the adverb `well`; `"went good"` is the single most common
   interference slip at this level.*
4. **She presented the 2027 vision clearly and answered every challenge confidently.**
   *— both adverbs describe **how** she presented / answered — not the vision or the challenges.*
5. **It's a genuinely critical decision for the innovation portfolio.**
   *— `genuinely` is an adverb strengthening the adjective `critical`; an adjective (`genuine`) can't
   modify another adjective.*
6. **The market reacted badly to the delay, but the leadership stayed calm.**
   *— `badly` = adverb on the action `reacted`; `calm` = adjective after the linking verb `stayed`
   (it describes the leadership). One line, the whole decision rule.*
7. **Read the risk section carefully before you sign off.** *(everyday)*
   *— `carefully` describes **how** to read; `careful` would need a noun to attach to.*
8. **She speaks English fluently after a year in London.** *(everyday)*
   *— `fluently` describes the action `speaks`; `"speaks fluent"` drops the ending that Portuguese
   (`fluentemente`) would keep.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to role-play a CEO debrief on a deal that went wrong. Force yourself to narrate
  **how** things happened — *"we responded slowly", "legal reviewed it carefully", "the market
  reacted badly"* — so every action verb pulls an **adverb**, not a bare adjective.
- Then run the *good / well* drill live: describe three recent initiatives using *"went well",
  *"performed well"*, *"scaled well"* — and immediately contrast with *"the results look good",
  "the forecast seems good"* (linking verbs → adjective). Getting this flip automatic is worth ten
  minutes of pure repetition.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 079):**
1. **a bold, long-term strategic plan** — opinion (*bold*) before description, and within description
   the fixed order (age *long-term* → purpose/type *strategic*). Opinion always leads.
2. **"The numbers sound optimistic."** — after the linking verb *sound* you describe the subject
   (*the numbers*), so you need the **adjective**, never the adverb *optimistically*.
3. **"a brilliant young analyst"** — *opinion before age*: the opinion word (*brilliant*) must come
   before the factual age word (*young*).

**Spaced review:**
- **~1 week (Unit 073):** **Yes, it's optional** — *"the vendor we chose"*. Test: if the relative
   pronoun is the **object** of the clause (something else *does* the verb — *we* chose it), you can
   drop it. You could only **not** drop it if it were the subject (*"the vendor **who** chose us"*).
- **~1 month (Unit 049):** **"I watched the CFO hesitate, then nod."** — bare *hesitate*, because you
   saw the **complete** action from start to finish. *"hesitating"* would frame it as **in progress /
   mid-action**, which doesn't fit the "then nod" sequence.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **calmly** — *responded* is a real action verb, so the adverb describes **how** she responded.
2. **"went well"** — after the action verb *went*, use the adverb *well*, not the adjective *good*.
3. **surprisingly** — you're modifying the adjective *bold*, and only an adverb can modify an
   adjective (*surprising* would need a noun).
4. **good** — *look* here is a **linking verb** (it describes the numbers, not an action), so it takes
   the **adjective** *good*. This is the rival of today's rule: action verb → adverb, linking verb →
   adjective.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

O adjetivo descreve um **substantivo** (*a **quick** decision*, *the plan is **clear***); o advérbio
descreve um **verbo** — como a ação aconteceu — e também intensifica adjetivos e outros advérbios
(*decide **quickly***, *explained it **clearly***, *a **genuinely** critical call*). A regra de
decisão é uma pergunta só: **a palavra fala do quê?** Se fala da coisa → adjetivo; se fala da ação (ou
reforça outro adjetivo/advérbio) → advérbio (geralmente adjetivo + *-ly*). Decore duas: *good → well*,
e *fast/hard/early/late* que não mudam.

As armadilhas são de interferência do português falado. No dia a dia a gente usa o adjetivo cru como
advérbio (*"respondeu claro"*, *"foi bom"*), e isso vaza pro inglês: o certo é *"responded **clearly**"*
e *"went **well**"*, nunca *"responded clear"* / *"went good"*. E cuidado com o intensificador: *"a
**real** important decision"* está errado — para reforçar o adjetivo *important* você precisa do advérbio
**really**. Exceção que continua valendo da aula de ontem: depois de *look, seem, feel, sound, stay,
be* volta o **adjetivo** (*the numbers **look good***, não *well*) — verbo de ação puxa advérbio, verbo
de ligação puxa adjetivo.
