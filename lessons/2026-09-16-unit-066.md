### 1. 📘 Unit 066 — not + any / no / none

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Where are we on the AI budget?
> **You:** There's **no** approval yet. The board hasn't released **any** funds, so we have
> **none** to start the pilot.
> **CEO:** And the data to justify the case?
> **You:** We don't have **any** hard numbers yet — **none** from the market, **none** from the
> CFO. There's **no** benchmark we can point to.
> **CEO:** So what's blocking us? Who owns the delay?
> **You:** Honestly, **no one**. It's a sequencing problem, not a people problem. We just have
> **no** time to waste before Q4.

Notice three ways of saying *zero*, all living together. **not + any** rides on a **negative
verb** (*hasn't released any*, *don't have any*). **no** does the negating **by itself**, so the
verb stays **positive** (*there's no*, *we have no*). And **none** stands **alone** — the noun is
already understood, so you don't repeat it (*we have none*). For people, that "alone" form becomes
**no one**. Same meaning, different machinery — and English lets you use **only one** negative per
clause. That's the whole lesson.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Three tools for the same idea — *zero / not any* — chosen by **where** the word sits and **whether
a noun follows**.

| Situation | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| after a **negative** verb (*don't / isn't / hasn't*) | **any** + noun | *"We don't have **any** data."* |
| with a **positive** verb (the word does the negating) | **no** + noun | *"We have **no** data."* |
| at the **start of a sentence** | **no** (never *not any*) | *"**No** budget was approved."* |
| **alone**, noun already understood | **none** | *"How much budget? — **None**."* |
| **alone**, about **people** | **no one / nobody** | *"Who signed off? — **No one**."* |
| picking from a **known set** | **none of** + the/my/these | *"**None of** the options work."* |

**Key facts:**
- **not + any = no.** Two ways to say the same negative: *"We don't have **any** time" = "We have
  **no** time."* Pick one — **never both**.
- **One negative per clause.** English is not Portuguese here: *"We don't have **no** time"* stacks
  two negatives and is **wrong**. Either the verb is negative (*don't … any*) **or** the noun word
  is negative (*no …*) — not both.
- **no takes a positive verb**, because *no* is already the negative: *"We **have no** budget,"* not
  *"we don't have no budget."*
- **none** replaces **no + noun** when the noun is dropped: *"— How much runway? — **None**."*
  (= no runway). No noun comes after it.
- **none of** + a specific group needs *the / my / these / them*: *"**None of the** pilots is live."*
- **Verb after none:** singular is always safe and more formal (*"**None of it is** ready"*); with a
  plural countable, both are heard in practice (*"None of the pilots **is / are** live"*).

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do three forms exist?** English insists on **exactly one** negative marker per clause — no
more, no less. The catch: *any* is **not itself negative**. It's an "open, unspecified" word (from
Unit 065) that only *becomes* negative when a negative verb (*don't, isn't, hasn't*) sits in front
of it. *no* and *none*, by contrast, **carry the negative inside them**, so they pair with a
**positive** verb. Choose the wrong combination and you either **double** the negative
(*"don't have no"*) or **lose** it (*"we have any data"* — which says nothing coherent). Getting
this right is what makes your English sound native instead of translated.

**Decision rule #1 — any vs no (the verb decides).** Ask: *is the verb already negative?*
- **Negative verb** (*don't / doesn't / didn't / isn't / hasn't*) → **any**: *"We **didn't** approve
  **any** headcount."*
- **Positive verb** → **no** does the work: *"We approved **no** extra headcount."*

Minimal pair — same zero, mirror-image grammar:

| not + any (negative verb) | no (positive verb) |
|---|---|
| *"The board **hasn't** released **any** funds."* | *"The board released **no** funds."* |
| *"We **don't** have **any** benchmark."* | *"We have **no** benchmark."* |
| *"There **isn't any** time to lose."* | *"There's **no** time to lose."* |

*no* is a touch more **emphatic and formal** — it's the version you'd write in a board memo. *not
any* is the everyday spoken default. Same meaning; register is the only difference.

**Decision rule #2 — no vs none (is a noun coming?).** *no* is an **adjective** — it needs a noun
right after it (*no budget, no time*). *none* is a **pronoun** — it stands **alone**, replacing
*no + noun* once the noun is known.
- *"We have **no** budget."* (noun follows) → *"— How much budget do we have? — **None**."* (alone)
- ❌ *"We have none budget."* / ❌ *"— How much? — No."* (needs the noun, or needs *none*).

**Decision rule #3 — none vs no one/nobody (things vs people).** For **quantities, money, data,
things** → **none**. For **people** → **no one** (or *nobody*).
- *"— How much data do we have? — **None**."* (a thing/quantity)
- *"— Who approved the vendor? — **No one**."* (people — not *"none"* here)

**PT→EN mapping — where this quietly betrays Levi.** Portuguese runs on **double (even triple)
negatives** as the *normal, correct* pattern: *"**Não** temos **nenhum** dado," "**Ninguém não**
aprovou **nada**."* Every extra negative in Portuguese **reinforces**; in English every extra
negative **cancels** and reads as an error. So the single biggest trap is copying that structure
across:
- *"**Não** temos **nenhum** orçamento."* → ✅ *"We **don't** have **any** budget."* **or** *"We
  have **no** budget."* — **never** *"~~We don't have no budget~~."*
- *"**Nenhuma** das opções funciona."* → ✅ *"**None of the** options work(s)."*
- *"**Ninguém** aprovou."* → ✅ *"**No one** approved it."* (one negative — *no one* already is it).
- Also: *nada* = *nothing* (positive verb) **or** *anything* (negative verb): *"We achieved
  **nothing**" = "We **didn't** achieve **anything**"* — again, pick one negative.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. ❌ *"We **don't** have **no** data to back the forecast."* → ✅ *"We **don't** have **any** data,"*
   **or** *"We have **no** data."* — one negative per clause; the Portuguese *não…nenhum* stacking
   doesn't transfer.
2. ❌ *"We have **any** information about the vendor."* → ✅ *"We **don't** have **any** information,"*
   **or** *"We have **no** information."* — *any* alone (no negative verb) leaves the sentence
   without its negative; it needs *don't/isn't* in front, or switch to *no*.
3. ❌ *"— Who was at the board pre-read? — **None**."* → ✅ *"— **No one**."* — for **people** standing
   alone, use *no one / nobody*; *none* is for quantities and things.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"We have **no** budget approved for the AI pilot yet."*
   *— "no + noun" with the positive verb "have"; "no" does the negating, so never "don't have no". More emphatic than "don't have any" — right for a board line.*
2. *"The board **hasn't** released **any** funds, so the program can't start."*
   *— negative verb "hasn't" → "any"; this is the two-part negative done correctly (one negative, on the verb).*
3. *"How much runway do we have? — **None**."*
   *— "none" stands alone, replacing "no runway"; you don't repeat the noun. Answering a "how much" (quantity), so not "no one".*
4. *"**No** decision was made at yesterday's board meeting."*
   *— the noun phrase opens the sentence → must be "No", never "Not any"; "not any" can't start a sentence.*
5. *"**None of the** options the CFO proposed actually reduce cost."*
   *— "none of + the + plural" picks zero from a known set; contrast with a bare "no option" (no specific set in mind).*
6. *"We **don't** have **any** time to lose before the Q4 review."*
   *— the everyday-work default: negative verb "don't" → "any". Swap to "We have no time to lose" for a firmer, more formal ring.*
7. *"Who owns the delay on the roadmap? — **No one**. It's a sequencing problem."*
   *— people, standing alone → "no one" (not "none"); one word already carries the negative.*
8. *"There's **no** milk left, and there **isn't any** clean cup either."*
   *— everyday: "no + noun" on a positive "there's" vs "not any" on a negative "isn't" — both correct patterns for zero, side by side in one line.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Ask your tutor to run a **"kill the double negative" drill**: they say a Portuguese sentence
   built on stacked negatives (*"não temos nenhum dado," "ninguém aprovou nada," "não sobrou
   nenhum recurso"*) and you snap back **both** clean English versions each time — the *don't…any*
   one **and** the *no / none* one (*"We don't have any data / We have no data," "No one approved
   anything," "There aren't any resources left / There are no resources left"*). The goal is to
   break the reflex of carrying a second negative across.
2. Then a **"positive verb, negative meaning" switch**: the tutor gives you a negative-verb
   sentence and you convert it live to the emphatic *no*-form, and back — *"We didn't approve any
   headcount" ↔ "We approved no headcount," "There isn't any benchmark" ↔ "There's no
   benchmark."* This builds the board-memo register (*no / none*) alongside the everyday spoken one
   (*not any*), so you can pick the tone on purpose.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 065 — some / any, countable & uncountable):**
1. **any / some** → ✅ *"We don't have **any** data yet, but the CFO shared **some** useful
   research."* First blank is a **negative** statement → *any*; second is **affirmative** → *some*.
   (Both nouns are uncountable, so neither takes a number or plural — the same *data / research*
   traps.)
2. **some** → ✅ *"Would you like **some** feedback…?"* It's an **offer/request**, the special case
   where *some* wins even though the sentence is a question — you're assuming the feedback exists and
   offering it. *"any feedback"* here would sound cold, as if you doubt they want it.
3. Two fixes → ✅ *"The **information** in the deck **is** useful."* *information* is **uncountable**:
   no plural *-s* (never *informations*), and it takes a **singular** verb (*is*, not *are*).

**Spaced review:**
- *(Unit 059 — subject/object pronouns)* **me** → *"This stays between the CFO and **me**."* After
   the preposition *between*, English uses the **object** form; test by dropping the name —
   *"between **me**"* ✅, *"between **I**"* ✗. The polite-sounding *"between you and I"* is a classic
   overcorrection.
- *(Unit 038 — imperative)* Remove **both** *to*s → ✅ *"**Let's align** on the vision, and please
   **send** the deck by Friday."* *Let's* is followed by the **base verb** (*Let's align*, not
   *Let's to align*), and there's **no *to* after *please*** (*please send*, not *please to send*).

**Recognition drill:**
1. One negative, not two → ✅ *"We **don't** have **any** data,"* **or** *"We have **no** data."*
   English allows **one** negative per clause: put it on the **verb** (*don't … any*) **or** on the
   **noun** (*no*), never both. *"Don't have no"* is the direct copy of Portuguese *não…nenhum*.
2. **no** → ✅ *"The board **approved no** extra headcount."* The verb *approved* is **positive**, so
   the negative must live on the noun → *no*. If you switched to the negative verb *didn't approve*,
   you'd then need *any*: *"The board **didn't approve any** extra headcount."* Same zero, opposite
   machinery — the verb decides.
3. **No one** → ✅ *"— **No one**, honestly."* The question is about **people** (*who?*), and the
   answer stands **alone**, so it's *no one / nobody* — not *none* (which is for quantities/things).
4. **no / None** → ✅ *"We have **no** time for a tenth pilot. — And budget? — **None**."* *no* is an
   **adjective** and needs the noun after it (*no time*); *None* is a **pronoun** standing **alone**
   (= *no budget*), so the noun isn't repeated.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Existem três formas para dizer *zero* em inglês, e a escolha é mecânica. **not + any** vai com verbo
**negativo** (*don't / isn't / hasn't*), porque *any* sozinho **não** é negativo — ele só ganha
sentido negativo com o *não* no verbo: *"We **don't** have **any** budget."* Já **no** carrega o
negativo **dentro de si**, então o verbo fica **positivo**: *"We have **no** budget."* As duas
frases significam a mesma coisa (*no* soa um pouco mais enfático/formal, para memorando de board).
E **none** fica **sozinho**, substituindo *no + substantivo* quando o substantivo já é conhecido:
*"— How much budget? — **None**."* Para **pessoas** sozinhas, use **no one / nobody**, não *none*.
Regra de ouro: *no* precisa de substantivo depois; *none* fica só; a pergunta "tem substantivo
vindo?" decide entre os dois.

A grande armadilha é o **negativo duplo**. O português **empilha** negativas como padrão correto
(*"não temos nenhum dado," "ninguém aprovou nada"*), mas o inglês aceita **uma só** por oração —
cada negativa extra **cancela** e vira erro. Então nunca *"~~we don't have no data~~"*: escolha **um**
lado, ou o verbo (*don't … any*) ou o substantivo (*no / none*). E cuidado com *any* solto sem verbo
negativo (*"~~we have any data~~"*), que deixa a frase sem o seu negativo. Se o par *some × any* ou a
diferença contável × incontável ainda embolar, releia `lessons/2026-09-15-unit-065.md` (some/any) —
esta unidade é a continuação direta dela.
