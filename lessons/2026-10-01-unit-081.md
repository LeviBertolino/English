### 1. 📘 Unit 081 — adjectives & adverbs 2: *well · fast · late · hard / hardly*

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** You look tired. Are you OK?
> **You:** I'm **well**, thanks — just a long week. The team worked **hard** on the AI roadmap.
> **CEO:** How hard?
> **You:** Honestly, I've **hardly** slept. We stayed **late** three nights running.
> **CFO:** Has adoption moved at all **lately**?
> **You:** It's climbing **fast** now. Two weeks ago we'd **hardly** seen any uptake; this week the
> numbers are up forty percent.
> **CEO:** Good. Just don't burn the team out — we move **fast**, but we move **well**.

Watch the small words carefully, because two pairs look almost identical and mean opposite things.
**hard** (worked a lot) sits one line away from **hardly** (almost not at all) — adding *-ly* here does
**not** make an adverb of *hard*; it makes a completely different word. Same trap with **late** (not on
time) vs **lately** (recently). Meanwhile **well** is doing two jobs: *move **well*** (the adverb of
*good*) and *I'm **well*** (an adjective meaning *healthy*). And **fast** stays **fast** whether it
describes a thing or an action — no *-ly* ever.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Yesterday's default was *adjective + -ly → adverb*. This unit is the **exceptions** — the words that
break that rule. Four groups:

| Group | adjective | adverb | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **same word** | *a **fast** car · a **hard** job · a **late** flight · an **early** start* | *drive **fast** · work **hard** · arrive **late** · leave **early*** | **no *-ly*** — never *"fastly"*, *"hardly"* (as adverb of hard), *"lately"* (as adverb of late) |
| **good → well** | *a **good** plan* | *the plan went **well*** | *well* is the irregular adverb of *good* |
| ***well* = healthy** | *I feel **well** · she's not **well*** | — | here *well* is an **adjective** meaning *in good health* (not *good*) |
| **the false twins** | — | ***hardly*** = almost not · ***lately*** = recently | these are **real words with their own meaning** — not the adverbs of *hard* / *late* |

**The two dangerous pairs, side by side:**

| word | meaning | example |
|---|---|---|
| **hard** (adverb) | with a lot of effort | *The team worked **hard**.* |
| **hardly** | almost not / very little | *The team **hardly** worked.* (= did almost nothing) |
| **late** (adverb) | after the expected time | *We finished **late**.* |
| **lately** | recently, in the last while | *Adoption has grown **lately**.* |

**hardly** pairs with a few fixed partners: *hardly **any*** (= almost none), *hardly **ever*** (=
almost never), *hardly **anyone / anything***, *can **hardly*** (= almost can't). *"I can hardly hear
you."*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (the core section)

**Why do these exceptions exist?** Because a handful of very old, very common English words never took
the *-ly* ending — *fast, hard, late, early* work as both adjective and adverb unchanged. The problem is
that two of them (*hard, late*) **do** have an *-ly* form — but that form was captured long ago for a
**different meaning**. So *hardly* and *lately* are not "the adverbs of hard/late"; they are separate
words that happen to look related. Mistaking one for the other reverses what you say.

**Decision rule — the three traps:**

**(1) hard vs hardly.** Ask: *do I mean "with effort" or "almost not"?*
- *with effort* → **hard**: *We worked **hard** on the pitch.*
- *almost not* → **hardly**: *We **hardly** had time to prepare.*

| ✅ *The board worked **hard** to reach consensus.* | they put in a lot of effort |
| ✅ *The board **hardly** worked on it — they rubber-stamped it.* | they did almost nothing |

These two sentences are opposites. This is the single highest-value distinction in the unit.

**(2) late vs lately.** Ask: *do I mean "not on time" or "recently"?*
- *not on time / near the end* → **late**: *The report came in **late**.*
- *recently* → **lately**: *The report has slipped **lately**.*

**(3) well — adverb or adjective?** *Well* is the adverb of *good* (*the launch went **well***), **but**
*well* is also an **adjective** meaning *healthy*. So after *be / feel / look* (linking verbs),
*"I'm well"* and *"I feel well"* mean *I'm in good health* — both correct. *"I'm good"* is also used
(especially AmE, casual), but *well* is the safe, professional choice for health.

**PT→EN mapping.** This is where Portuguese quietly betrays you:
- *"ultimamente"* → **lately**, never *"late"*. *"Ultimamente a adoção cresceu"* → *"Adoption has grown
  **lately**"*, not *"grown late."*
- *"trabalhar duro / pesado"* → **work hard**. The adverb is *hard* — adding *-ly* gives *hardly*, which
  flips the meaning to *almost didn't work*. There is no safe *-ly* here.
- *"chegou tarde"* → *"arrived **late**"*, not *"arrived lately."*
- *"ele mal dormiu"* → *"he **hardly** slept"* — Portuguese *mal* ≈ English *hardly* (almost not); don't
  reach for *hard*.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"The team worked hardly all quarter."* → ✅ *"The team worked **hard** all quarter."* (You mean
   *with effort* — *hardly* says the opposite, that they barely worked.)
2. ❌ *"We haven't seen much growth late."* → ✅ *"…much growth **lately**."* (*late* = not on time;
   *lately* = recently.)
3. ❌ *"I'm good, thanks"* when asked about health in a formal setting → ✅ *"I'm **well**, thanks."*
   (*well* = the adjective for *in good health*; both exist, but *well* is the precise one.)

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **The team worked hard to get the AI budget approved before year-end.**
   *— `hard` is the adverb (with effort) and takes no `-ly`. Write `hardly` here and you'd say they
   barely worked — the opposite of your point.*
2. **We've hardly made any progress on the integration this sprint.**
   *— `hardly` = almost none; note its fixed partner `any`. This is a different word from `hard`, not
   its adverb form.*
3. **Adoption has climbed fast lately, especially in the sales org.**
   *— `fast` (no `-ly`) describes how it climbed; `lately` = recently. `late` would mean "not on time",
   which makes no sense here.*
4. **The board meeting started late because two directors were travelling.**
   *— `late` = after the scheduled time; it's both the adjective and the adverb unchanged.*
5. **The pilot went well, so I'm confident presenting it to the C-level.**
   *— `well` here is the irregular adverb of `good`, describing how the pilot went (recap of Unit 080).*
6. **"How are you?" "I'm well, thanks — just back from leave."**
   *— different `well`: here it's an adjective meaning healthy, after the linking verb `am`.*
7. **I could hardly hear the CFO on the call — the line kept dropping.** *(everyday)*
   *— `can / could hardly` = almost couldn't; a very common fixed pattern worth memorising whole.*
8. **She leaves early on Fridays but still delivers everything on time.** *(everyday)*
   *— `early` is the adverb unchanged (no `-ly`); it mirrors `late` as its opposite.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to drill the *hard / hardly* opposition live: describe one initiative where the team
  *"worked hard"* and one where you *"hardly had time"* / *"hardly made progress"* — back to back, until
  reaching for *hardly* to mean *effort* feels impossible. This single pair causes the most expensive
  misunderstandings at the executive level ("we hardly worked on it" is a very different message to a CEO).
- Then run a quick status round using *lately*: *"adoption has grown lately", "we've hardly shipped
  anything lately", "the board has met more often lately"* — so *ultimamente → lately* becomes automatic
  and never surfaces as *"late."*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 080):**
1. **clearly** — ask *"what is the word about?"* It's about **how** she explained (the verb), not about
   the strategy (the noun), so you need the adverb *clearly*.
2. **"went well"** — after the action verb *went* you need the adverb *well*; *good* is an adjective and
   can't describe the verb. *"went good"* is the classic interference slip.
3. **good** — *look* here is a **linking verb**, so the word describes the subject (*the numbers*), which
   takes the **adjective** *good*. Action verb → adverb; linking verb → adjective — that's the flip.

**Spaced review:**
- **~1 week (Unit 074):** **whose** — *"the vendor **whose** platform we piloted"*. It shows
   **possession**, standing in for *its* (the vendor, **its** platform). *Who's* = *who is* (wrong here);
   *that* can't show possession.
- **~1 month (Unit 052):** **got approved** — *"the vision finally **got approved**"*. This is
   *get + past participle*, the "soft passive": a change that **happened to** the subject. It replaces
   the heavier *"was approved"* in natural speech.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **hard** — you mean *with effort*. *Hardly* would say they barely worked — the opposite.
2. **hardly** — *"we've **hardly** touched the backlog"* = almost nothing got done. (*Hard* would need a
   different structure and would mean the reverse.)
3. **lately** — = *recently*. *Late* means *not on time*, which doesn't fit a question about recent change.
4. **"I'm well"** — for health in a careful/professional register, *well* is the precise adjective (*in
   good health*). *"I'm good"* is understood and common but informal; *well* is the safe upgrade.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Esta aula é a lista de **exceções** à regra de ontem (adjetivo + *-ly*). Primeiro: *fast, hard, late,
early* são iguais como adjetivo e advérbio — **não levam *-ly*** (*drive **fast***, *work **hard***,
*arrive **late***). Segundo: *good* tem advérbio irregular *well* (*went **well***), e *well* também é
**adjetivo** significando *saudável* (*I'm **well*** = estou bem de saúde). Terceiro, e mais importante:
*hardly* e *lately* **não** são os advérbios de *hard* e *late* — são palavras com significado próprio.
*hardly* = quase não (≈ *mal*: *he **hardly** slept* = ele mal dormiu), e *lately* = ultimamente.

As armadilhas são de tradução. *"Trabalhar duro"* é *"work **hard**"* — se você puser *-ly* (*hardly*)
você diz o **oposto**: que quase não trabalhou. *"Ultimamente"* é *"**lately**"*, nunca *"late"* (que é
*atrasado / tarde*). E *"mal consegui ouvir"* é *"I could **hardly** hear"*. Decore os pares em bloco —
*work hard* × *hardly worked*, *arrived late* × *lately* — porque trocar um pelo outro inverte a
mensagem, e no nível de board isso custa caro.
