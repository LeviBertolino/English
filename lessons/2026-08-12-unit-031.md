### 1. 📘 Unit 031 — can and could

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** **Can** we have the AI platform live before the offsite?
> **You:** We **can** hit the technical date, but we **can't** promise adoption
> that fast — teams need training.
> **CEO:** A year ago we **couldn't** even run these models internally. Big jump.
> **You:** True. **Could** you give me two more engineers? Then I **can** pull
> the launch forward.
> **CEO:** Done.

Notice the pattern before any rule. *can* is doing "possible / able right now"
(*we can hit the date*), *can't* is "not possible" (*we can't promise*). Then
two different *coulds*: *"a year ago we couldn't"* is the **past** of *can*, but
*"Could you give me…"* isn't past at all — it's a **polite request** happening
now. Same word, two jobs.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

*can* and *could* are modals, like *will* and *might*. Same form for every
subject, always followed by the **base verb** — no *-s*, no *to*, no *do/does*.

| | Positive | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present | **can** + base | **can't / cannot** + base | **Can** + subject + base? |
| Past / polite | **could** + base | **couldn't** + base | **Could** + subject + base? |

- One form for all subjects: *I / you / he / we / they **can** go.* Never *he
  cans*, never *can to go*.
- *can't* is the everyday negative; *cannot* is the full one word (not *can
  not*). Past negative is *couldn't / could not*.
- **could** has **two lives**: (a) the **past** of *can* — *"I could run 10 km
  at 20"*; (b) a **softer, more tentative present** — requests (*Could you…?*),
  suggestions (*We could wait*), possibility (*This could work*). Context, not
  the word, tells you which.
- For a future ability you don't have yet, *can* borrows **be able to**: *"We'll
  **be able to** scale next year,"* not *"we can scale next year"* when you mean
  the future capability.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these structures exist?** *can* packs three everyday meanings into one
small word: **ability** (*I can present*), **possibility** (*this can go wrong*),
and **informal permission/requests** (*Can I leave? / Can you help?*). *could*
then does two things nothing else does so neatly: it's the **past** of that
ability/possibility, and it's the **polite, tentative** version for the present.

**Decision rule #1 — the two faces of *could* (the confusion that bites most).**
When you see *could*, ask: is this about the **past**, or about **now, softened**?

| *could* = past ability | *could* = polite / tentative present |
|---|---|
| *"Ten years ago we **couldn't** run these models."* | *"**Could** you send the numbers today?"* (request now) |
| *"As a kid I **could** speak fluent French."* | *"We **could** wait for Q4."* (open suggestion now) |
| looks **backward** in time | looks at **now**, just more gently than *can* |

**Decision rule #2 — *can* vs *could* for requests (politeness, not time).**
Both ask now; *could* is simply softer and safer with senior people:
- *"**Can** you send me the deck?"* — fine, direct, colleague-level.
- *"**Could** you send me the deck?"* — more polite; the default with a CFO or
  the board. *could* here is **not** past — using it doesn't mean yesterday.

**Decision rule #3 — *can/could* vs *might* for possibility.** All three can mark
possibility, with a subtle split:
- *"This **can** go wrong"* = it's **generally possible** / it happens (a
  characteristic, a standing risk).
- *"This **might / could** go wrong"* = there's a **real chance in this specific
  case** (the tentative maybe you drilled in Unit 030).
So *can* leans "possible in principle," *might/could* lean "may actually happen
this time."

**The past-ability trap — *could* vs *was able to* / *managed to*.** For a
**general** past ability, *could* is right: *"I could read financial statements
early in my career."* But for **one specific achievement that succeeded**,
English drops *could* and uses *was able to* or *managed to*: *"We **were able
to** close the deal on Friday,"* not *"We could close the deal on Friday."*
(*Couldn't* is fine for the negative in both: *"We couldn't close it."*)

**PT→EN mapping.** Portuguese *poder* is the source of most of the interference,
because it covers **all** of these at once — ability, possibility, and
permission — while English splits them:
- *"Eu sei fazer isso"* / *"Eu consigo fazer isso"* → **"I can do it,"** not
  *"I know to do it."* English uses *can* for learned ability.
- *"Você poderia me mandar os números?"* → **"Could you send me the numbers?"**
  — the conditional *poderia* maps cleanly to polite *could*.
- *"Conseguimos fechar o acordo na sexta"* → **"We managed to / were able to
  close the deal on Friday,"** **not** *"We could close the deal on Friday"* — a
  single successful action, so not *could*.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"I can to present the strategy."* → ✅ *"I **can present** the strategy."*
  — *can* + **base verb**, never *can to*.
- ❌ *"I know to use this tool."* → ✅ *"I **can** use this tool."* — for ability,
  English uses *can*, not *know to*.
- ❌ *"We could sign the contract yesterday."* (meaning: we succeeded) → ✅ *"We
  **were able to** sign the contract yesterday."* — one specific past success
  needs *was able to / managed to*, not *could*.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"We **can** hit the technical launch date, but we **can't** promise adoption
   that fast."*
   *— Present ability/possibility: can states what's achievable now, can't what isn't.*
2. *"**Could** you give me two more engineers for the AI platform?"*
   *— Polite request happening now, not past; could softens the ask to the CEO.*
3. *"A year ago we **couldn't** run these models internally."*
   *— Genuine past: couldn't is the past of can't, pinned to "a year ago".*
4. *"We **were able to** close the funding round last week."*
   *— One specific past success → was able to, not "could close"; could would sound wrong here.*
5. *"An unmanaged AI rollout **can** create real reputational risk."*
   *— General standing possibility ("this is a thing that happens") → can, not might.*
6. *"The board **might** push back on the timeline, so we **could** prepare a fallback."*
   *— might = real chance this time; could = tentative suggestion for now (the soft present).*
7. *"**Can** you walk me through the innovation portfolio before the sync?"*
   *— Direct, colleague-level request; can is fine and natural with a peer.*
8. *"I **can't** join the 3 p.m. call, but I **could** do it at four."*
   *— can't = not possible now; could = a gentle alternative offer, present, not past.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **request-politeness ladder**: same ask, three
  registers — *"Can you send the deck?"* (peer), *"Could you send the deck?"*
  (senior), *"Could you possibly send the deck by noon?"* (careful). Practise
  picking the right rung for a CFO vs a teammate, and feel that *could* is about
  **politeness, not the past**.
- Then a **could vs was-able-to drill**: your tutor names past situations from
  your world (*"the exec team, five years ago"* → general ability; *"last
  Friday's negotiation"* → one specific success) and you produce *"We could
  read the market well"* vs *"We were able to close the deal"* — training the
  reflex that stops the classic *"we could close it yesterday"* error.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 030 — might):**
1. ***might*** both blanks — *"We **might** move… or we **might** wait"*: nothing
   is decided, so each is an **open possibility**; *will* would wrongly claim
   certainty about which one happens.
2. *"I **might open** a parallel negotiation."* — *might* + **base verb**; *might
   to* is never correct.
3. *"The CFO **might not sign** before the re-forecast."* — one modal (*might
   not*) absorbs the *talvez… não*; no extra *maybe*, verb stays in base form.

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 024)** ***taken*** — *"The board has **taken** a final position."* The
  helper *has* forces the **participle** *taken*; *took* is the bare past simple
  and can't follow a perfect helper.
- **(Unit 002)** *"**Is** the pilot already live?"* — the single move is
  **inverting subject and verb**: *the pilot is* → *is the pilot*. The *to be*
  verb jumps to the front; nothing else changes.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ❌ *can to* → ✅ *"I **can present** the AI roadmap on Thursday."* — *can* is a
   modal, so it's followed by the **base verb**, never *can to*.
2. ***were able to*** — *"we finally **were able to** sign the contract."* This is
   **one specific past success**, so English uses *was able to / managed to*, not
   *could* (which would fit only a **general** past ability).
3. ***might*** both times — *"the board **might** approve… or it **might** not."*
   The case is **specific and uncertain**, exactly *might*'s job; *can* would say
   it's *generally possible*, which misses the "this time, maybe" meaning.
4. **No — it's not past.** *"**Could** you send the numbers today?"* is a
   **polite request happening now**; *could* here softens the ask, it does not
   move it into the past.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** *can* concentra três sentidos num só: **habilidade**
(*I can present*), **possibilidade** (*this can go wrong*) e **pedido/permissão
informal** (*Can you help?*). É sempre *can/could* + **verbo na base**, igual
para todos os sujeitos, sem *-s* e sem *to*. O ponto delicado é o *could*, que
tem **duas vidas**: (a) o **passado** de *can* — *"a year ago we couldn't run
these models"*; e (b) uma versão **mais educada e tentativa do presente** —
pedidos (*Could you…?*), sugestões (*We could wait*), possibilidade específica
(*This could work*). O contexto, não a palavra, diz qual é.

**As armadilhas.** O vilão é o *poder* do português, que junta habilidade,
possibilidade e permissão que o inglês separa. (1) Nunca *can to* — depois de
*can* vem o verbo puro; e "sei fazer / consigo fazer" é *"I can do it"*, não *"I
know to do it"*. (2) *could* em pedido (*"Could you send…?"*) é **educação, não
passado** — não significa ontem. (3) A maior cilada: para **um sucesso
específico no passado**, use *was able to / managed to*, não *could* — *"We were
able to close the deal on Friday"*, jamais *"We could close the deal"*; *could*
só serve para habilidade **geral** no passado. Se as possibilidades (*can* vs
*might/could*) embolarem, releia `lessons/2026-08-11-unit-030.md`.
