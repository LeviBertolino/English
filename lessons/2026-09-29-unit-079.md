### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** First impression of the new strategy deck?
> **You:** Strong. It's a **clear, ambitious long-term** plan — and it **looks realistic**, not
> just aspirational.
> **CFO:** The numbers **sound optimistic** to me.
> **You:** They **seem aggressive** on paper, I agree. But the market data **feels solid**, and
> the modelling **smells right** once you see the assumptions.
> **CEO:** You **look tired**, though.
> **You:** Long quarter. Honestly the whole planning cycle has **been exhausting** — but the
> outcome **feels good**.

Two things are happening. First, notice the stack of adjectives *before* the noun — **a clear,
ambitious long-term plan** — in a fixed order that English won't let you rearrange. Second, look
at the words after **looks, sound, seem, feels, smells, been, look**: they're all **adjectives**
(*realistic, optimistic, aggressive, solid, right, tired, exhausting, good*) — never adverbs. You'd
never say *"it looks realistically"* or *"the numbers sound optimistically."*

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

**Part A — order of adjectives before a noun.** Opinion comes first, then the facts, in this order:

| Opinion | Size | Age | Shape/Colour | Origin | Material | Purpose | + NOUN |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *bold* | *large* | *long-term* | — | — | — | — | *strategy* |
| *impressive* | — | *new* | — | *American* | — | *AI* | *startup* |
| *clunky* | — | *old* | — | — | — | — | *dashboard* |

- **Opinion before description.** *a **brilliant young** analyst* (not *a young brilliant analyst*).
- Two adjectives of the **same type** get a comma or *and*: *a **clear, ambitious** plan*.

**Part B — adjectives after "linking" verbs.** After **be, get, become, seem, appear** and the
sense verbs **look, feel, sound, taste, smell**, use an **adjective**, not an adverb — because the
word describes the **subject**, not the action:

| Verb | + adjective | Describes |
|---|---|---|
| *look* | *the plan looks **realistic*** | the plan |
| *sound* | *the numbers sound **optimistic*** | the numbers |
| *feel* | *the data feels **solid*** | the data |
| *seem* | *the risk seems **manageable*** | the risk |
| *be / get* | *the CFO got **nervous*** | the CFO |

- Add **like** + a noun after the same verbs: *"It **looks like** a strong bet"*, *"That **sounds
  like** a plan."*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (a seção central)

**Why does adjective order exist?** English has no rule you can hear — it's a fixed habit native
speakers share. Break it (*"a long-term bold strategy"*) and nothing is *wrong* grammatically, but
it sounds foreign and makes the listener stumble. Getting it right is pure fluency signalling: it's
what makes *"a clear, ambitious long-term plan"* land as effortless instead of translated.

**Why adjective (not adverb) after look/feel/sound?** Because these verbs don't describe an
**action** — they **link** the subject to a quality. *"The plan looks realistic"* means *the plan
is realistic (in appearance)*. There's no "looking" being done well or badly. The adjective is
really describing the plan, so it must be an adjective. Save the adverb for verbs that describe a
real action.

**Decision rule vs the rival (adjective vs adverb):** ask *what does the word describe?*
- Describes a **thing / person** (the subject) → **adjective**, after be/look/feel/sound/seem/taste.
- Describes **how an action is done** → **adverb** (-ly), after an ordinary action verb.

| Adjective (describes the subject) | Adverb (describes the action) |
|---|---|
| *The proposal sounds **good**.* (the proposal = good) | *The team performed **well**.* (how they performed) |
| *She seems **confident**.* (she = confident) | *She presented **confidently**.* (how she presented) |
| *The results look **impressive**.* | *The pilot scaled **impressively**.* |

**PT→EN mapping.** Two classic interference points:
- **Order:** Portuguese usually puts the adjective **after** the noun — *"uma estratégia ousada"*,
  *"um plano ambicioso"*. English puts it **before**, and stacks several in a fixed order. Direct
  translation of *"uma nova plataforma americana"* → *"a new American platform"* happens to match,
  but *"uma plataforma boa e nova"* must flip to *"a **good new** platform."*
- **"soa bem / parece bom":** Portuguese uses the **adverb** *bem* (*"soa bem"*, *"vai bem"*), so
  Brazilians reach for *well* — *"it sounds well"*, *"the plan looks well."* ❌ In English these
  linking verbs demand the **adjective**: *"it sounds **good**"*, *"the plan looks **good**."*
  (*Well* only returns as an adjective meaning *healthy*: *"I don't feel well today."*)

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"The strategy sounds well."* → ✅ *"The strategy sounds **good**."* (*sound* links to the
   subject → adjective; *well* here would wrongly mean "in good health").
2. ❌ *"We built a long-term bold plan."* → ✅ *"We built a **bold long-term** plan."* (opinion/quality
   before the age/duration fact).
3. ❌ *"The board seemed nervously about the budget."* → ✅ *"The board seemed **nervous**."* (*seem*
   links to the board → adjective, not adverb).

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"It's a **bold, ambitious long-term** strategy."*
   *— opinion words (bold, ambitious) before the fact (long-term); the natural executive order.*
2. *"The Q3 plan **looks realistic** now that the numbers are in."*
   *— look links to "the plan" → adjective (realistic), never "looks realistically."*
3. *"The forecast **sounds optimistic**, but the data **feels solid**."*
   *— both verbs describe their subjects (forecast, data) → adjectives; the PT trap is "sounds well."*
4. *"We backed an **impressive young American** AI company."*
   *— opinion (impressive), age (young), origin (American), purpose (AI) — the exact stacking order.*
5. *"The CFO **seemed nervous** about the AI budget."*
   *— seem links to the CFO → adjective (nervous), not "nervously."*
6. *"That **clunky old** dashboard finally got replaced this quarter."*
   *— opinion (clunky) before age (old); everyday-office register, same rule.*
7. *"You **look tired** — long week?"*
   *— look + adjective describing the person; "look tiredly" would be nonsense.*
8. *"This coffee **tastes bitter**, but the pastry **smells amazing**."*
   *— everyday life: taste and smell are sense verbs → adjective (bitter, amazing), not "bitterly."*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 077):**
1. **excited** — *"The board was excited by the roadmap."* The one question: *is this the thing that
   causes the feeling (-ing) or the person who feels it (-ed)?* The board feels it → **-ed**.
2. Wrong: *"interesting."* Fix: *"I'm very **interested** in the AI budget."* *Interesting* describes
   a cause, so *"I'm interesting"* accidentally says *you* are the fascinating spectacle — not that
   you care about the topic.
3. *"The vendor demo was **confusing**, so half the room left **confused**."* *Confusing* points at
   the demo (the cause); *confused* points at the people (the feelers).

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 072:** *"The consultant **who** advises the board is very expensive."* The relative pronoun
   *who* is missing. It's the **subject** of *advises*, and a subject relative pronoun can never be
   dropped — without it, *advises* has no subject and the sentence collapses.
- **Unit 051:** **funding** — *"committed to **funding** the pilot."* Test: can you put *it* after
   *to*? *"committed to **it**"* works, so *to* is a **preposition**, and a verb after a preposition
   takes **-ing**.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **good** — *sound* is a linking verb describing the roadmap → adjective. *Well* would mean
   "in good health," which makes no sense for a roadmap.
2. *"an **impressive long-term** AI strategy."* *Impressive* (opinion) moves to the front, before the
   *long-term* (age/duration fact).
3. Wrong: *"nervously."* Fix: *"The market **seems nervous** about the rate decision."* *Seem* is a
   linking verb, so it needs an **adjective** describing the market, not an adverb.
4. **impressively** — here *scaled* is a real action verb, so you need an **adverb** to say *how* it
   scaled. Opposite of item 1, where the verb only linked a quality to the subject.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Duas regras num só tema. **Ordem:** em inglês o adjetivo vem **antes** do substantivo (ao contrário do
português) e, quando há vários, a **opinião vem primeiro**, depois os fatos (tamanho, idade, origem,
material): *"a bold long-term strategy"*, *"an impressive young American startup"*. **Verbos de
ligação:** depois de *be, seem, look, feel, sound, taste, smell*, use **adjetivo**, não advérbio,
porque a palavra descreve o **sujeito**, não a ação — *"the plan looks realistic"*, *"the numbers
sound optimistic"*.

A armadilha número um do brasileiro é o *bem*: dizemos *"soa bem"*, *"parece bom"*, e traduzimos como
*"sounds well"* / *"looks well"* — errado. Esses verbos pedem o adjetivo: *"sounds **good**"*,
*"looks **good**."* (*Well* só volta como adjetivo no sentido de saúde: *"I don't feel well."*) A
segunda armadilha é a ordem dos adjetivos — não diga *"a long-term bold plan"*; a opinião (*bold*) vem
antes do fato (*long-term*). E lembre: o advérbio (-ly) só entra quando o verbo é uma **ação de
verdade** — *"the pilot scaled impressively"* —, nunca depois de *look/feel/sound*.
