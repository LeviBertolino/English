### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Can we greenlight the acquisition this quarter?
> **Levi:** Honestly, it's **too early** to commit. We don't have **enough data** on their margins yet.
> **CFO:** The price looks **too high** to me as well.
> **Levi:** It does. And we're moving **too fast** — the team isn't **big enough** to integrate another product right now.
> **CEO:** So you're saying we're not ready.
> **Levi:** Not quite. The thesis is **strong enough** to keep exploring — but it's **too risky to** close before Q2.

Notice what each word signals. **too** always means *more than is good* — *too early*, *too high*, *too fast*, *too risky*: every one marks a **problem**, a limit crossed. **enough** means *as much as is needed* — *enough data*, *big enough*, *strong enough*. And watch **where enough sits**: **before** a noun (*enough **data***) but **after** an adjective (*big **enough***, *strong **enough***). That flip is the whole trick of this unit — plus one warning for a Portuguese speaker: **too is not *very*.** *Very expensive* is just a fact; *too expensive* means *so expensive we can't / won't do it*.

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

**too** = *more than necessary or wanted* (a negative limit). **enough** = *as much as necessary* (the needed amount).

| Pattern | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **too** + adjective / adverb | *too* goes **before** | *too **expensive** · too **slowly** · too **risky*** |
| **too much** + uncountable noun | before the noun | *too much **pressure** · too much **detail*** |
| **too many** + countable plural | before the noun | *too many **options** · too many **meetings*** |
| adjective / adverb + **enough** | *enough* goes **after** | *big **enough** · fast **enough** · mature **enough*** |
| **enough** + noun | *enough* goes **before** | ***enough** data · **enough** budget · **enough** people* |

**The two rules that settle everything:**
- **`enough` flips around its word.** **After** an adjective or adverb (*good enough*, *quickly enough*), but **before** a noun (*enough time*). Never *"enough good"* or *"time enough."*
- **`too` always sits in front** of the adjective/adverb — and it always means *excessively*, a problem, never just *very*.

**The `… to` tail (both take it).** Both patterns often continue with **to + verb** to say what the limit blocks or allows:
> *too + adjective + **to** …* → *"It's **too risky to** approve."* (the risk **blocks** approval)
> *adjective + enough + **to** …* → *"We're **ready enough to** pilot it."* (the readiness **allows** the pilot)

**`not … enough`** = *less than needed*: *"The team is **not big enough** to scale,"* *"We didn't move **fast enough**."*

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these two words exist?** Comparatives and superlatives (last week's units) rank things *against each other*. **enough** and **too** do something different: they measure a thing against a **standard** — *is there as much as we need, or more than we can handle?* **enough** says *we've hit the mark*; **too** says *we've overshot it, and that's a problem*. For a leader this is the language of **thresholds and go/no-go calls**: *the thesis is strong **enough** to explore, but the price is **too** high to close.*

**Decision rule — `too` vs `very` (THE one to internalise).** Both can translate Portuguese *muito*, but they are **not** the same:
- **very** = high degree, *neutral* — just states how much. *"The market is **very** competitive"* = a plain fact, possibly fine.
- **too** = *excessive*, a limit crossed, always a **problem**. *"The market is **too** competitive"* = so competitive that we can't win / should not enter.

Minimal pairs — feel the meaning move:
- *"This project is **very** ambitious."* (bold — could be good) ≠ *"This project is **too** ambitious."* (so ambitious it will fail / we must cut it back).
- *"The report is **very** long."* (just long) ≠ *"The report is **too** long."* (so long nobody will read it — fix it).
- *"She's **very** young for the role."* (observation) ≠ *"She's **too** young for the role."* (disqualifying — won't work).

Rule of thumb: if the sentence is a **compliment or a neutral fact**, you want **very**. If it's a **complaint, a blocker, or a reason to stop**, you want **too**.

**Decision rule — `too much` vs `too many` vs `too` + adjective.** Look at what follows:
- a **noun you can count** (plural) → **too many**: *too many meetings, too many options.*
- a **noun you can't count** → **too much**: *too much pressure, too much detail.*
- an **adjective or adverb** (no noun) → plain **too**: *too slow, too expensive.*

**`enough` placement — the flip that outs a non-native speaker.** English puts *enough* **after** the adjective but **before** the noun. Portuguese *suficiente / o suficiente* comes **after** both (*grande o suficiente*, *dados suficientes*), so the noun order feels backwards at first:
- adjective: *grande o suficiente* → *big **enough*** (enough **after**).
- noun: *dados suficientes* → ***enough** data* (enough **before**).

**PT→EN mapping (where translation betrays you):**
- *muito* (neutro / elogio) → **very**; *muito* (demais, problema) / *… demais* → **too**: *caro demais* → *too expensive*; *muito caro* (só caro) → *very expensive*.
- *cedo demais / tarde demais* → *too early / too late.*
- *o suficiente / suficientemente* → **enough** (after adj: *bom o suficiente* → *good enough*).
- *tempo / verba / gente suficiente* → **enough** time / budget / people (before the noun).
- *pressão demais* → *too much pressure*; *reuniões demais* → *too many meetings*.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"The team is **too** talented"* (meaning praise) / *"Our growth was **too** strong this year"* (meaning good). → ✅ *"**very** talented"* / *"**very** strong."* Using *too* for a compliment is the #1 slip — *too* is always a problem. Keep *too* for blockers: *"too expensive," "too slow," "too risky."*
2. ❌ *"The platform isn't **enough mature** to scale"* / *"We don't have **enough** experienced people — the team isn't **enough big**."* → ✅ *"mature **enough**"* / *"**big enough**."* *enough* goes **after** the adjective (but **before** a noun: *enough people* is correct).
3. ❌ *"We have **too much** risks"* / *"There are **too many** pressure on the roadmap."* → ✅ *"too **many** risks"* (countable) / *"too **much** pressure"* (uncountable). Match *much* / *many* to the noun, exactly as you already do with *much/many* alone.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **It's too early to commit to the acquisition this quarter.**
   *— `too + adjective + to`: the earliness **blocks** the commitment. "too early" = a problem, not just "very early."*
2. **We don't have enough data on their margins yet.**
   *— `enough` sits **before** the noun (`enough data`) — the needed amount is missing. Portuguese "dados suficientes" reverses the order; English does not.*
3. **The price is too high to close before Q2.**
   *— `too high` marks a limit crossed (a blocker), and `too … to` names what it blocks. Note: `too high`, not "very high" — this is a reason to stop.*
4. **The team isn't big enough to integrate another product.**
   *— `adjective + enough` (`big enough`), after the adjective; `not … enough` = less than needed. Contrast error ❌ "enough big."*
5. **The thesis is strong enough to keep exploring.**
   *— `strong enough to` = the readiness **allows** the next step (the positive mirror of "too risky to"). `enough` after the adjective again.*
6. **There are too many initiatives in the portfolio and too much pressure on the team.**
   *— countable plural → `too many initiatives`; uncountable → `too much pressure`. One sentence, both forms, so the contrast is unmistakable.*
7. **This coffee is too hot to drink.** *(everyday)*
   *— the everyday shape of the executive pattern: `too + adjective + to`. The heat blocks drinking it — a problem, so `too`, not `very`.*
8. **We got to the airport early enough to catch the earlier flight.** *(everyday)*
   *— `early enough to` = enough after the adverb, then `to` for the result it allows. Compare with #1's `too early to` — same frame, opposite meaning.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 111 — superlatives):**
1. **in** — *"the biggest cloud provider **in** Latin America."* Places and regions take **in**, never *of*. The Portuguese *da América Latina* is the trap that pulls you to *of*.
2. Drop *most*: *"the **worst** quarter we've ever had."* *worst* is already the superlative of *bad*, so *the most worst* double-marks it — use *-est*/irregular **or** *the most*, never both.
3. **Plural noun:** *"one of the biggest **risks** in our portfolio."* *one of the …* picks one item out of several top ones, so the noun must be plural (*risks*, not *risk*).

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 105 (so / such):** *"The timing was **so** tight that…"* (**so** + adjective **alone**) and *"It was **such** a bold bet that…"* (**such** + a noun phrase, with *a* kept before the adjective). What decides it: is there a **noun** after? If just a describing word → *so*; if a noun phrase → *such*.
- **Unit 084 (comparatives):** *"Building in-house is **cheaper than** licensing, but the vendor's model is **more scalable than** ours."* *cheap* is short → *-er + than*; *scalable* is long (3 syllables) → *more … than*. Both need *than* because two things are being compared.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **very** — *"Our Q3 results were **very** strong — the board was thrilled."* *very* states a high degree neutrally, which fits a compliment. *too strong* would mean *excessively* strong — a problem — which contradicts "the board was thrilled."
2. **scalable enough** — *"The platform isn't **scalable enough** to handle enterprise load."* *enough* goes **after** an adjective, never before it.
3. **too many risks** (countable plural) and **too much technical debt** (uncountable). Match *many* to count nouns, *much* to uncountable ones.
4. (a) *"The deal is **too** risky **to** approve today."* — *too + adjective + to* = the risk **blocks** approval. (b) *"The thesis is mature **enough to** pilot next quarter."* — *adjective + enough + to* = the maturity **allows** the pilot. Same *… to* tail, opposite direction.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra:** **too** quer dizer *demais* — passou do limite, é sempre um **problema** (*too expensive*, *too risky*, *too late*). **enough** quer dizer *o suficiente* — chegou na medida necessária. A posição é o pulo do gato: *enough* vem **depois** do adjetivo/advérbio (*big **enough***, *strong **enough***) mas **antes** do substantivo (***enough** data*, ***enough** budget*). Com substantivo contável use *too **many*** (*too many meetings*); com incontável, *too **much*** (*too much pressure*). E os dois costumam puxar *… **to** + verbo*: *too risky **to** approve* (bloqueia) × *mature enough **to** pilot* (libera).

**As armadilhas (interferência do português):** a número 1 é confundir **too** com **very**, porque *muito* traduz os dois. **very** = grau alto, neutro ou elogio (*very strong* = resultado ótimo); **too** = excesso, sempre um problema (*too strong* = forte **demais**, algo a corrigir). Regra prática: elogio ou fato neutro → *very*; reclamação ou motivo pra parar → *too*. ❌ *"the team is too talented"* (elogio) → ✅ *"very talented"*. A número 2: *enough* **depois** do adjetivo — ❌ *enough mature* → ✅ *mature enough* (mas *enough people* está certo, antes do substantivo). A número 3: *too much* × *too many* conforme o substantivo — ❌ *too much risks* → ✅ *too many risks*.
