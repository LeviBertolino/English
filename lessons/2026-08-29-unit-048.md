### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **You:** Before we vote — **let me walk** you through the two scenarios.
> **CEO:** Go ahead.
> **You:** The first model **makes us commit** the full budget in year one. The second
> **lets us stage** it and **lets the board pause** after the pilot.
> **CFO:** The second one **makes me nervous** about momentum, honestly.
> **CEO:** It shouldn't. It **lets us learn** cheaply. Don't **let one bad quarter
> make us abandon** the whole bet.

Notice the pattern before any rule. After **let** and **make**, the next verb arrives
**bare** — no *to*: *let me **walk***, *makes us **commit***, *lets us **stage***, *lets
the board **pause***, *lets us **learn***. Compare that with last week's *"I want you
**to** decide"* (Unit 044), which **kept** the *to*. *Make* and *let* are the rebels:
object + **base verb**, and the little *to* vanishes.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Both verbs take an **object + the base verb** (the infinitive **without** *to*):

| Verb | Meaning | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **make** | **force / cause** somebody to do it | *make + object + **base verb*** | *"The board **made us rethink** the plan."* |
| **let** | **allow / permit** somebody to do it | *let + object + **base verb*** | *"They **let us run** the pilot."* |

**Key facts:**
- **No *to*.** It is *"make us **commit**"*, *"let me **explain**"* — **never** *"make us
  to commit"*, *"let me to explain"*. The base verb comes straight after the object.
- **Object is obligatory.** You *make **somebody*** do, *let **somebody*** do — there is
  always a person/thing in the middle: *let **me** know*, *make **the team** move*.
- **let** has no future/perfect tricks here — it stays *let* (*"I'll let you know"*),
  and its everyday chunks are fixed: ***let me know***, ***let me explain***, ***let
  me check***, ***let's*** (= *let us*) ***decide***.
- **help** is the flexible cousin: *"help us **understand**"* **or** *"help us **to
  understand**"* — **both** correct. Only *make* and *let* fully ban the *to*.
- **One exception to file away:** in the **passive**, *make* gets its *to* **back** —
  *"We **were made to** sign off early."* (Active *made us sign*, passive *were made to
  sign*.) *Let* isn't used this way — English switches to *"were **allowed to**"*.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these two exist?** They compress **causation** and **permission** into one
verb. *Make* = "I had no choice, X forced it": *"The numbers **made us pause**."* *Let*
= "the door was open, X permitted it": *"The board **let us proceed**."* In a room where
you're constantly describing what forced a decision and what a stakeholder allowed,
*make* and *let* are the two verbs you reach for — and both drop the *to*.

**Decision rule #1 — make/let (no *to*) vs the rival, want/tell/ask/allow (+ *to*).**
This is *the* seam, because the meanings are neighbours but the form flips:

| | **make / let** | **want / tell / ask / allow / expect / permit** |
|---|---|---|
| Form | object + **base verb** (no *to*) | object + **to** + verb |
| Example | *"They **let us run** the pilot."* | *"They **allowed us to run** the pilot."* |
| Example | *"The CEO **made me rethink** it."* | *"The CEO **told me to rethink** it."* |

Minimal pairs — same idea, the verb decides the form:
- *"They **let us decide**."* (let → no *to*) vs *"They **allowed us to decide**."*
  (allow → *to*). ✅ both; *let* and *allow* mean almost the same, but only *allow* keeps
  the *to*.
- *"The board **made me revise** the deck."* (make → no *to*) vs *"The board **asked me
  to revise** the deck."* (ask → *to*). Note the meaning too: *made* = forced;
  *asked* = requested.

**Decision rule #2 — make = force vs let = allow.** Don't mix the meanings:
- *"The risk **made us slow down**."* = something forced it (no choice).
- *"The risk **let us slow down**."* ❌ nonsense — a risk doesn't *permit*. Use *let*
  only when someone **grants permission**: *"The CEO **let us slow down**."*

**PT→EN mapping — where Portuguese betrays Levi.**
- *"**Deixa** eu explicar" / "**Deixe-me** explicar"* → ✅ *"**Let me explain**"*, ❌
  *"Let me **to** explain"* (Portuguese *deixar* = *let*, and no *to* follows).
- *"O CEO me **fez repensar** o roadmap"* → ✅ *"The CEO **made me rethink** the
  roadmap"*, ❌ *"made me **to** rethink"* (*fazer alguém fazer* = *make somebody do*,
  bare).
- *"Isso **me deixa** nervoso"* → ✅ *"That **makes me nervous**"* — careful: for a
  **feeling**, Portuguese *deixar* ("me deixa nervoso") maps to English **make**, not
  *let*: *make me nervous / make me proud / make it clear*.
- *"**Me avise** / **me deixe saber**"* → ✅ *"**Let me know**"* (fixed chunk), ❌ *"Let
  me to know"*, ❌ *"Advise me"* in casual use.
- *"A diretoria **nos permitiu** lançar"* → ✅ *"The board **let us launch**"* **or**
  *"**allowed us to** launch"* — *permitir* can go either way, but pick the matching
  form (*let* → no *to*; *allow* → *to*).

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"The CEO **made me to rethink** the roadmap."* → ✅ *"The CEO **made me rethink**
  the roadmap."* — after *make*, the verb is **bare**; the *to* never appears (active).
- ❌ *"**Let me to explain** the two scenarios."* → ✅ *"**Let me explain** the two
  scenarios."* — *let* + object + **base verb**; *"let me to…"* is the #1 slip.
- ❌ *"That **lets me** nervous."* → ✅ *"That **makes me** nervous."* — a **feeling**
  you don't control is *make* (force), not *let* (permit); Portuguese *"me deixa
  nervoso"* misleads here.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"**Let me walk** you through the two scenarios before we vote."*
   *— let + object + bare verb; the fixed opener "let me walk / let me explain", never "let me to walk".*
2. *"The first model **makes us commit** the full budget in year one."*
   *— make = force; bare "commit" after the object "us". Compare "requires us to commit" (require keeps the to).*
3. *"The staged option **lets the board pause** after the pilot."*
   *— let = grant permission; "lets the board pause", no to. Third-person "lets" still drops it.*
4. *"Don't **let one bad quarter make us abandon** the whole bet."*
   *— two of them stacked: let (permit) + make (force), each followed by a bare verb — "make us abandon", not "to abandon".*
5. *"The revised forecast **made me nervous** about momentum."*
   *— feeling caused by something → make, not let; "made me nervous" (Portuguese "me deixou nervoso" tempts "let").*
6. *"The board **let us run** the pilot, but they **asked us to report** monthly."*
   *— the whole seam in one line: let → bare "run"; ask → "to report". Same sentence, two forms.*
7. *"**Let me know** as soon as finance sends the numbers."*
   *— fixed executive chunk; "let me know", never "let me to know" or "advise me".*
8. *"My daughter won't **let me leave** the house without a hug."*
   *— everyday life; let + object + bare "leave"; permission (she allows / withholds), no to.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **"cause vs permission" drill**: they fire a scenario from your
  world — *"the board approved the pilot", "the CFO forced a budget cut", "one bad
  quarter", "the CEO opened the door to staging"* — and you must reframe each out loud
  with **make** (force) or **let** (allow) + a **bare verb**: *"The board **let us run**
  it", "The CFO **made us cut** the budget"*. Goal: pick the right verb for the meaning
  and drop the *to* automatically.
- Then a **"make/let vs ask/allow" bait round**: have your tutor mix the two families —
  *make, let* against *tell, ask, want, allow, expect* — and you supply the form live
  (*"made me rethink"* vs *"told me to rethink"*, *"let us decide"* vs *"allowed us to
  decide"*). This is the exact seam where the *to* wrongly appears, so drill it until
  *"let me to explain"* never surfaces in a real room.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 047 — adjective + to-infinitive):**
1. ***hard to predict*** — *"It's **hard to predict** the market three years out."* An
   adjective of judgement takes **to + base verb**; *"hard predict"* drops the obligatory
   *to* (Portuguese *"difícil prever"* shows none, so it's easy to forget).
2. ***It's*** and ***to align*** — *"**It's** important **to align** on the vision…"*
   English needs the empty subject *it*, and the verb after the adjective is **to +
   base**. *"Is important align"* copies *"É importante alinhar"*, which hides both.
3. ***to raise*** — *"I was **afraid to raise** the risk, so I stayed quiet"* = I was
   scared and therefore **didn't do it**. The other form, *"afraid **of raising**"*,
   would mean I feared the act *might happen* — which doesn't fit "so I stayed quiet".

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 041)** ***"Neither do I."*** (or *"Me neither."*) — you're agreeing with a
  **negative** (*"I don't have…"*), so the sign is **Neither** + the borrowed auxiliary
  *do*. *"Me too"* only agrees with **positives**, so it's the wrong sign here.
- **(Unit 016)** ***yet*** — *"Have you sent the memo **yet**?"* A **neutral** "is it done
  by now?" question uses **yet** at the end. *"…already?"* would add **surprise** ("so
  soon?!"); Portuguese *já?* feels neutral and pushes Brazilians toward *already*.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ❌ *"…made me **to** reconsider…"* → ✅ *"The CEO **made me reconsider** the
   timeline."* — after *make* the verb is **bare**; the *to* never appears in the active.
2. ***"They let us run the pilot."*** (let → **bare** *run*) **and** ***"They allowed us
   to run the pilot."*** (allow → **to** *run*). Same meaning, but *let* bans the *to* and
   *allow* requires it — match the form to the verb.
3. ***made*** — *"The disappointing numbers **made me question** the whole roadmap."*
   *Make* = force/cause (the numbers left no choice). *Let* means *permit*, and numbers
   don't grant permission, so it can't fit.
4. ***makes*** — *"Presenting to the board always **makes me nervous**."* A **feeling**
   caused by something is *make*, not *let*. Portuguese *"me deixa nervoso"* (*deixar*)
   misleads you toward *let*, but here *deixar* maps to English **make**.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** *Make* e *let* são os dois verbos "rebeldes": vêm sempre com
**objeto + verbo na base**, **sem *to***. *Make* = **forçar/causar** (*"The board **made
us rethink** the plan"* — não houve escolha); *let* = **permitir/deixar** (*"They **let
us run** the pilot"* — a porta estava aberta). Servem para descrever, numa reunião, o que
**obrigou** uma decisão e o que um stakeholder **permitiu** — e os dois **derrubam o
to**: *"let me **explain**", "makes us **commit**"*, nunca *"let me **to** explain"*.
Lembre dos blocos fixos: ***let me know**, **let me explain**, **let's decide***. O primo
flexível é *help*, que aceita as duas formas (*"help us (to) understand"*); só *make* e
*let* proíbem o *to* de vez (na ativa — na passiva o *to* volta: *"we were **made to**
sign"*).

**As armadilhas.** (1) **Nunca ponha *to* depois de *make/let***: *"made me **rethink**"*,
*"let me **explain**"* — o *"made me to…" / "let me to…"* é o erro nº 1, porque em
português *fazer alguém fazer* e *deixar alguém fazer* não têm marca. (2) **make × verbos
com *to***: *let/make* = base nua; mas *want, tell, ask, allow, expect, permit* pedem
**to** (*"allowed us **to** run"*, *"asked me **to** report"*) — mesma ideia, forma
diferente. (3) **Sentimento é *make*, não *let***: *"me deixa nervoso"* vira *"**makes**
me nervous"* (o *deixar* aqui é *make*, não *let*). Se o par to × base nua ainda embolar,
releia `lessons/2026-08-25-unit-044.md` (verb + object + **to**) — é o rival direto desta
unidade.
