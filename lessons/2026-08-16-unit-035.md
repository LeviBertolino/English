### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CFO:** Before we announce the AI investment, what's the process?
> **You:** We **have to** get sign-off from the audit committee first — that's a
> hard requirement. And we **'ve got to** update the risk register the same week.
> **CFO:** Do we **have to** wait for the full board?
> **You:** No, we **don't have to** wait for the full board — the committee is
> enough. But we **mustn't** brief the market before that sign-off lands.
> **CFO:** Understood. And last quarter?
> **You:** Last quarter we **had to** cut two pilots to protect the flagship bets.

Notice the pattern before any rule. Every *have to* here is an **external
obligation** — a rule, a process, a hard need coming from **outside your
opinion**. It's not advice (*should*) and not your own insistence — it's *"this is
required."* Portuguese says it with *"ter que / ter de"* — *"temos que conseguir a
aprovação"* — and that clean, familiar mapping is both the gift and the trap.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Unlike *should* and *must*, **have to** is **not** a pure modal — it behaves like
an ordinary verb. So it changes for *he/she/it*, and it needs **do / does / did**
for questions and negatives.

| | have to + base verb | |
|---|---|---|
| Positive | **have to** / **has to** | *We **have to** file the report. / She **has to** sign it.* |
| Negative | **don't** / **doesn't have to** | *We **don't have to** decide today.* |
| Question | **Do** / **Does** … **have to**…? | ***Do** we **have to** wait for the board?* |
| Past (all) | **had to** | *Last quarter we **had to** cut two pilots.* |
| Future | **will have to** / **'m going to have to** | *We**'ll have to** re-forecast in Q3.* |

**Key facts:**
- **has to** for *he / she / it*: *"The CFO **has to** approve it"* — the *-s* lives
  on *have*, and the main verb stays **base**: *has to **approve***, never
  *has to approves*.
- Questions/negatives use **do/does/did** — you cannot say *"Have we to…?"* in
  modern English: *"**Do** we **have to**…?"*, *"We **don't have to**…"*
- **had to** is the **only** past of obligation. *must* has **no past at all** —
  when the obligation was in the past, you must switch to *had to*.
- **have got to** (**'ve got to / 's got to**) means the same as *have to*, but is
  **informal and spoken**, and feels more **immediate/one-off**: *"We**'ve got
  to** move on this today."* In writing and about habits/rules, prefer plain
  *have to*.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does *have to* exist?** English needs a verb for **obligation and necessity
that comes from outside you** — a rule, a law, a process, a fact of the situation.
*have to* says: *"the circumstances require this; it isn't my choice."* It's also
the **workhorse** of obligation, because it works everywhere *must* can't —
questions, past, future, and after other verbs (*"We're going to have to…"*). For a
senior leader that's most of real speech: you describe what the **process, the
regulator, or the board requires**, far more often than what you personally
command.

**Decision rule #1 — *have to* vs *must* (outside obligation vs inside insistence).**
Both mean obligation, but the **source** differs — and in day-to-day business
English, *have to* is by far the more common.

| | *have to* | *must* |
|---|---|---|
| Source of the obligation | **external** — rule, law, process, situation | **internal** — the speaker's own authority / strong conviction |
| Typical voice | *"the regulator requires it"* | *"I'm telling you / I really feel we should"* |
| Questions, past, future | yes — *do/does/did have to*, *had to*, *will have to* | **no** — *must* has no past and no natural question form |
| Example | *We **have to** file the compliance report (the law says so).* | *We **must** win this deal (I'm insisting).* |

Minimal pair — same words, different origin of the pressure:
- *"We **have to** cut costs."* → something external forces it — the numbers, the
  board mandate. ✅
- *"We **must** cut costs."* → **I** am insisting on it; it's my strong personal
  push. ✅

Decision rule: **is the pressure coming from outside me (a rule/fact) or from my
own insistence?** Outside → *have to*. My own authority → *must*. And whenever you
need a **question, a past, or a future**, you have no choice: use *have to*.

**Decision rule #2 — *don't have to* vs *mustn't* (no need vs forbidden). This is
the one that trips up almost every Brazilian speaker, and the meanings are
opposite:**
- *"You **don't have to** wait for the full board."* → it is **not necessary**;
  you *may*, but you don't need to. ✅
- *"You **mustn't** brief the market yet."* → it is **forbidden**; do not do it. ✅

*don't have to* = **no obligation** (optional). *mustn't* = **prohibition** (a wall).
They are **not** interchangeable — telling the board *"you don't have to share
these numbers"* when you mean *"you're forbidden to"* is a dangerous mistranslation.

**Decision rule #3 — *have to* vs *should* (necessity vs advice).** This builds
straight on yesterday: *should* is your **recommendation** (room to disagree);
*have to* is a **requirement** (no choice). *"We **should** brief the CFO"* =
good idea; *"We **have to** brief the CFO"* = the process demands it.

**PT→EN mapping — where *ter que* helps and where it betrays.** The core mapping is
clean: *"ter que / ter de"* → **have to**. *"Temos que apresentar o roadmap"* →
*"We **have to** present the roadmap."* Two places it betrays Levi: (1) *"não
preciso / não é necessário"* maps to **don't have to**, **not** *mustn't* — *"Não
preciso esperar o board"* → *"I **don't have to** wait for the board,"* never *"I
mustn't wait."* (2) Portuguese happily uses *ter que* in the past and in questions
with no helper; English needs **had to** for the past and **do/does** for
questions: *"Você teve que cortar?"* → *"**Did** you **have to** cut?"*, not *"Had
you to cut?"*

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"You **don't have to** share these figures"* (meaning it's forbidden) → ✅
  *"You **mustn't** share these figures."* — *don't have to* only removes the
  **necessity**; it never means *forbidden*. This is the single highest-stakes
  mix-up.
- ❌ *"Yesterday we **must** present to the board."* → ✅ *"Yesterday we **had to**
  present to the board."* — *must* has **no past**; past obligation is always
  *had to*.
- ❌ *"**Does** the CFO **has to** sign it?"* / *"Have we to wait?"* → ✅ *"**Does**
  the CFO **have to** sign it?"* — after *do/does*, *have to* drops the *-s* (the
  *-s* is already on *does*), and modern English never says *"Have we to…?"*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"We **have to** get sign-off from the audit committee before we announce."*
   *— External process requirement, not your opinion — so have to, not should.*
2. *"We **don't have to** decide on the vendor today; we still have two weeks."*
   *— Removes the necessity ("no need"), leaving a choice — not a prohibition, so not "mustn't".*
3. *"**Does** the board **have to** approve every pilot, or just the flagship bets?"*
   *— A genuine question about the rule; must can't ask this, so have to + does is the only option.*
4. *"Last quarter we **had to** cut two pilots to protect the flagship bets."*
   *— Past obligation; must has no past, so it becomes had to.*
5. *"We**'re going to have to** re-forecast before the offsite."*
   *— Future obligation stacked on going-to; must can't go here, have to can.*
6. *"You **mustn't** brief the market before the sign-off — but you **don't have
   to** memorize the numbers."*
   *— Same breath, opposite meanings: mustn't = forbidden, don't have to = not necessary.*
7. *"The CFO **has to** approve the budget before it reaches the board."*
   *— Third-person: the -s sits on "has", and the main verb stays base ("approve").*
8. *"I **have to** pick up my daughter at six, so let's wrap the sync by five-thirty."*
   *— Everyday life: an external constraint (the school time) forcing a choice — classic have to.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **"don't have to vs mustn't" firing drill**: they throw
  situations at you — *"the numbers are confidential," "attendance at the offsite
  is optional," "the NDA forbids sharing the deck," "you may skip the pre-read"* —
  and you answer fast with *"You **mustn't**…"* (forbidden) or *"You **don't have
  to**…"* (not necessary). This is the highest-value contrast for you, and it has
  to become automatic before it costs you in a real board room.
- Then a **"source of the obligation" role-play**: your tutor plays the CFO
  probing your AI process, and you answer using *have to* for anything external
  (*"We **have to** get audit sign-off"*) and *must* only when you're personally
  insisting (*"We **must** win this account"*) — plus deliberately put one
  obligation in the **past** (*"Last quarter we **had to**…"*) so the *must → had
  to* switch stops feeling strange.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 034 — should):**
1. ***approve*** — *"The CFO should **approve** the budget."* *should* is a modal
   followed by the **base verb**: no *-s* (*approves*) and no phantom *to*
   (*to approve*) — that *to* is the *deveria + infinitivo* reflex.
2. ***should*** — *"We **should** hire a Head of AI."* It's your **recommendation**,
   with room to disagree; *have to* would wrongly imply it's an external
   requirement with no choice.
3. ***mustn't*** — *"You **mustn't** share these figures externally."* Sharing is
   **forbidden**; *shouldn't* would downgrade a prohibition to *"unwise,"*
   understating the risk to the board.

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 028)** ***I'll*** — *"No problem — **I'll** do it."* You decide **in the
  moment you speak**, and an on-the-spot decision takes *will*, not the
  pre-planned *going to*.
- **(Unit 012)** ❌ *"Did the board **approved** the AI budget yesterday?"* → ✅
  *"**Did** the board **approve** the AI budget yesterday?"* — once *did* carries
  the past, the main verb snaps back to its **base form**; the sentence never
  marks the past twice.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ***mustn't*** — *"You **mustn't** brief the market yet."* It's **forbidden**;
   *don't have to* would say it's merely *not necessary* (optional) — the opposite
   meaning, and a dangerous one to get wrong.
2. ***have to*** — *"We **have to** file the compliance report by Friday."* A
   **regulator's requirement** is an obligation, not advice; *should* would imply
   it's only *recommended*, and therefore skippable.
3. **Drop the *-s*** — *"**Does** the CFO **have to** sign the budget first?"* The
   past/third-person marker is already on *does*, so *have to* stays **base**;
   *does … has to* doubles the *-s*.
4. ***must* is impossible** — *"Last quarter we **had to** cut two pilots."*
   *must* has **no past form**; past obligation is always *had to*.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** *have to* é o verbo da **obrigação que vem de fora** —
uma regra, uma lei, um processo, a situação: *"esse é o requisito, não é escolha
minha."* Equivale ao *"ter que / ter de"*: *"Temos que conseguir a aprovação do
comitê"* → *"We **have to** get the committee's sign-off."* Diferente de *should*
(conselho, dá para discordar) e de *must* (insistência **sua**, interna), o *have
to* aponta para a pressão **externa** — e é o cavalo de batalha da obrigação,
porque funciona onde *must* não vai: em **perguntas** (*Do we have to…?*), no
**passado** (*had to*) e no **futuro** (*will have to*). Forma: *have/has to +
verbo base*, com *do/does/did* nas perguntas e negativas.

**As armadilhas.** (1) A mais cara: *don't have to* ≠ *mustn't*. *don't have to* =
*"não é necessário"* (opcional); *mustn't* = *"proibido"* (parede). Nunca troque
os dois — dizer *"you don't have to share"* querendo dizer *"é proibido"* inverte o
sentido. (2) *must* **não tem passado**: *"ontem tivemos que cortar"* é *"yesterday
we **had to** cut"*, nunca *"we must cut"*. (3) Nas perguntas, o *-s* fica no
*does*, não no verbo: *"**Does** the CFO **have to** sign?"*, não *"Does… has
to"*, e jamais *"Have we to…?"*. Se obrigação vs recomendação ainda embolar,
releia `lessons/2026-08-15-unit-034.md` (should) e
`lessons/2026-08-13-unit-032.md` (must, mustn't, don't need to).
