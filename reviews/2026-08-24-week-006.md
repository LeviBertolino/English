# 🔁 Review Week 6 — Units 37 to 42

This week was a **register-and-machinery** week. After a month on modals, the
lessons shifted to the small, high-frequency structures that make English sound
native in a room: the polite *would like*, the bare-verb imperative, the empty
*it*, question tags, agreement echoes (*So am I / Neither do I*), and the fixed
*verb + -ing* pattern. Two of them — tags and *So/Neither* — run on the **same
auxiliary machine**, so this review leans hard on telling them apart.

No new grammar here. This is consolidation **through contrast**: for each pair
that a Brazilian speaker confuses, when does each one win? Retrieve first — the
answers, each with a one-line reason, are in section 6.

---

## 2. 📋 Recap — one line per unit

- **Unit 037 — would like.** *I'd like to… / Would you like…?* = the **polite**
  version of *want* — a softened wish, offer, or invitation for the C-level room.
- **Unit 038 — the imperative.** *Pick two. / Don't overload them. / Let's
  focus.* — bare verb, no subject, no *to*; the most **direct** voice in English.
- **Unit 039 — empty *it*.** English **forces a subject**, so a dummy *it* fills
  the seat: *It's critical to move, It took us two quarters, It's almost four.*
- **Unit 040 — question tags.** A statement + a mini-question that **flips the
  polarity** and **echoes the auxiliary**: *…, aren't we? / …, has it?*
- **Unit 041 — So am I / Neither do I.** Two-word **agreement echoes**: same
  auxiliary machine as tags, but you **invert** (aux before subject) to agree.
- **Unit 042 — verb + -ing.** *suggest, stop, enjoy, consider, avoid, keep,
  risk, recommend…* force the **-ing** form on the next verb — a fixed pattern.

## 3. 🗺️ Confusion map — when each one wins

The week's real work is in six contrasts. Learn the **decision rule**, not the
label.

### A. A specific wish now vs a general taste — **would like** vs **like**

| | **would like** | **like** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | want / offer — **this time** | enjoy — **in general** |
| *"___ to present?"* | **Would you like** = an **invitation** | **Do you like** = do you **enjoy** it? |
| Register | polite, softened (*gostaria*) | plain preference (*gosta*) |

Decision rule: **naming a wish/offer for now → *would like*; an always-true
preference → *like*.** *"Would you like to join the offsite?"* invites; *"Do you
like to join offsites?"* only asks about his taste. And offering to do it **for**
someone is *would like + me to*, never *"that I"*.

### B. Blunt vs diplomatic — **want** vs **would like**

Same meaning, different **social register**. Both correct. *"I want the
forecast"* can land as an order; *"I'd like the forecast"* is the safe default
with a board chair or in writing. Choose by the room, not by grammar.

### C. Ordering vs proposing together — **imperative** vs **Let's** vs **should**

| Form | Who acts | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| ***Pick* the two pilots.** | the other person / team | direct instruction |
| ***Let's* pick the two.** | **us, together** (includes you) | collegial proposal |
| **We *should* pick the two.** | us, but as **advice** | softer recommendation |

Decision rule: rallying the room to act **with** you → *Let's + base*; telling
someone else to act → bare imperative; giving advice → *should*. Negative of the
joint form is *Let's not…*, not *"Don't let's."*

### D. Existence vs the empty seat — **there is/are** vs empty **it**

| | **there is/are** | **empty *it*** |
|---|---|---|
| Job | says something **exists** | fills the subject slot (weather/time/distance/*to*-clause) |
| Example | ***There's** a real risk in rushing.* | ***It's** risky **to** rush.* |
| PT trap | *"tem um risco"* ≠ *has* → **there is** | *"é arriscado"* still needs **it** → *It's* |

Both are dummy subjects, but they do different jobs. *There's* introduces a new
thing onto the stage; *it's* carries an adjective + *to*-clause or the
clock/weather. *"Is important to align"* (no subject) is the classic slip — it
must be *"**It's** important to align."*

### E. Checking vs agreeing — **question tag** vs **So/Neither + aux**

Both run the **same auxiliary machine** (echo the aux; borrow *do/does/did* if
there's none). The difference is the job and the word order:

| | **Question tag** | **So am I / Neither do I** |
|---|---|---|
| Job | **check / invite agreement** on your own statement | **agree** with what someone else said |
| Polarity | **flips** (positive → negative tag) | **matches** (positive → *So*; negative → *Neither*) |
| Word order | statement, then *aux + pronoun* | **inverted**: *aux + subject* |
| Example | *We're aligned, **aren't we?*** | *"I'm worried." → "**So am I.**"* |

Decision rule: adding a little question to **your own** sentence to check → tag
(and flip the sign). Reacting to **someone else's** sentence to say *me too* →
*So/Neither* (and keep the sign, invert the order). After *Neither* the auxiliary
stays **positive**: *"Neither **have** I,"* never *"Neither haven't I."*

### F. -ing verbs vs to-infinitive verbs — **suggest/stop/enjoy** vs **decide/want/would like**

The verb **in front** decides the pattern — it's fixed, not a meaning choice.

| Take **-ing** | Take **to** + base |
|---|---|
| enjoy, finish, stop, suggest, recommend, consider, avoid, keep, risk, admit, imagine, postpone, miss | decide, want, would like, hope, plan, agree, promise, offer, need |

Minimal pair: *"We **considered acquiring** the startup, but **decided to
build** in-house."* — *consider* → -ing, *decide* → *to*. The one meaning-based
trap: **stop + -ing** = quit the activity; **stop + to** = pause **in order to**
do something. *"Stop debating"* (end it) ≠ *"stop to debate"* (pause so you can
debate).

## 4. 🧪 10 recognition exercises (~8 minutes)

Mostly contrast items — choose the right structure between rivals. Decide first;
answers with reasons in section 6.

1. **(would like vs like)** You want to **invite** the CFO to the strategy
   offsite. *"___ join us at the offsite next month?"* — **Do you like to** or
   **Would you like to**? What does the wrong one actually ask her?

2. **(would like — the offer form)** You want to offer to present the roadmap
   yourself. *"___ present the AI roadmap to the board?"* — **Would you like
   that I** or **Would you like me to**?

3. **(imperative vs Let's)** You want to rally the room to act **with** you.
   *"___ position the company as an AI-first player."* — **Let's** or **You
   should**? Which one includes you in the action?

4. **(there is vs empty it)** Fill both: *"___ a real risk in rushing, but ___
   worth piloting first."* — one slot needs **There's**, one needs **it's**.
   Which goes where, and why?

5. **(empty it — missing subject)** Spot and fix the error: *"Is critical to
   align the board before we commit the budget."*

6. **(question tag)** Add the tag: *"The board hasn't approved the AI budget
   yet, ___?"* — **has it** or **hasn't it**? Watch the polarity **and** the
   pronoun.

7. **(tag vs So/Neither)** Your CEO says *"I'm worried about the timeline."* You
   agree. Do you say *"So am I"* or *"I'm worried too, aren't I?"* — and what is
   the difference in **job** between the two structures?

8. **(So/Neither — the sign)** Reply to *"The board hasn't signed off yet."* to
   say finance hasn't either: *"___ finance."* — **Neither has** or **Neither
   hasn't**? Why does the auxiliary stay positive?

9. **(-ing vs to)** Choose: *"We ___ the startup, but we ___ in-house."* —
   options: **considered acquiring / decided to build** vs **considered to
   acquire / decided building**. Which verb forces which?

10. **(stop + -ing vs stop + to — the meaning trap)** You want the team to **end
    the vendor debate and decide**. *"Let's ___ the vendor and make the call."* —
    **stop debating** or **stop to debate**? What would the wrong one mean?

## 5. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

Three themes from this week are worth drilling with your tutor — pick the two
that feel shakiest:

- **The polite-request register (Units 37 + 38).** Ask your tutor to role-play a
  board chair. Practise opening asks three ways and **feeling the register
  shift**: blunt (*"I want the forecast"*), diplomatic (*"I'd like the
  forecast"*), and offering to do it for them (*"Would you like me to draft
  it?"*). Kill the *"Would you like that I…?"* reflex until *me to* is automatic.

- **The auxiliary machine — tags vs echoes (Units 40 + 41).** These share
  machinery and are the easiest to blur. Have your tutor make statements and you
  react **live**: first checking with a tag (*"…, isn't it?"*), then agreeing
  with an echo (*"So do I / Neither have I"*). The drill is switching between
  *flip the sign* (tags) and *keep the sign, invert the order* (So/Neither) on
  demand.

- **The -ing reflex (Unit 42).** Ask the tutor to fire verbs at you — *suggest,
  stop, decide, consider, want, avoid, recommend* — and you complete each with a
  second verb in the **correct** form, out loud, fast. The goal is to override
  the Portuguese *"sugiro rodar"* → *"suggest to run"* interference.

## 6. ✅ Answers

1. **Would you like to** — *"**Would you like to** join us at the offsite?"* is
   an **invitation** to this event. *"Do you like to join…?"* only asks whether
   she **enjoys** offsites in general — not an invitation at all (Unit 37, rule A).

2. **Would you like me to** — English offers to do something **for** someone with
   *would like + me to*. *"Would you like that I…"* is a word-for-word copy of
   *"gostaria que eu…"* and isn't English (Unit 37).

3. **Let's** — *"**Let's** position the company…"* *Let's = let us*, so it
   **includes you** in the action and rallies the room together. *"You should"*
   is advice aimed outward and leaves you out of the doing (Unit 38, rule C).

4. **There's** a real risk / **it's** worth piloting. *There's* introduces the
   risk as something that **exists**; *it's worth* carries the judgement about the
   **action**. Swap them and the sentence breaks (Unit 39, rule D).

5. **Add *It's*** → *"**It's** critical to align the board…"* English sentences
   must have a subject; the empty *it* fills the seat. *"Is critical to…"* copies
   the subjectless Portuguese *"É crítico…"* (Unit 39).

6. **has it** — the statement is **negative** (*hasn't*), so the tag flips to
   **positive** (*has*), and *the board/budget* becomes the pronoun **it**:
   *"…, **has it?**"* *hasn't it* would double the negative (Unit 40, rule E).

7. **"So am I"** — you're **agreeing** with someone else's statement, which is the
   *So/Neither* job (match the sign, invert: *aux + subject*). A question **tag**
   does a different job — it hooks onto **your own** statement to **check**
   agreement — so it doesn't fit here at all (Units 40 + 41, rule E).

8. **Neither has** — *"**Neither has** finance."* After *Neither*, the auxiliary
   stays **positive** because *neither* already carries the "no"; echo *has*,
   invert before the subject. *"Neither hasn't"* is a double negative (Unit 41).

9. **considered acquiring / decided to build** — *consider* forces **-ing**;
   *decide* forces **to** + base. The verb in front picks the pattern, not the
   meaning (Unit 42, rule F).

10. **stop debating** — *stop + -ing* = **end** the activity (quit the debate),
    which is what you want. *"Stop to debate"* means **pause in order to** debate
    — the opposite of ending it (Unit 42, the meaning trap).

## 7. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**O que deve estar sólido.** Esta semana foi menos sobre modais e mais sobre a
*mecânica fina* do inglês executivo. Três coisas deveriam já sair no automático:
(1) o registro educado — *"I'd like to propose"* / *"Would you like me to
draft?"* no lugar do direto *"I want"*, e nunca *"Would you like that I"*; (2) o
imperativo de verbo nu para instruções (*Pick, Don't overload*) e *Let's + verbo*
para chamar a sala a agir **junto**; (3) o *it* vazio obrigatório — inglês
**exige sujeito**, então *"É crítico alinhar"* vira *"**It's** critical to
align"*, jamais *"Is critical…"*.

**O que provavelmente ainda precisa de treino.** Os dois pontos mais escorregadios
são os que dividem a mesma máquina de auxiliares. **Question tags** (Unit 40)
**invertem o sinal** e ecoam o auxiliar (*"We're aligned, aren't we?"*), enquanto
**So am I / Neither do I** (Unit 41) **mantêm o sinal** mas invertem a ordem
(*auxiliar antes do sujeito*), e depois de *Neither* o auxiliar fica **positivo**
(*"Neither have I"*, nunca *"Neither haven't I"*). O outro tropeço clássico é o
**verbo + -ing** (Unit 42): *suggest, stop, avoid, consider* pedem *-ing*, contra
a interferência do português (*"sugiro rodar"* → **errado** *"suggest to run"*,
**certo** *"suggest running"*) — e cuidado com *stop + -ing* (encerrar) vs *stop +
to* (pausar para). Se algo ainda embolar, releia
`lessons/2026-08-21-unit-040.md` (tags) e `lessons/2026-08-22-unit-041.md`
(So/Neither) lado a lado — são a mesma máquina apontada para jobs diferentes.
