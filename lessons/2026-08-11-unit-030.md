### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Where do you land on the acquisition — are we doing it?
> **You:** Honestly, I don't know yet. We **might** move on it, we **might** wait
> for Q4 numbers. It depends on the diligence.
> **CEO:** And the AI platform vendor?
> **You:** They **might not** renew on the current terms — there's a real chance
> they push a price rise. I **might** open a parallel negotiation just in case.
> **CEO:** Fine. Keep the options live.

Notice what *might* is doing before any rule: every sentence is a **maybe**. You're
not promising, not predicting confidently — you're naming a **possibility**. "We
might move, we might wait" = both are open. That uncertainty *is* the meaning.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

*might* is a modal, like *will*. Same form for every subject, always followed by
the **base verb**. No *-s*, no *to*, no *do/does*.

| | Positive | Negative | (Question — rare) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Form | **might** + base | **might not** + base | **Might** + subject + base? |
| Example | *I **might go**.* | *I **might not go**.* | *(**Might** it **work**?)* |

- One form for all subjects: *I / you / he / she / we / they **might** go.* Never
  *he mights*, never *might to go*.
- Negative is **might not** (rarely contracted — *mightn't* exists but sounds old;
  prefer the full *might not*).
- **may** is an almost exact twin of *might* for possibility: *"We may move on it"*
  ≈ *"We might move on it."* *may* feels a touch more formal; in most business talk
  they're interchangeable. (In this unit, focus on *might*.)
- Questions with *might* are uncommon. To ask about possibility, English usually
  reaches for *Do you think… will…?* — *"Do you think it will work?"* rather than
  *"Might it work?"*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** *might* exists to mark a future or present as
**possible but not certain** — a maybe. It's the modal of the **open option**:
this could happen, it could not, and you're not committing to which. Nothing else
carries that "50/50, don't hold me to it" weight so cleanly.

**Decision rule — *will* vs *might* (the rival that matters most).** Both look at
the future. The split is **certainty**:

| | *will* | *might* |
|---|---|---|
| Confidence | sure / predicting confidently | possible, not sure |
| Feels like | "this is happening" | "this may or may not happen" |
| Portuguese | *vai / vou* (com certeza) | *pode ser que… / talvez* |

Minimal pair — same event, very different commitment:
- *"The board **will** approve the budget."* → you're confident; you're predicting
  it as good as done.
- *"The board **might** approve the budget."* → it's genuinely open; could go
  either way.

Say the wrong one and you mislead the room: *will* to a CEO sounds like a promise;
*might* signals "plan for both outcomes." Choosing between them is choosing how much
certainty you're putting your name behind.

**A second rival — *might* vs *could*.** Both express possibility and often swap
freely: *"We might/could lose the vendor."* Rough feel: *could* leans toward
"possible in principle / one option among several," *might* toward "there's a real
chance this specific thing happens." For a genuine maybe about the future, *might*
is the safe default.

**PT→EN mapping.** Brazilian Portuguese has no single modal for this — it spreads
"maybe" across *talvez*, *pode ser que*, *é capaz de*, and subjunctive verbs. That
gap is exactly where interference lives:
- *"Talvez a gente feche o acordo"* → **"We might close the deal,"** *not* "Maybe
  we close the deal" (present) and *not* "We will maybe close." English packs the
  *talvez* **into the verb** with *might*.
- *"Pode ser que eles não renovem"* → **"They might not renew."** One modal does
  the whole job; you don't need an extra "maybe."
- *Talvez* often pulls the Portuguese verb into subjunctive, so Brazilians reach
  for odd English shapes. English stays simple: *might* + base verb, full stop.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"I might to open a parallel negotiation."* → ✅ *"I **might open** a parallel
  negotiation."* — *might* + **base verb**, never *might to*.
- ❌ *"Maybe I will go, maybe I will not go"* (heavy, non-native rhythm) → ✅ *"I
  **might** go, I **might not**."* — let the modal carry the maybe; drop the extra
  *maybe + will*.
- ❌ *"The board **will** maybe approve it."* → ✅ *"The board **might** approve it."*
  — don't glue *maybe* onto *will*; *will* means certainty, so it fights *maybe*.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"We **might** move on the acquisition, or we **might** wait for Q4 numbers."*
   *— Two open options side by side; might names each as a live possibility, not a plan.*
2. *"The vendor **might not** renew on the current terms."*
   *— A real risk, not a certainty; might not flags "prepare for it" without predicting it.*
3. *"If diligence looks clean, we **might** sign before the quarter closes."*
   *— Possibility hanging on a condition; will would over-promise an outcome that isn't settled.*
4. *"I'm not sure the CFO will back it — she **might** ask for a re-forecast first."*
   *— A hedged guess about someone's move; might fits uncertainty where will would sound too sure.*
5. *"The AI-first positioning **might** take two full cycles to land in the market."*
   *— An estimate you can't guarantee; might keeps it honest instead of stating it as fact.*
6. *"Push the vote to next week — the numbers **might** change after the audit."*
   *— Justifying caution with an open possibility; the whole point is that it's not certain.*
7. *"I **might** work from home tomorrow if the strategy call moves."*
   *— Everyday tentative plan; nothing's fixed, so might, not will.*
8. *"Take an umbrella — it **might** rain later."*
   *— Classic everyday possibility; you're not predicting rain, just flagging the chance.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to play a CEO pressing you for a yes/no on a decision that
  genuinely isn't settled (an acquisition, a vendor switch, a reorg). Hold the
  ambiguity **only with *might* / *might not***: "We might move, we might wait" —
  practise resisting the urge to over-commit with *will*.
- Run the certainty dial out loud: take one future event (say, board approval) and
  say it three ways — *"The board **will** approve it"* (sure), *"The board
  **might** approve it"* (open), *"The board **won't** approve it"* (sure it
  won't). Feel how *might* sits in the uncertain middle between the two *wills*.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 028 — will / shall):**
1. *"**I'll draft** it this afternoon."* — the need lands on the table and you
   decide **as you speak**; that on-the-spot decision is *will*, not the
   pre-planned *going to*.
2. *"The vendor **won't** lower the fee"* — *won't* here means **refuses to**, not
   just "future negative"; will's negative can carry a refusal.
3. *"**Shall I** open the board meeting?"* — an **offer/suggestion** with *I*;
   *Will I…?* would be asking about your own future, a different meaning entirely.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 023:** *"**Does** the board **have** everything…?"* — you need the helper
  **does** to build the question, and after it the main verb drops to base form
  (*have*). Main-verb *have* doesn't invert on its own in modern English — *"Has the
  board everything?"* sounds archaic.
- **Unit 002:** *"**Is** the pilot already live?"* — the single move is **inverting
  subject and verb**: *the pilot is* → *is the pilot*. The *to be* verb jumps to the
  front; nothing else changes.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ***will*** — *"the board **will** approve the deal"*: you state you're confident,
   so it's a prediction, not an open maybe.
2. *"They **might push** a price rise."* — *might* + **base verb**; *might to* is
   never correct.
3. ***might*** both times — *"we **might** go with vendor A, or we **might** stay"*:
   "nothing's decided" signals two open possibilities, exactly what *might* is for;
   *will* would wrongly claim certainty.
4. *"The CFO **might not sign** before the re-forecast."* — one modal (*might not*)
   absorbs the *talvez… não*; no extra *maybe*, and the verb stays in base form.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** *might* é o modal do **talvez**: marca algo como
**possível, mas não certo** — uma porta aberta que pode ou não acontecer. É *might*
+ verbo na base, igual para todos os sujeitos (*I / he / we might go*), negativo
*might not*, sem *-s* e sem *to*. Serve para futuros e situações incertas: *"We
might close the deal"* (pode ser que a gente feche), *"They might not renew"* (é
capaz de não renovarem). O primo *may* é quase idêntico, só um pouco mais formal.

**As armadilhas.** A confusão-chave é *will* vs *might*: *will* é certeza
(*"o board vai aprovar"*), *might* é possibilidade (*"o board pode aprovar, ou
não"*) — escolher um é escolher **quanto de certeza você assume**. O português
espalha o "talvez" por *talvez / pode ser que / é capaz de* e joga o verbo no
subjuntivo, então o inglês tende a sair torto: (1) nunca *might to* — depois de
*might* vem o verbo puro; (2) não cole *maybe* no *will* (*"will maybe approve"*) —
o *will* já é certeza e briga com o *maybe*; deixe o *might* carregar a dúvida
sozinho; (3) *"Talvez a gente feche"* vira *"We might close,"* não *"Maybe we
close"* no presente.
