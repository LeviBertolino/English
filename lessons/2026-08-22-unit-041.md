### 1. 📘 Unit 041 — So am I / Neither do I

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** I'm worried the board will push back on the timeline.
> **You:** **So am I.** But I don't think we should slow the flagship pilots.
> **CFO:** **Neither do I.** The risk is in over-committing, not in moving fast.
> **You:** Exactly. I haven't seen the revised numbers yet, though.
> **CFO:** **Neither have I** — finance sends them Friday.
> **CEO:** Then let's decide Monday. I think **so**, anyway.

Notice the pattern before any rule. Nobody repeats the whole sentence to agree.
When the CEO says *"I'm worried,"* you don't say *"I am also worried"* — you fire
back a two-word echo: **"So am I."** When the CFO agrees with a **negative** —
*"I haven't seen them"* — the echo flips to **"Neither have I."** Each reply grabs
the **auxiliary** from what was just said (*am, do, have*) and puts *I / we / the
board* after it. It's the same auxiliary machine as yesterday's question tags,
pointed at **agreeing** instead of **checking**.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

To say *"me too"* with an auxiliary, English inverts the word order: the auxiliary
comes **before** the subject.

| You agree with a… | Use | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Positive** statement | **So** | *So + auxiliary + subject* | *"I'm in." → "**So am I**."* |
| **Negative** statement | **Neither / Nor** | *Neither + auxiliary + subject* | *"I don't agree." → "**Neither do I**."* |

Two iron rules — the same two as question tags, minus the polarity flip:

1. **Echo the auxiliary.** Take the auxiliary from the other person's sentence
   (*is, are, was, have, will, should, can*…). If there's **no** auxiliary (present
   or past simple), borrow **do / does / did**: *"We invested early." → "**So did
   we**."*
2. **Invert: auxiliary before subject.** *So **am** I*, *Neither **do** we*, *So
   **has** the board* — **never** *"So I am."*

**Worth memorising:**
- **So** matches a **positive**; **Neither / Nor** matches a **negative**. After
   *neither/nor* the auxiliary stays **positive** (the *neither* already carries the
   "no"): *"Neither **have** I,"* never *"Neither haven't I."*
- Casual spoken shortcuts: **"Me too"** (agreeing with a positive) and **"Me
   neither"** (agreeing with a negative). Correct, but flat — the *So/Neither +
   auxiliary* form is the executive register.
- **I think so / I hope so / I'm afraid so** = a short "yes" to a whole idea; the
   negatives are **I don't think so / I hope not / I'm afraid not**.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** To agree **fast and precisely** without parroting
the whole sentence. *"So am I"* says *"that's true of me as well"* in two words, and
it signals alignment in the room — which is exactly what you want when you're
building consensus with a CEO or board. A leader who can drop a crisp *"So do we"* or
*"Neither does the board"* sounds like a peer in the conversation, not someone
translating in their head.

**Decision rule #1 — So vs Neither (which one).** It's decided **entirely by the sign
of the sentence you're agreeing with**, not by whether you feel positive:
- Agree with a **positive** → **So** + aux: *"We're committed." → "**So are we**."*
- Agree with a **negative** → **Neither** + aux: *"We're not committed." → "**Neither
   are we**."*
The trap is that *"Neither"* still means *agreement* — you are agreeing with a
negative. *"I don't have the figures." → "Neither do I"* = *I also don't have them*
(agreement), **not** disagreement.

**Decision rule #2 — So am I (agreeing) vs question tags (checking).** Same auxiliary
engine, opposite job. Yesterday's tag **checks**: *"You're worried, **aren't
you?**"* (you want confirmation). Today's reply **agrees**: *"I'm worried." → "**So
am I**."* (you're confirming). If you're the one **responding** to someone's
statement → *So / Neither*. If you're the one **adding a mini-question** to your own
statement → question tag.

**PT→EN mapping — where Portuguese betrays Levi.** Portuguese agrees with a fixed
pair: **"Eu também"** (positive) and **"Eu também não" / "Nem eu"** (negative). Both
are invariant — you never change the verb. English refuses that:
- *"Eu também."* → ✅ *"So do I / So am I / So are we"* (**rebuild the auxiliary**),
   or casually *"Me too."* ❌ *"I also"* / ❌ *"Me too"* in a formal board reply is
   thin.
- *"Eu também não." / "Nem eu."* → ✅ *"Neither do I / Neither have I / Nor do we."*
   ❌ *"Me too"* (that's for positives — a classic slip), ❌ *"I also not,"* ❌
   *"Also no."*
- *"Acho que sim."* → ✅ *"I think so."* ❌ *"I think yes."* And *"Acho que não."* →
   ✅ *"I don't think so,"* not *"I think no."*
The reflex to build: the instant you'd say *"também"* or *"nem eu,"* stop and ask
*"which auxiliary was in their sentence — and was it positive or negative?"*

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"I don't have the numbers." — "**Me too.**"* → ✅ *"**Me neither**" / "**Neither
   do I**."* — *Me too* only agrees with **positives**; agreeing with a negative
   needs *neither/me neither*.
- ❌ *"**So I am.**"* / *"**Neither I do.**"* → ✅ *"**So am I**" / "**Neither do
   I**."* — the auxiliary must come **before** the subject (inversion); *"So I am"*
   is only correct as emphasis ("yes, so I am").
- ❌ *"I'm not sure the board will agree." — "**Neither don't I.**"* → ✅ *"**Neither
   do I**."* — after *neither* the auxiliary stays **positive**; the *neither*
   already carries the negative.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"**So am I.**"* (— reply to "I'm worried about the timeline.")
   *— Positive statement with "am" → "So am I": echo the auxiliary, invert it before "I". Not "So I am".*
2. *"**Neither do I.**"* (— reply to "I don't think we should slow the pilots.")
   *— Negative "don't" → "Neither do I": the sign flips to Neither, but the auxiliary stays positive (do, not don't).*
3. *"**Neither have we.**"* (— reply to "The board hasn't approved the AI budget yet.")
   *— Perfect negative "hasn't" → "Neither have we": echo "have", keep it positive after neither. Not "Neither haven't we".*
4. *"We're positioning as an AI-first player, and **so is our biggest competitor**."*
   *— "So" + auxiliary "is" + subject "our biggest competitor": you can agree about a third party, not only yourself.*
5. *"The CFO backed the pilot last quarter, and **so did the COO**."*
   *— No auxiliary in "backed" (past simple) → borrow "did" → "so did the COO". Agreeing with a plain past-tense fact.*
6. *"I haven't seen the revised forecast — **neither has finance**, apparently."*
   *— Negative agreement about someone else: "neither has finance". Third-person "has", still positive after neither.*
7. *"Will the board sign off Monday? **I think so**, but I'm not certain."*
   *— "I think so" = a short yes to the whole idea; the negative would be "I don't think so", never "I think no".*
8. *"You couldn't make the 8 a.m. review — **neither could half the team**, honestly."*
   *— Everyday-ish: negative "couldn't" → "neither could half the team"; the reply mirrors the modal exactly.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **"agree in two words" drill**: they say a mix of positive
   and negative statements from your world — *"I'm confident in the roadmap," "The
   board hasn't decided," "We invested early," "I don't trust that vendor," "Finance
   is nervous"* — and you must fire back the correct *So / Neither + auxiliary + subject*
   reply out loud, choosing the sign and rebuilding the auxiliary each time (*"So am I"
   / "Neither has it" / "So did we" / "Neither do I" / "So is finance"*). Goal: kill
   the reflex to answer *"Me too"* / *"I also."*
- Then a **board-alignment role-play**: your tutor plays the CEO and CFO reacting to
   your AI budget pitch, and you agree or align with each of them using *So/Neither*
   plus a short *I think so / I don't think so / I hope not* where it fits — so
   agreement sounds like a peer confirming, not a learner translating.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 040 — Question tags):**
1. **Should be *aren't we?*** → *"We're on track for the offsite, **aren't we?**"* A
   positive statement takes a **negative** tag. *"…, are we?"* (both positive) reads as
   sceptical/challenging — the opposite of seeking agreement.
2. ***"We're aligned on the roadmap, aren't we?"*** — positive *"we're"* → negative
   *aren't* + pronoun *we*. *"…, no?"* is a raw copy of *né?* and isn't English; *"…,
   isn't it?"* uses the wrong pronoun (*it* for *we*) and the wrong verb.
3. ***didn't he?*** — *"The CFO signed off…, **didn't he?**"* *Signed* is past simple
   with **no auxiliary**, so the tag borrows **did** → *didn't*. *Doesn't he?* is
   present (wrong tense); *hasn't he?* would need a perfect statement (*has signed*).

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 034)** ***should protect*** — *"We **should protect** the two flagship
   bets."* *Should* is a modal → base verb after it (*protect*, never *should to
   protect* / *should protects*). *Deveríamos* maps to *should*, not *would*.
- **(Unit 018)** ***I've been leading it for eight months*** — *leading* is an ongoing
   **action** → *have been + -ing*; and *for* + a length of time (*for eight months*),
   *since* only before a start point (*since January*). Portuguese *"faz oito meses"*
   uses the present, but English needs the present perfect for something still going on.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **Word order** → *"**So am I.**"* You must **invert**: auxiliary *am* before the
   subject *I*. *"So I am"* is only correct as emphatic confirmation ("yes, so I am"),
   not as *"me too."*
2. ***"Neither do I."*** — you're agreeing with a **negative** (*"I don't have…"*), so
   the sign is **Neither**, and the missing auxiliary is *do*. *"Me too"* agrees only
   with **positives** (wrong sign); *"So do I"* also needs a positive statement.
3. ***"So did we."*** — *approved* is past simple with no auxiliary → borrow **did**;
   positive statement → **So**. Not *have* (that would need a perfect: *has approved*).
4. Optimistic: ***"I think so."*** / ***"I hope so."*** Doubtful: ***"I don't think
   so."*** / ***"I'm afraid not."*** Never *"I think yes"* or *"I think no."*

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** Para concordar sem repetir a frase toda, o inglês usa
**So** (com afirmações) ou **Neither / Nor** (com negações) + **auxiliar + sujeito**,
sempre com **inversão** (o auxiliar vem antes do sujeito): *"I'm worried." → "**So am
I**"*; *"I don't agree." → "**Neither do I**."* É a mesma máquina de auxiliares das
*question tags* de ontem — você pega o auxiliar da fala do outro (*is, have, should,
did*…), e se não houver auxiliar (present/past simple), empresta **do/does/did**
(*"We invested early." → "**So did we**"*). Depois de *neither* o auxiliar fica
**positivo** (*"Neither **have** I"*, nunca *"Neither haven't I"*), porque o *neither*
já carrega o "não". Serve para **alinhar rápido** numa reunião — soar como par do CEO/
board, não como quem traduz na cabeça.

**As armadilhas.** (1) A cilada nº 1 é o português invariável: *"Eu também"* vira
*"So do I / So am I"* (reconstrua o auxiliar), e *"Eu também não / Nem eu"* vira
*"Neither do I / Me neither"* — **nunca** *"Me too"* para concordar com uma negação
(erro clássico). (2) **Ordem**: é *"So am I"*, não *"So I am"*; o auxiliar vem antes
do sujeito. (3) *Neither* significa **concordância** com algo negativo, não
discordância. (4) *"Acho que sim/não"* = *"I think so / I don't think so"*, jamais
*"I think yes/no"*. Se a mecânica do auxiliar ainda embolar, releia
`lessons/2026-08-21-unit-040.md` (question tags) — é o mesmo auxiliar reaparecendo.
