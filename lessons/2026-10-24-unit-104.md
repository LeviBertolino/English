### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** How did the platform cutover go?
> **Levi:** It went **smoothly** — we rolled it out **fast** and the teams adapted **quickly**.
> **CEO:** And adoption?
> **Levi:** Honestly, **lately** people have warmed to it. Two weeks ago they **hardly** touched it; now they use it **daily**.
> **CFO:** So the business case holds?
> **Levi:** It holds **strongly**. This was a **strategically** important bet, and it's paying off **well**.
> **CEO:** Good. Present it **clearly** to the board — numbers first.

Notice three different things happening. Most of these words are **adjective + -ly** describing *how* an action was done: *adapted **quickly***, *holds **strongly***, *present it **clearly***. A few are irregular: *rolled it out **fast*** (no *-ly*!), *paying off **well*** (not *good*), *they **hardly** touched it* (which does **not** mean "with effort"). And one adverb is sharpening an adjective, not a verb: *a **strategically** important bet*. That's the whole unit — the adverb is the word that describes an **action**, or turns up the volume on an **adjective**.

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

An **adjective** describes a **noun** (*a **quick** decision*). An **adverb** describes a **verb** (*decide **quickly***), an **adjective** (***surprisingly** quick*), or another **adverb** (*incredibly quickly*).

**Part A — the regular pattern: adjective + -ly.**

| Adjective | Adverb | Spelling note |
|---|---|---|
| quick | quick**ly** | just add -ly |
| careful | careful**ly** | just add -ly |
| easy | eas**ily** | -y → -ily |
| probable | probab**ly** | -le → -ly |
| strategic | strategic**ally** | -ic → -ically |
| true | tru**ly** | drops the -e |

**Part B — the irregular set (this is where you get caught):**

| Adjective | Adverb | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| good | **well** | never *"goodly"*; *well* = adverb (and "healthy") |
| fast | **fast** | same word — never *"fastly"* |
| hard | **hard** | same word for "a lot of effort" |
| hard | **hardly** | ⚠️ different meaning: *almost not* |
| late | **late** | same word — "not on time" |
| late | **lately** | ⚠️ different meaning: *recently* |
| early | **early** | same word |
| friendly / lovely / lively | *(no -ly adverb)* | use *"in a friendly way"* |

**Part C — an adverb can sharpen an adjective or another adverb.**
To turn up an adjective, you need an **adverb**, not another adjective: *a **completely** new strategy* (not "a complete new strategy"), *the plan is **absolutely** critical*, *the rollout was **incredibly** fast*.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this distinction exist?**
The adjective and the adverb point at **different things**. *A **clear** strategy* describes the strategy (a noun). *She explained it **clearly*** describes the explaining (an action). Swap them and you either describe the wrong thing or say something that isn't English. And a whole group of these words (*hard/hardly*, *late/lately*) change **meaning**, not just form — so the error isn't just "foreign-sounding," it can flip what you said.

**Decision rule — adjective or adverb? (the core question):**
Ask: **what is the word describing — a THING, or an ACTION / another description?**
- **Describes a noun → ADJECTIVE.** *a **fast** rollout*, *a **hard** decision*, *a **clear** memo*.
- **Describes a verb → ADVERB.** *we rolled out **fast***, *we pushed **hard***, *she wrote it **clearly***.
- **Describes an adjective/adverb → ADVERB.** *a **completely** new strategy*, *it's **strategically** critical*, *they moved **incredibly** fast*.

This stacks on Unit 103: **after a linking verb** (*be, look, seem, sound, feel*) you still use an **adjective** (*the plan looks **solid***), because it describes the subject; **after an action verb** you use an **adverb** (*the team delivered **solidly***).

Side-by-side minimal pairs — same root, opposite job (and sometimes opposite meaning):
- *a **clear** strategy* (adjective → the strategy) ⇆ *she explained it **clearly*** (adverb → the explaining)
- *we worked **hard** on it* (adverb = a lot of effort) ⇆ *we **hardly** worked on it* (adverb = almost not — opposite!)
- *the report arrived **late*** (adverb = not on time) ⇆ ***lately**, reports slip* (adverb = recently)
- *a **fast** decision* (adjective) ⇆ *we decided **fast*** (adverb, same word — never "fastly")

**PT→EN mapping:**
- **The -ly ↔ -mente map mostly helps you.** *claramente → clearly*, *rapidamente → quickly*, *estrategicamente → strategically*. Where Portuguese uses *-mente*, English almost always uses *-ly*. Trust it.
- **But three things betray you.** (1) *fast* and *hard* take **no** ending — the *-mente* reflex produces ❌ *"fastly," "hardly"* (meaning "with effort"). (2) *hardly* is a **false friend**: it looks like *hard* but means *quase não* — say *"the board hardly discussed it"* and you've said they **barely** discussed it, not that they discussed it intensely. (3) To intensify an adjective, Portuguese often reuses an adjective-looking word (*"um approach completo diferente"*), but English **must** use an adverb: *"a **completely** different approach."*

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. **Inventing *"fastly"* / treating *hard* as regular:**
   - ❌ *"We need to move fastly on the AI roadmap."* · ❌ *"The team worked hardly all quarter."* (this last one means they **barely** worked!)
   - ✅ *"We need to move **fast** on the AI roadmap."* · ✅ *"The team worked **hard** all quarter."* *(fast and hard are already adverbs; add nothing)*
2. **Using an adjective to sharpen another adjective:**
   - ❌ *"This is a complete different strategy."* · ❌ *"The timing is real important."*
   - ✅ *"This is a **completely** different strategy."* · ✅ *"The timing is **really** important."* *(to modify an adjective you need an adverb)*
3. ***good* where *well* belongs (action verb):**
   - ❌ *"The rollout went good."* · ❌ *"She presented really good."*
   - ✅ *"The rollout went **well**."* · ✅ *"She presented really **well**."* *(action verb → adverb* well*; reserve* good *for nouns and linking verbs:* "a good rollout," "it looks good"*)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **We rolled the new platform out fast, but we onboarded the teams carefully.**
   *— Executive. Two adverbs describing actions: *fast* (same form, never "fastly") and *carefully* (regular -ly). Both describe HOW, so both are adverbs — just different shapes.*
2. **The board hardly discussed the AI budget — we only had ten minutes.**
   *— Executive. *hardly* = almost not. This says the board **barely** touched the budget. If you meant they debated it intensely, you'd need "discussed it hard" / "at length" — the opposite meaning.*
3. **This is a completely new operating model, so expect a learning curve.**
   *— Executive. *completely* (adverb) sharpens the adjective *new*. "A complete new model" is wrong — you can't use the adjective *complete* to modify another adjective.*
4. **She presented the 2027 vision clearly and answered every board challenge confidently.**
   *— Executive. *present* and *answer* are action verbs → adverbs *clearly* / *confidently* describe how she did them. Contrast the linking-verb rule from Unit 103: "the vision looks clear" (adjective).*
5. **Lately, the CFO has been pushing back on almost every discretionary spend.**
   *— Executive. *lately* = recently (a sentence adverb about the period). Don't confuse it with *late* ("arrived late" = not on time) — same root, different word, different meaning.*
6. **This is a strategically critical decision, so let's not rush the timing.**
   *— Executive. *strategically* (-ic → -ically) is an adverb sharpening the adjective *critical*. Not "a strategic critical decision" — you need the adverb form to modify *critical*.*
7. **The quarter went well, even though the pilot started badly in week one.**
   *— Everyday + work. *go* and *start* are action verbs → adverbs *well* / *badly*. Never "went good"; *well* is the adverb that pairs with action verbs.*
8. **He speaks English really well now — two years ago he could hardly order a coffee.**
   *— Everyday. *really* (adverb) sharpens the adverb *well*; *hardly* = almost not (he was barely able to). Two different adverbs doing two different jobs in one sentence.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 103):**
1. **"a large German software vendor"** — a pure fact sequence: size (*large*) → origin (*German*) → type (*software*). You can't say "a software German large vendor"; facts follow the fixed order.
2. **sounds solid / look strong** — both are linking verbs describing the subject (*the roadmap*, *the numbers*), so both take a **plain adjective**, never the -ly adverb. The test: does the verb describe the **subject** (→ adjective) or **how** an action was done (→ adverb)?
3. **"The quarter went well and the early results look good."** *go* is an **action** verb → adverb *well* (never "went good"); *look* is a **linking** verb → adjective *good*.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 097:** ❌ *which* → a person is **never** *which*. ✅ *"She's the leader **who** drives our AI strategy"* or *"…the leader **that** drives…"* (*who* or *that* for people).
- **Unit 073:** Drop **that** (optional — the clause reads fine as *"The roadmap the board approved…"*), and **delete *it*** entirely. *The board* is already the subject of *approved*, so the relative pronoun **is** the object; adding *it* is the Portuguese *"que … ele/isso"* doubling error.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **quickly** — *go* is an action verb here, so the adverb *quickly* describes **how** the migration went. (Compare the fixed idiom "went well"; "went quick" is casual/nonstandard — use *quickly*.)
2. Error: **complete → completely**. To modify the adjective *different* you need an **adverb**: *"a **completely** different approach."* The adjective *complete* can't sharpen another adjective.
3. **Barely at all.** *hardly* = almost not. The sentence says the committee **barely** reviewed it before approving — a red flag, not praise. (For "reviewed it intensely" you'd say "reviewed it thoroughly / at length.")
4. **"The results look strong, and the team delivered consistently."** *look* = linking verb → adjective *strong* (describes the results); *deliver* = action verb → adverb *consistently* (describes how they delivered). The other pairings mix the two rules up.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra:** o **adjetivo** descreve um **substantivo** (*a **quick** decision*); o **advérbio** descreve um **verbo** (*decide **quickly***), um **adjetivo** (***completely** new*) ou outro advérbio (*incredibly fast*). A pergunta decisiva: a palavra descreve uma **coisa** (→ adjetivo) ou uma **ação / outra descrição** (→ advérbio)? Isso se encaixa na Unit 103: depois de verbo de ligação (*look, seem, sound*) use **adjetivo** (*looks solid*); depois de verbo de ação use **advérbio** (*delivered solidly*). A boa notícia: *-mente* vira *-ly* quase sempre (*claramente → clearly*, *estrategicamente → strategically*) — pode confiar.

**As armadilhas:** (1) *fast* e *hard* **não** ganham terminação — ❌ *"fastly"* não existe, e *"work hard"* (esforçar-se muito) é diferente de *"work **hardly**"*, que significa **quase não trabalhar** — um falso amigo perigoso. (2) *lately* = recentemente, não confunda com *late* (atrasado). (3) para intensificar um adjetivo, o inglês **exige** um advérbio: ❌ *"a complete new strategy"* → ✅ *"a **completely** new strategy"*, ❌ *"real important"* → ✅ *"**really** important"*. (4) e com verbo de ação use *well*, nunca *good*: *"the quarter went **well**"*. Se a diferença advérbio × adjetivo depois de verbo ainda embolar, releia `lessons/2026-10-23-unit-103.md`.
