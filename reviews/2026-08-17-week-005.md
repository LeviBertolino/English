# 🔁 Review Week 5 — Units 30 to 35

This week was one long theme: **modals** — the small words that carry
*possibility, ability, obligation, advice* — plus **there is / there are**.
These are exactly the words that separate a fluent executive voice from a
hesitant one. The danger isn't the form (they're easy: bare infinitive, no
*to*). The danger is **choosing the wrong one** and saying something you didn't
mean — telling the board something is *forbidden* when you meant *optional*, or
sounding *certain* when you meant *maybe*. This review is all contrast: same
sentence, different modal, different meaning.

---

## 2. 📋 Recap — one line per unit

- **Unit 030 — might** → *possibility, not certainty.* "We **might** move on the
  acquisition." One word replaces *talvez* — no extra *maybe* needed.
- **Unit 031 — can and could** → *ability & requests.* "We **can** hit the date."
  / "**Could** you send the numbers?" *Could* = past ability **or** polite present.
- **Unit 032 — must, mustn't, don't need to** → *strong obligation, prohibition,
  no-necessity.* "We **must** show ROI." / "You **mustn't** promise savings." /
  "You **don't need to** read all forty slides."
- **Unit 033 — there is / there are** → *existence.* "**There's** a budget gap and
  **there are** two open contracts." Not *has*, not *have* — the *tem* trap.
- **Unit 034 — should** → *advice & recommendation (your judgment).* "We
  **should** slow the smaller pilots."
- **Unit 035 — have to / have got to** → *obligation from outside (a rule, an
  authority).* "We **have to** get sign-off from the audit committee."

---

## 3. 🗺️ Confusion map — when each one wins

The whole week lives in these six contrasts. Learn the *decision rule* for each
and the modals stop being a guessing game.

### A. Possibility vs certainty — **might** vs **will**

| You mean… | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Maybe yes, maybe no | **might** (or *could*) | "We **might** wait for Q4 numbers." |
| I'm sure it happens | **will** | "We **will** close by Q4." |

> *might* leaves the door open; *will* shuts it. Never say *"might to"* — no *to*.

### B. General capacity vs a specific chance — **can** vs **might**

| You mean… | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A standing, general truth | **can** | "An unmanaged rollout **can** create risk." |
| A one-off possibility on this occasion | **might** | "This rollout **might** create risk." |

> *can* = it's in the nature of the thing (always possible). *might* = it may
> happen *this* time.

### C. Past ability vs one specific past success — **could** vs **was/were able to**

| You mean… | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| General ability in the past | **could** | "A year ago we **couldn't** run these models." |
| We managed to do it *once*, successfully | **was/were able to** | "After months of talks, we **were able to** sign." |

> ❌ *"we could finally sign"* — for a single achieved event, English refuses
> *could* and demands *were able to* (or *managed to*).

### D. My conviction vs an outside rule — **must** vs **have to**

| The obligation comes from… | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Me — my own judgment/urgency | **must** | "We **must** show a clear ROI horizon." |
| Outside — a rule, authority, process | **have to** / **have got to** | "We **have to** get audit sign-off first." |

> Both mean *obrigatório*. *must* = *I* impose it. *have to* = the world imposes
> it. In speech, *have to* is the safe everyday default.

### E. Forbidden vs not necessary — **mustn't** vs **don't need to / don't have to**

| You mean… | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| It is **forbidden** — do NOT do it | **mustn't** | "You **mustn't** share these figures." |
| It is **optional** — no need, but you may | **don't need to / don't have to / needn't** | "You **don't need to** read the appendix." |

> ⚠️ **The most expensive trap of the week.** *mustn't* and *don't have to* are
> **opposites**, not variations. Say the wrong one to the board and you invert
> your instruction.

### F. Advice vs obligation — **should** vs **must / have to**

| You mean… | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I recommend it — it's my judgment | **should** | "We **should** hire a Head of AI." |
| It's required — no real choice | **must / have to** | "We **have to** file before the deadline." |

> *should* = "here's my read, you decide." *must/have to* = "this isn't
> optional." Downgrading a real requirement to *should* makes you sound unsure;
> upgrading advice to *must* makes you sound bossy.

### Bonus — existence vs description: **there is/are** vs **it is**, and the *tem* trap

- **Introduce** something new → *"**There's** a problem with the forecast."*
- **Describe/comment** on something known → *"**It's** a serious problem."*
- Portuguese *tem* / *há* = **there is/are**, **never** *has/have*:
  ❌ *"Has a meeting tomorrow"* → ✅ *"**There's** a meeting tomorrow."*
- Agreement follows the **noun after** it, not *there*: *"There **are** two
  contracts"*, *"There **is** one gap."*

## 4. 🧪 10 recognition exercises (~8 minutes)

Mostly contrast items — choose the right modal and know *why*. Answers with
explanations in section 6. Retrieve first.

1. You're genuinely unsure whether the deal happens: *"We ___ move on the
   acquisition — diligence isn't done."* → **will move** / **might move**?

2. Fix if wrong: *"I might to open a parallel negotiation just in case."*

3. General, standing risk (always true of the technology): *"An ungoverned AI
   rollout ___ create reputational risk."* → **can** / **might**?

4. One specific past success after a long effort: *"After months of talks, we
   ___ sign the vendor contract."* → **could** / **were able to**?

5. Sharing the board's numbers outside the room is **forbidden**: *"You ___
   share these figures externally."* → **mustn't** / **don't have to**?

6. The appendix is **optional** reading: *"You ___ open the appendix — the
   summary covers it."* → **mustn't** / **don't need to**?

7. The obligation is a **compliance rule**, not your preference: *"We ___ get
   audit sign-off before we announce."* → **should** / **have to**?

8. It's your **recommendation**, not a rule: *"We ___ hire a Head of AI this
   year."* → **should** / **must**?

9. Choose *there is / there are*: *"___ one budget gap and ___ two contracts
   still open."*

10. Introduce a new issue to the CEO for the first time: *"___ a problem with
    the Q3 forecast."* → **There's** / **It's**? (And why is *Has* simply wrong?)

## 5. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

Bring these three contrasts to your tutor — they're the ones most likely to
misfire in a real board room:

1. **mustn't vs don't have to (prohibition vs optional).** Ask your tutor to
   fire rapid "is this forbidden or just optional?" scenarios about your AI
   governance rules — you answer with the right one instantly. This is the
   trap that can flip your meaning in front of the board.

2. **must vs have to vs should (my conviction / outside rule / my advice).**
   Role-play a board Q&A where the tutor asks *"Is that a requirement or a
   recommendation?"* about each item on your AI roadmap. Force yourself to pick
   *must*, *have to*, or *should* and justify the choice out loud.

3. **might vs will (hedging vs committing).** Practice presenting your 2027
   tech vision: use *might/could* for the uncertain scenarios and *will* only
   where you're truly committed. A CFO listens for exactly this line.

## 6. ✅ Answers

1. **might move.** Real uncertainty → *might*. *will* would promise the deal is
   happening, which you don't mean. (Unit 030)
2. **"I might open…"** — drop *to*. Modals take the bare infinitive: *might do*,
   never *might to do*. (Unit 030)
3. **can.** A general, standing property of the technology → *can*. *might*
   would narrow it to this one occasion. (Units 031/030 contrast)
4. **were able to.** One specific, achieved past success → *was/were able to*
   (or *managed to*). *could* only works for *general* past ability. (Unit 031)
5. **mustn't.** Forbidden = prohibition = *mustn't*. *don't have to* would say
   it's merely optional — the opposite, and a costly one. (Unit 032)
6. **don't need to.** Optional / not necessary → *don't need to* (or *needn't*).
   *mustn't* would forbid reading it — wrong meaning. (Unit 032)
7. **have to.** The obligation comes from an outside rule/authority → *have to*.
   *should* would demote a hard requirement to mere advice. (Units 035/034)
8. **should.** Your judgment/recommendation → *should*. *must* would present
   your opinion as a non-negotiable rule. (Units 034/032)
9. **There is** one budget gap and **there are** two contracts. Agreement
   follows the noun *after* the verb (one → *is*; two → *are*), never *there*.
   (Unit 033)
10. **There's** a problem — you're **introducing** it for the first time; *It's*
    assumes the problem is already known. *Has* is the *tem*-interference error:
    Portuguese *tem/há* maps to *there is/are*, never *has/have*. (Unit 033)

## 7. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**O que já deve estar sólido:** a *forma* dos modais — todos pedem infinitivo
sem *to* (*might do*, *should hire*, *must show*), e um único modal já carrega o
sentido de *talvez / consegue / tem que / deveria*, sem precisar de palavra
extra. *There is/are* para dizer que algo *existe* também deve estar mais
natural — lembre que o *tem* do português vira **there is/are**, nunca *has/have*.

**O que provavelmente ainda precisa de treino:** as *escolhas* entre rivais.
Três merecem atenção especial. (1) **mustn't × don't have to** — são
**opostos**: um proíbe, o outro diz que é opcional; trocar os dois inverte a sua
instrução diante do board. (2) **must × have to × should** — obrigação minha
(*must*), obrigação de fora / regra (*have to*), conselho meu (*should*);
rebaixar uma exigência real para *should* soa inseguro. (3) **could × were able
to** no passado — para um sucesso *específico* que aconteceu ("conseguimos
assinar"), o inglês exige *were able to*, não *could*. Se for reler dois
arquivos, releia **Unit 032 (must/mustn't/don't need to)** e **Unit 035 (have
to)** — é ali que mora a maior parte das armadilhas desta semana.
