### 1. 📘 Unit 056 — Phrasal verbs 2: the particle has meaning (out & in)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Before the board, I want the AI strategy **laid out** on one page.
> **You:** Done. I've **mapped out** the three-year plan and **spelled out** what each
> phase costs. We still need to **rule out** the two vendors that can't scale.
> **CFO:** Fine — but if the numbers don't **work out**, I'll **pull out** of the second
> pilot. I won't be **locked in** before I see the unit economics.
> **You:** Understood. I'll **factor in** the ramp costs and **bring in** procurement early
> so nobody feels **left out** of the decision.
> **CEO:** Good. And make sure the risks are **pointed out**, not buried.

Notice the pattern before any rule. Yesterday was about *where the object goes*. Today look
at the **little word itself**. *laid out, mapped out, spelled out, ruled out, work out,
pull out* all share **out** — and it keeps signalling the same handful of ideas: *make it
fully clear, finish it, or remove it*. *factor in, bring in, locked in, left out* turn on
**in / out** meaning *include vs exclude*. The particle isn't decoration — it carries the
meaning. Learn the particle and you can decode dozens of verbs at once.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

A phrasal verb is **verb + particle** (Unit 055). The new idea: the **particle runs in
meaning-families**. Two of the most useful for executives are **out** and **in**.

**OUT** carries three recurring ideas:

| "out" means… | Phrasal verbs | Plain sense |
|---|---|---|
| **make fully clear / complete it** | *work out, sort out, figure out, map out, lay out, spell out, carry out* | do it thoroughly, to the end |
| **remove / eliminate** | *rule out, phase out, cut out, leave out* | take it off the table |
| **exit / withdraw** | *pull out, drop out, back out, opt out* | step away from something you were in |

**IN** carries the opposite pull — **include, add, or join**:

| "in" means… | Phrasal verbs | Plain sense |
|---|---|---|
| **include / add into the plan** | *factor in, build in, bring in, lock in, take in* | make it part of the whole |
| **join / commit** | *opt in, buy in, sit in, come in* | become part of it |
| **submit / hand over** | *hand in, turn in, put in (a bid)* | give it to someone in charge |

The particle is stable across verbs — that's the whole point. Once *out = finish/remove/exit*
clicks, *work out / rule out / pull out* stop being three things to memorise and become one
idea in three verbs.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why learn the particle, not just the verb?** Because it turns memorising into decoding.
English has hundreds of phrasal verbs; nobody learns them one card at a time. But if *out*
reliably means *finish it / remove it / exit it*, then a verb you've never met — *thrash it
out, iron out the details, tease out the signal* — is already half-understood. The particle
is the reusable part. This is also **why the phrasal verb often beats its Latinate rival
even in writing**: *"work out the numbers"* implies *arrive at them through effort*; *point
out the risk* implies *make it visible to others*; *"calculate"* and *"indicate"* lose that
extra shading the particle smuggles in.

**Decision rule — same verb, different particle = different meaning.** This is the real trap
today (Unit 055 was *where the object sits*; this is *which particle*). Changing the little
word changes everything:

| Verb + particle | Meaning | Executive use |
|---|---|---|
| *work **out*** | solve / calculate / turn out | *"Let's work out the ROI."* |
| *work **up*** | develop / draft from scratch | *"I'll work up a first proposal."* |
| *pull **out*** | withdraw | *"The investor pulled out."* |
| *pull **off*** | succeed at something hard | *"We pulled off the launch."* |
| *rule **out*** | eliminate an option | *"We ruled out the cheaper vendor."* |
| *roll **out*** | launch / deploy | *"We rolled out the tool."* |

Minimal pair, one particle apart, opposite reality:
- *"The fund **pulled out** of the round."* → they **left**; the money is gone.
- *"The team **pulled off** the round."* → they **succeeded**; the money is in.

**PT→EN mapping — where the translation betrays Levi.** Portuguese has no particle system,
so there is **no instinct at all** for which little word to pick — the reflex is to grab the
Latinate cognate (*eliminar, incluir, desenvolver, resolver*) and skip the particle
entirely, or to pick one particle and reuse it everywhere. Two specific interferences:
(1) *tirar/eliminar* collapses *rule out, cut out, leave out, phase out* into one idea, but
each is different (*rule out* = decide against; *leave out* = forget/omit; *phase out* =
retire gradually); (2) *incluir* hides the difference between *factor in* (weigh it in the
calculation) and *build in* (make it a permanent part of the design).

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"We should **eliminate** the second vendor from the list."* → ✅ *"We should **rule out**
  the second vendor."* — not wrong grammar, but *eliminate* sounds like a written procurement
  memo; *rule out* is what people say deciding it in the room. Reach for the particle.
- ❌ *"I'll **develop up** a proposal / **work up it**."* → ✅ *"I'll **work up** a proposal"* /
  *"**work it up**."* — right idea, wrong shape: it's *work up* (draft), and (Unit 055) the
  pronoun splits it — *work it up*, never *work up it*.
- ❌ *"The investor **pulled off** of the deal."* → ✅ *"The investor **pulled out** of the
  deal."* — wrong particle flips the meaning: *pull off* = succeed, *pull out* = withdraw.
  One vowel decides whether the money stayed or left.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"I've **mapped out** the three-year AI roadmap on a single page for the board."*
   *— out = do it thoroughly/completely; "map out" beats "plan" because it implies the whole thing laid end-to-end. Separable: "map it out", never "map out it".*
2. *"We **ruled out** the cheaper vendor — it can't scale past 10,000 seats."*
   *— out = remove/eliminate an option. "Rule out" is the boardroom verb for deciding against; "eliminate" reads like a written report.*
3. *"If the unit economics don't **work out**, the CFO will **pull out** of pilot two."*
   *— two different "out"s in one line: work out = turn out/come good (result); pull out = withdraw/exit. Same particle, two of its three senses.*
4. *"Let's **factor in** the ramp costs before we commit to the number."*
   *— in = include it in the calculation. "Factor in" ≠ "build in": you're weighing the cost, not making it a permanent feature.*
5. *"I'll **bring in** procurement early so no one feels **left out** of the call."*
   *— in = add a party to the process; out = exclude. The in/out contrast in one sentence is exactly the meaning-family in action.*
6. *"We **pulled off** the launch two weeks early despite the outage."*
   *— pull OFF = succeed at something hard — not pull OUT (withdraw). The particle, not the verb, carries the win.*
7. *"Can you **sort out** the seating before the guests arrive?"*
   *— everyday: out = fix it/finish it completely. "Sort out" = resolve the mess, more natural than "organise".*
8. *"I need to **work out** how much the trip will cost before I book it."*
   *— everyday: work out = calculate/figure out. Same verb as the executive #3, same "arrive at it through effort" sense.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **"particle-swap" drill** on your own material: they give a verb
  and you produce two versions with different particles and explain the meaning gap —
  *work out / work up*, *pull out / pull off*, *rule out / roll out*, *bring in / bring up*.
  Goal: stop treating the particle as noise and start hearing it as the meaning.
- Then a **"decode the risk statement" round**: the tutor reads a formal board sentence
  (*"We will eliminate the option, include the cost, and withdraw from the pilot"*) and you
  re-say it live in natural phrasal English (*"we'll rule that out, factor the cost in, and
  pull out of the pilot"*), placing every pronoun correctly. This welds the meaning-family
  onto the word-order rule from Unit 055.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 055 — phrasal verbs, word order):**
1. ***carried it out*** — *"The board hasn't **carried it out** yet."* *carry out* is
   **separable**, and a **pronoun** object **must** sit between the verb and the particle. A
   noun could go either side (*carried out the plan / carried the plan out*), but *it* has no
   choice — *"carried out it"* is the classic slip.
2. ***look into them*** — *look into* is **inseparable**; the object (noun **or** pronoun)
   always stays **after** the particle. Unlike *carry out*, it can't split at all, because
   *into* is a preposition holding its object.
3. ***"put the meeting off"*** is the natural choice in a live sync — the phrasal verb is the
   **spoken register**. *"Postpone"* is correct but reads formal/written; save it for the
   board brief or the follow-up email.

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 049)** ***saw … hesitate*** (base verb) and ***saw … climbing*** (-ing). After
  perception verbs, **base verb** = you saw the **complete** action (the CFO hesitated, start
  to finish); **-ing** = you caught it **in progress** (adoption was mid-climb, a slice of
  it). Same verb *see*, two shapes, two meanings — and never a *to*.
- **(Unit 021)** ***was made by*** — *"That decision **was made by** the CFO."* Passive =
  **be + past participle** (*was made*), with the doer added by **by**. You **drop** the
  *"by the CFO"* whenever the doer is obvious, unknown, or unimportant (*"The decision was
  made last week"*) — keeping it only when *who* did it actually matters.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ***pulled out*** — *"…**pulled out** of the round."* *pull out* = withdraw/exit. *"Pulled
   off"* would mean the investor **succeeded** at the round — the opposite of getting cold
   feet.
2. ***left out*** — *"…isn't **left out** of the offsite."* *out* = excluded. To **include**
   the ramp costs in the model you'd say **factor** them **in** (or *build them in*).
3. ***work up*** = draft/develop from scratch; ***work out*** = calculate/solve. The particle
   decides: *up* pulls toward *build it into existence*, *out* pulls toward *arrive at the
   answer through effort*. Same verb, two jobs.
4. ✅ *"We should **rule out** that option and **work out** the impact."* — pronoun placement:
   *that option* → *it* → *"rule **it** out"* (separable + pronoun splits it), never *"rule
   out it"*. Both phrasal verbs sound native in the room where *eliminate/calculate* sound
   like a memo.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** Ontem (Unit 055) o foco foi **onde o objeto entra**; hoje é
**qual partícula** — e a ideia central é que a partícula **tem sentido próprio e estável**.
*out* costuma significar três coisas: *terminar/deixar totalmente claro* (*work out, map
out, spell out, sort out*), *eliminar* (*rule out, cut out, leave out*) ou *sair/retirar-se*
(*pull out, drop out, opt out*). *in* puxa para o oposto — *incluir/entrar* (*factor in,
bring in, lock in, opt in*). Aprender a partícula é o atalho: em vez de decorar cada verbo,
você **decodifica** dezenas de uma vez, e ainda ganha o matiz que o cognato latino perde
(*work out the numbers* = *chegar neles com esforço*, não só *calcular*).

**As armadilhas.** A pior é **trocar a partícula** — muda tudo: *pull out* (retirar-se) vs
*pull off* (conseguir algo difícil); *work out* (calcular/resolver) vs *work up* (rascunhar);
*rule out* (descartar) vs *roll out* (lançar). Como o português não tem esse sistema, o
reflexo é agarrar o cognato (*eliminar, incluir, desenvolver*) e pular a partícula, ou usar
sempre a mesma — mas *rule out ≠ leave out ≠ phase out*, e *factor in ≠ build in*. E não
esqueça a regra de ontem: nos separáveis o **pronome** racha o verbo (*rule **it** out*,
nunca *rule out it*). Se travar entre partículas ou entre o phrasal e o verbo latino, releia
`lessons/2026-09-05-unit-055.md` (ordem do objeto) e volte a este arquivo (sentido da
partícula).
