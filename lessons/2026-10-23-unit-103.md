### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Give me the headline on the vendor shortlist.
> **Levi:** We've narrowed it to one serious option: a **large German software vendor** with a **strong security track record**.
> **CEO:** How does the proposal feel to you?
> **Levi:** The strategy **sounds solid** and the pricing **looks reasonable**. The one thing that **seems weak** is onboarding.
> **CEO:** You look tired, by the way.
> **Levi:** Three days of board prep. But the plan itself **feels right** — I'm confident.
> **CEO:** Good. Let's frame it as a **bold, long-term strategic bet**, not a tool purchase.

Notice two different things happening. First, the piled-up adjectives *before* the nouns come in a fixed order: *a **large German software** vendor*, *a **bold, long-term strategic** bet* — you can't shuffle them freely. Second, after *sound / look / seem / feel*, Levi uses plain adjectives — *sounds **solid***, *looks **reasonable***, *feels **right*** — **not** adverbs (*not* "sounds solidly").

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

This unit has two parts. Both are about *where* an adjective goes and *what shape* it takes.

**Part A — order of adjectives BEFORE a noun.**
Opinion adjectives come first; fact adjectives follow in a fixed sequence:

| Opinion | → | Size | Age | Colour | Origin | Material / type | NOUN |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| bold | | large | three-year | — | German | software | vendor |
| exciting | | — | new | — | — | — | market |

- **Opinion before fact:** *an **exciting new** market* (not "a new exciting market"); *a **bold, long-term** bet*.
- **Among facts, follow the sequence:** *a **large German software** vendor* (size → origin → type), *a **small round** table* (size → shape).
- Two or three is the natural ceiling — nobody says five adjectives in a row. Past that, break them up (*a bold strategic plan, built over three years*).

**Part B — adjectives AFTER a linking verb.**
After *be, look, seem, appear, feel, sound, taste, smell, become, get, stay*, use an **adjective** — it describes the **subject**, not the action:

| Linking verb + ADJECTIVE | Why |
|---|---|
| The strategy **sounds solid**. | describes *the strategy* |
| You **look tired**. | describes *you* |
| The numbers **seem strong**. | describes *the numbers* |

**Do not** use an adverb here: ❌ *sounds solidly*, ❌ *looks reasonably*, ❌ *you look tiredly*.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this matter?**
Part A is about sounding native: the *meaning* survives a wrong order (*"a German large vendor"* is understandable), but it instantly marks you as a non-native speaker. Part B is about *grammar* and sometimes *meaning*: after *look / seem / feel* the word points back at the subject, so it must be an adjective — and the adverb version is simply wrong.

**Decision rule — adjective or adverb after a verb? (the core question):**
Ask: **is the verb describing the SUBJECT, or the ACTION?**
- **Linking verb → describes the subject → ADJECTIVE.** *be, look, seem, appear, feel, sound, taste, smell, become, get, stay.* → *The plan looks **good**. The deal seems **risky**. She became **nervous**.*
- **Action verb → describes HOW the action is done → ADVERB.** → *She presented the plan **clearly**. The team delivered **quickly**. He explained it **well**.*

Side-by-side minimal pairs — same adjective root, opposite jobs:
- *The results **look good**.* (adjective — describes the results) ⇆ *The team **performed well**.* (adverb — describes how they performed)
- *The pitch **sounds strong**.* (adjective — the pitch itself) ⇆ *She **argued strongly** for the budget.* (adverb — how she argued)
- *You **seem confident**.* (adjective — your state) ⇆ *You **spoke confidently**.* (adverb — how you spoke)

The one everyone gets wrong: **good vs well.** *Good* is the adjective; *well* is the adverb (and also the adjective for *health*).
- *The pilot **went well**.* (★ *go* is an action verb → adverb — never "went good")
- *The pilot results **look good**.* (★ *look* is a linking verb → adjective)
- *"How are you?" — "I'm **good**."* (fine, informal) / *"I'm **well**."* (health — both are accepted today).

**PT→EN mapping:**
- **Order flips.** Portuguese usually puts the adjective *after* the noun and is flexible: *"um grande fornecedor alemão"* → English stacks them *before* the noun in a fixed order → *"a **large German** vendor"* (not "a vendor German large," not "a German large vendor"). When you translate word-for-word, the order comes out wrong.
- **After a linking verb, PT actually helps you.** *"A estratégia parece sólida / soa bem"* uses an adjective, exactly like English (*seems solid / sounds good*). So the danger isn't the adjective — it's **overcorrecting** to an adverb because you've been told "add -ly," producing ❌ *"sounds solidly."* Trust the PT instinct here: *parece sólida* → *seems solid*.

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. **Wrong adjective order — opinion placed after fact, or facts shuffled:**
   - ❌ *"a new exciting market"* · ❌ *"a software German large vendor"* · ❌ *"a strategic bold plan"*
   - ✅ *"an exciting new market"* · ✅ *"a large German software vendor"* · ✅ *"a bold strategic plan"* *(opinion first; then size → age → origin → type)*
2. **Adverb instead of adjective after a linking verb (overcorrection):**
   - ❌ *"The roadmap sounds solidly."* · ❌ *"The numbers look strongly."* · ❌ *"The plan seems ambitiously."*
   - ✅ *"The roadmap sounds solid."* · ✅ *"The numbers look strong."* · ✅ *"The plan seems ambitious."* *(these verbs describe the subject → plain adjective)*
3. **good / well confusion:**
   - ❌ *"The quarter went good."* · ❌ *"The team performed good."*
   - ✅ *"The quarter went well."* · ✅ *"The team performed well."* *(action verb → adverb* well*; reserve* good *for linking verbs:* "the results look good"*)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **We're framing this as a bold, long-term strategic bet, not a tool purchase.**
   *— Executive. Three adjectives before the noun in order: opinion (bold) → age/duration (long-term) → classifying type (strategic). "A strategic bold long-term bet" would sound foreign.*
2. **We short-listed a large German software vendor with a strong security record.**
   *— Executive. Pure fact sequence: size (large) → origin (German) → type (software). You can't say "a software German large vendor."*
3. **The strategy sounds solid, but the timeline looks unrealistic.**
   *— Executive. Two linking verbs (sound, look) → two plain adjectives. Never "sounds solidly" or "looks unrealistically" — the words describe the strategy and the timeline, not an action.*
4. **The board seemed nervous about the burn rate, so I kept the tone calm.**
   *— Executive. "seemed nervous" (linking verb + adjective, describing the board). Contrast the action verb later: you could add "I presented calmly" — there the -ly adverb would be correct.*
5. **The AI pilot went well, and the early results look very promising.**
   *— Executive. The classic split: "went well" (go = action verb → adverb well, never "went good") vs "look promising" (look = linking verb → adjective).*
6. **I want a clear, simple one-page summary for the CEO — no 40-slide deck.**
   *— Executive. Opinion (clear, simple) before fact (one-page). Two opinion adjectives can sit together with a comma; the fact adjective stays closest to the noun.*
7. **You look tired — three days of board prep will do that.**
   *— Everyday + work. "look tired" = linking verb + adjective (describes you). "You look tiredly" is simply wrong; -ly adverbs don't follow look/seem/feel.*
8. **The coffee smells great, but honestly it tastes bitter this morning.**
   *— Everyday. smell and taste are linking verbs → adjectives (great, bitter). "Smells greatly / tastes bitterly" would change or break the meaning.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 102):**
1. The market is **exciting** (it causes the feeling → -ing); **you** are **excited** (you have the feeling → -ed). Same root, opposite directions.
2. They literally said **"I am a dull/boring person"** (*boring* = the cause, describing them). They *meant* **"I'm bored"** (*bored* = their own feeling). Classic reversal.
3. *"I'm interested **in** the capability but worried **about** the timeline."* The fixed pairs are *interested **in*** and *worried **about*** — never *worried with*.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 096:** *"I wish they **wouldn't** keep reopening it."* Use *wish* + **would(n't)** when you want **someone else's repeated behaviour** to stop — not the past simple, which would describe a present *state* you'd change (e.g. *I wish they used one number*), not a recurring, annoying action you want them to quit.
- **Unit 072:** **No** — you can't drop *who* here. *Who* is the **subject** of its clause (it's doing the owning: *who owns the portfolio*). A relative pronoun is droppable only when it's the **object** of the clause. Drop it and you fuse two sentences into one wrong one.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **"a reliable large German vendor"** — *reliable* is the opinion, so it goes first; then size (*large*) → origin (*German*). ("a large reliable German vendor" is also heard, but opinion-first is the safe default.)
2. **optimistic** — *feel* is a linking verb describing the team → adjective. "felt optimistically" is wrong.
3. Error: **good → well**. *go* is an action verb → adverb: *"The quarter went **well**."* (Reserve *good* for linking verbs: *"the results look good."*)
4. **"She presented the strategy clearly and the board looked impressed."** *present* = action verb → adverb *clearly*; *look* = linking verb → adjective *impressed*. (The other pairings mix the two up.)

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Duas regras num só tópico. **(A) Ordem dos adjetivos antes do substantivo:** opinião vem primeiro, depois os fatos numa sequência fixa — tamanho → idade → cor → origem → tipo/material. Em português o adjetivo costuma vir *depois* do substantivo e a ordem é flexível (*"um grande fornecedor alemão"*), mas em inglês eles se empilham *antes* e numa ordem rígida: *"a **large German software** vendor"*, *"an **exciting new** market"*, *"a **bold strategic** plan"*. Traduzir palavra por palavra quase sempre erra a ordem.

**(B) Adjetivo depois de verbos de ligação:** depois de *be, look, seem, feel, sound, taste, smell, become, get* use **adjetivo**, porque a palavra descreve o **sujeito**, não a ação — *"the strategy sounds **solid**," "you look **tired**," "the numbers seem **strong**."* Aqui o português te ajuda (*"parece sólida"* = *seems solid*), então o perigo é **corrigir demais** e botar *-ly* onde não deve (❌ *sounds solidly*). A pergunta decisiva: o verbo descreve o **sujeito** (verbo de ligação → adjetivo) ou **como** a ação foi feita (verbo de ação → advérbio)? E grave o par *good/well*: ação → *well* (*"the quarter went **well**,"* nunca "went good"); ligação → *good* (*"the results look **good**"*).
