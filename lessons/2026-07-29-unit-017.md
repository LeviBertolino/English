### 1. 📘 Unit 017 — Have you ever…? (present perfect 3: ever / never / been)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **Board member:** **Have** you **ever** led a company-wide AI transformation
> before?
> **You:** I **have**. I've **been** through two of them — one at scale, one that
> failed early. I've **never** seen adoption happen without executive sponsorship,
> so that's non-negotiable for me.
> **Board member:** **Has** the team **ever** shipped a model into production?
> **You:** Yes — they've **done** it three times. But we've **never** run one at
> this level of regulatory exposure, so this is new ground.

Notice the pattern *before* any rule. Nobody says *when* any of this happened —
*ever*, *been*, *never*, *done it three times* all report **experience across your
whole life up to now**. The question isn't "what did you do on Tuesday" — it's
"is this somewhere in your record, at any point, yes or no?" That's what the
present perfect does here: it scans your **entire past** as one open period that
reaches the present.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Same engine as Units 014–016: **have / has + past participle**. This unit adds
three experience words and one special participle.

| Piece | Meaning | Where it goes | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ever** | at **any time in your life** (used in **questions**) | between subject and participle | *Have you **ever** presented to this board?* |
| **never** | at **no time** — the negative of *ever* (PT *nunca*) | between *have/has* and the participle | *I've **never** seen that work.* |
| **been** | the participle of *be* — "visited / experienced and came back" | after *have/has* | *I've **been** to two AI summits.* |

**Key facts:**
- **ever** almost always lives in a **question**: *"Have you ever…?"* = *"Você já…
  alguma vez?"*
- **never** already carries the negative — so the verb stays **positive**: *"I've
  **never** been"* (✅), **not** *"I haven't never been"* (❌ double negative).
- **been** vs **gone** (both come from *be/go*, and this is the classic trap):
  - *He has **been** to the summit* = he went **and is back** (experience).
  - *He has **gone** to the summit* = he went **and is still there** (location now).
- Common short answers keep just the auxiliary: *"Have you ever…?" → "Yes, I
  **have**." / "No, I **haven't**."*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** Because sometimes the point isn't *a* moment
in the past — it's whether an event exists **anywhere** in your record, from birth
to now. *"Have you ever run a turnaround?"* draws a box around your **whole
life** and asks: is there at least one inside? The present perfect is the only
tense that treats your past as one **unfinished period stretching to the
present** — which is exactly what "in your experience, ever" means.

**Decision rule — present perfect vs the rival, past simple.** The same test as
yesterday, applied to experience: **is a specific occasion in view, or the record
as a whole?**

| | Present perfect (*Have you ever…? / I've never…*) | Past simple (*Did you…? / I didn't…*) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | your **whole life up to now**, no set time | **one specific occasion** |
| Question shape | *"Have you **ever** met the CFO?"* | *"**Did** you meet the CFO **at Davos**?"* |
| What you mean | is it **anywhere** in your experience? | what happened **on that one occasion**? |

Minimal pairs — same topic, different frame:
- *"**Have** you **ever** worked with this vendor?"* → life-wide: at any point?
- *"**Did** you work with this vendor **last year**?"* → one named period.

- *"I've **never** presented to a board."* → not once, in my whole career.
- *"I **didn't** present to the board **on Monday**."* → just that one meeting.

The hinge is the same rule that governs the whole present perfect: **the instant a
finished time appears (*at Davos, last year, on Monday*), you must switch to the
past simple.** *ever / never* belong to the open, time-less record; a clock or
calendar breaks them.

**PT→EN mapping.** Two traps here for a Brazilian speaker:
- The **"já… alguma vez?"** question → *"Have you **ever**…?"* The Portuguese
  *"Você já apresentou pra diretoria?"* is a life-experience question, so it's
  **ever + present perfect**, not the past simple.
- **"nunca"** is a *single* negative in English. Portuguese happily doubles it
  (*"Eu nunca não vi isso"* is dialectal, but the instinct to add *não* is
  strong). In English, **never** is the whole negative by itself: *"I've **never**
  seen it"* — never pair it with *haven't*.
- **"ir" / "estar"** collapse the *been* vs *gone* distinction. Portuguese *"Ele
  foi ao summit"* doesn't tell you if he's back — English forces the choice:
  **been** (back) vs **gone** (still there).

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *I **haven't never** presented to the board.* → ✅ *I've **never** presented
  to the board.* — *"never" is already negative; adding "haven't" makes a double
  negative English doesn't allow.*
- ❌ *Have you ever **presented** to this board **last year**?* → ✅ *Have you
  **ever** presented to this board?* / ✅ ***Did** you present to this board **last
  year**?* — *a named past time (last year) kills "ever" and forces past simple.*
- ❌ *The CEO has **gone** to three AI summits, and he's here now.* → ✅ *The CEO
  has **been** to three AI summits.* — *"gone" means still away; for a finished,
  came-back experience you need "been".*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **Have** you **ever** led a company-wide AI transformation? — *scans the whole
   career for one instance; "ever" + present perfect is the standard experience
   question — not "Did you ever lead…" with a time.*
2. I've **never** seen adoption succeed without executive sponsorship. — *"never" =
   not once in my experience; single negative, verb stays positive — the claim is
   about my entire record, not one project.*
3. The team has **done** it three times, but **never** at this regulatory scale. —
   *"three times" without a date = experience count; "never at this scale" flags a
   first, all inside the open period up to now.*
4. **Has** the board **ever** approved a budget this size before? — *life-of-the-
   board question; "ever … before" asks if it exists anywhere in their history,
   not on a specific date.*
5. I've **been** to both major AI summits this year — great for benchmarking. —
   *"been" = went and returned (experience); "gone" would wrongly say I'm still
   there right now.*
6. We've **never** positioned ourselves as an AI-first player, so this is a shift.
   — *"never" marks the absence across the company's whole past; sets up the
   present result — it's new territory.*
7. **Have** you **ever** eaten here before? — *everyday life; classic "ever" +
   present perfect experience question, no time named — "Did you eat here
   yesterday?" would be the one-occasion rival.*
8. She's **gone** to the airport — she'll call when she lands. — *"gone" (not
   "been") because she is **still away**; the whole point is her current absence.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **"credentials interview"**: they play a board member
  probing your track record with *"Have you ever…?"* questions — *"Have you ever
  killed a project mid-flight?", "Has your team ever missed a compliance
  deadline?"* — and you answer with the record, not a date: *"I have — twice,"*
  *"We've never had to, but…"* The goal is reflex: **ever** in their question,
  **have/never** in your answer, and **no accidental time word** that would force
  the past simple.
- Then a **been-vs-gone snap drill**: your tutor names a person and a place
  (*"The CFO / the data centre"*, *"Your peer / the client site"*) and a status
  (back / still there), and you produce the right one — *"She's **been** to the
  data centre"* (back) vs *"She's **gone** to the client site"* (still there).
  Have them slip in a Portuguese *"Ele foi ao summit"* so you practice choosing
  the distinction English forces but Portuguese hides.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 016)**
1. **already** goes **inside** the verb — *"The CFO has **already** signed off"*;
   **yet** goes at the **end** — *"We haven't locked the risk memo **yet**."*
   *just/already sit after have/has; yet closes the sentence.*
2. **yet** — ✅ *"Have you sent it **yet**?"* — *a neutral "is it done as of now?"
   question takes "yet"; "already" would add surprise ("já?! tão rápido?"), which
   isn't the meaning here.*
3. ❌ *"I've just approved… yesterday."* → ✅ *"I **approved** the AI budget
   yesterday."* **or** ✅ *"I've **just** approved the AI budget."* — *a named
   finished time (yesterday) forces the past simple; "just" + a specific past time
   cannot share a sentence.*

**Spaced review**
- **(Unit 010)** *"The two vendors **weren't** aligned on the roadmap, but the
  budget **wasn't** enough."* — *plural subject (vendors) → **weren't**; singular
  (budget) → **wasn't**; both are the past of "be", no do/did needed.*

**Recognition drill**
1. **been** — ✅ *"The CTO has **been** to the summit."* — *she's back, so it's a
   finished, came-back experience; **gone** would mean she is **still there** and
   couldn't be "back with a report".*
2. ❌ *"I haven't never worked…"* → ✅ *"I've **never** worked with this vendor."* —
   *"never" is already the negative; pairing it with "haven't" makes a double
   negative English rejects — one negative only.*
3. Experience: ✅ *"**Have** you ever **presented** to the board?"* One occasion:
   ✅ *"**Did** you **present** to the board on Monday?"* — *"ever" + present
   perfect scans the whole record; a named day (Monday) switches you to past
   simple with "did".*
4. ❌ *"Have you ever presented these numbers last quarter?"* → ✅ *"Have you
   **ever** presented these numbers?"* **or** ✅ *"**Did** you present these numbers
   **last quarter**?"* — *"last quarter" is a finished time, so it kills "ever" and
   forces the past simple.*

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** Ainda no *present perfect* (**have/has + particípio**),
esta unidade o usa para **experiência de vida**: perguntar ou afirmar se algo
aconteceu **em qualquer momento até agora**, sem dizer *quando*. **ever** =
*alguma vez* (em perguntas: *"Have you **ever** led…?"*); **never** = *nunca*, o
negativo de *ever*, dentro do verbo (*"I've **never** seen it"*); e **been** = o
particípio de *be* para "foi e voltou" (experiência) — diferente de **gone** =
"foi e ainda está lá". A pergunta padrão de experiência é *"Have you **ever**…?"*,
e a resposta curta guarda só o auxiliar: *"Yes, I **have**." / "No, I **haven't**."*

**As armadilhas.** (1) **Duplo negativo:** *never* já é negativo sozinho — nunca
diga *"I haven't never"*; use *"I've **never**…"*. (2) O *já… alguma vez?* vira
**ever + present perfect**, não past simple: *"Você já apresentou pra diretoria?"*
= *"Have you **ever** presented to the board?"* (3) A **regra de ouro** continua:
assim que aparece um tempo terminado (*last quarter, on Monday, at Davos*), troque
para o **past simple** — *ever/never* não convivem com hora ou data específica.
(4) **been vs gone:** o português *"ele foi"* esconde a diferença; em inglês,
**been** = voltou, **gone** = ainda fora. Se o contraste presente-vs-passado ainda
embolar, releia `lessons/2026-07-28-unit-016.md` (just/already/yet) e
`lessons/2026-07-26-unit-014.md` (past simple, o rival direto).
