### 1. 📘 Unit 070 — all · every · whole

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Where do we stand on the AI rollout?
> **You:** **All the data** points the same way. **Every business unit** we surveyed wants it — that's
> **every one** of them, no exceptions.
> **CEO:** So why aren't we live?
> **You:** Because **the whole quarter** went to security review. **Every review** took longer than
> planned, and **all of it** landed on one team.
> **CFO:** Are they burning out?
> **You:** They worked **all week** on it — not **every week**, this one. **The whole team** needs a
> reset before we scale.

Notice three different jobs. **All** takes a **group as a mass** — *all the data*, *all of it*.
**Every** walks through a group **one member at a time** — *every business unit*, *every review*, and
even *every one of them*. **Whole** wraps a **single thing** and means *the entire, complete* one —
*the whole quarter*, *the whole team*. And catch the sharpest pair: *all week* (the entire week,
start to end) vs *every week* (each week, again and again). That one distinction is the heart of
today.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Three words, three grammar shapes — the noun after each is the giveaway:

| Word | Followed by | Meaning | Verb |
|---|---|---|---|
| **all** | plural or uncountable noun (*all pilots*, *all the data*) | the whole group / amount, as a mass | plural (*all pilots **are***) |
| **every** | **singular** countable noun (*every pilot*, *every day*) | each member of a group, one by one | **singular** (*every pilot **is***) |
| **whole** | *the / my / this* + **singular** noun (*the whole day*) | one thing, entire and complete | singular |

Two things to lock in:

- **all + of** works like Unit 068: bare noun needs no *of* (*all pilots*), but *of* is obligatory
  before *the / this / my* or a pronoun (*all **of** them*, *all **of** the data*). With a full noun
  you can drop it (*all the data* = *all of the data*); with a pronoun you can't (*all of them*, never
  *all them*).
- **word order — whole vs all:** *the* comes **before** *whole* but **before** *all* too, in different
  slots: **the whole** day = **all the** day (= *all day*). So *the whole quarter* = *all the quarter*
  = *all quarter* — same meaning, three shapes.

For **people**, prefer **everybody / everyone** over *all (the) people*: *Everyone agrees*, not
*All people agree*.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (the core section)

**Why do these words exist side by side?** Because "todo" in Portuguese is one word doing three jobs
that English splits apart. English forces you to decide *how* you're seeing the group: as **one mass**
(*all*), as a **line of individuals** (*every*), or as **one complete unit** (*whole*). That choice
changes the meaning, not just the wording.

**Decision rule — all vs every (the one that trips everyone):**

| Say | How you're viewing it | Executive example |
|---|---|---|
| **all** + plural/uncountable | the group as a **single mass** | *All the pilots are on hold.* (the batch, together) |
| **every** + singular | the group **member by member** | *Every pilot is on hold.* (I checked each — none escapes) |

They often describe the same reality, but *every* adds *"one by one, no exception"* — which is exactly
what you want when reassuring a board: *"**Every** business unit signed off"* sounds more thorough and
individual than *"**All** the business units signed off."*

**Decision rule — all vs whole:** *whole* attaches to **one singular thing** and means *entire*; *all*
takes **plurals or masses**. So:
- *the **whole** team* (one team, all of it) — ✅   ·   *the **whole** teams* — ❌ (whole can't take a plural)
- *for **all** teams* (many teams) — ✅   ·   with time words both work: *the **whole** day* = ***all**
  day*.

**Decision rule — all day vs every day (the killer pair):**
- *I was in reviews **all day**.* = the **entire single day**, morning to night — **duration**.
- *I'm in reviews **every day**.* = **each day**, repeatedly — **frequency**.
- Get these two crossed and you tell the CEO you're permanently stuck in meetings when you meant just
  today (or vice-versa).

**PT→EN mapping.** The whole trap is *todo / toda / todos*:
- *todo dia* / *toda semana* = **every day** / **every week** (frequency, singular noun).
- *o dia todo* / *o dia inteiro* = **all day** / **the whole day** (the entire one day).
- *todos os X* = **all the X** (plural mass).
- *cada* = **each / every**.
So *"trabalhei o dia todo"* → *"I worked **all day**"*, but *"trabalho todo dia"* → *"I work **every
day**"*. Same little Portuguese word, opposite English structures.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"We review the roadmap **every days**."* → ✅ *"…**every day**."* *Every* always takes a
   **singular** noun and a singular verb — never *every days*, never *every are*.
2. ❌ *"I was in the office **every day** yesterday."* → ✅ *"…**all day** yesterday."* *Yesterday* is
   one day → duration → *all day*. *Every day* would mean it happens repeatedly.
3. ❌ *"**All the team** is exhausted"* (word order) / *"**All people** agree."* → ✅ *"**The whole
   team** is exhausted"* and *"**Everyone** agrees."* Prefer *the whole + singular* for a single unit,
   and *everybody/everyone* for people.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **All the data** points to the same conclusion.
   *→ *Data* is uncountable → *all* (not *every*). *All the data* = *all of the data*; drop *of* freely before a full noun.*
2. **Every business unit** we surveyed wants the rollout.
   *→ Singular noun (*unit*) + singular verb (*wants*). *Every* = each one individually — sounds thorough to a board.*
3. **The whole quarter** went to the security review.
   *→ One quarter, entire → *whole* + singular. *All the quarter* / *all quarter* mean the same; *whole quarters* would be wrong.*
4. The team worked **all week** on the migration — not **every week**, just this one.
   *→ *All week* = the entire single week (duration). *Every week* would falsely make it a recurring thing.*
5. **Every one of them** cleared the compliance check.
   *→ *Every one of + plural pronoun* = each individual member. Note *every one* (two words) = each; *everyone* (one word) = everybody.*
6. **Everyone** on the board agreed the AI budget was too thin.
   *→ People → *everyone*, not *all the people*. Takes a singular verb (*agreed* here past, but *everyone **is***).*
7. We can't put **all of it** on one team.
   *→ Pronoun *it* → *of* is obligatory. *All it* is ungrammatical; only *all of it* works.*
8. I've reviewed **every line** of the vendor contract.
   *→ *Every* + singular (*line*) signals "one by one, none skipped" — the reassurance a CFO wants to hear.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 069 — both / either / neither):**
1. ✅ *"**None of the platforms** passed."* You had **four**, and *neither* is only for **exactly two**.
   Three or more always switches to *none / any*.
2. ✅ *"**Neither** of the vendors **is** cheap."* *Neither* is already negative, so a second negative
   (*aren't*) is illegal — the verb stays positive. (PT's *nenhum … não* double-negative interference.)
3. ✅ *"Neither the price **nor** the tech…"* The partner of *neither* is **nor**, never *or*
   (mirrors *nem … nem …*).

**Spaced review:**
- *(Unit 063 — the / general vs specific)* ✅ *"**AI** is reshaping **our industry**"* — **no article**
   before *AI*. When you talk about something **in general** (the whole idea, not one instance), English
   drops the article. PT keeps it (*"a IA reshaping…"*), which is exactly what pushes the error.
- *(Unit 038 — the imperative)* ✅ *"**Don't** spread the budget…"* The imperative **drops the subject
   *you*** entirely and negates with *Don't* + base verb — never *"You don't spread"* (that's a plain
   statement, not an instruction).

**Recognition drill:**
1. ✅ *"**Every** business unit reported in, and **all** the feedback was positive."* *Business unit* is
   singular → *every*; *feedback* is uncountable → *all*.
2. ✅ *"We run this security check **every day**, and it takes **the whole afternoon** (or **all
   afternoon**)."* *Every* needs a **singular** noun (*day*, not *days*); and *the all afternoon* is not
   English — use *the whole afternoon* or *all afternoon*.
3. ✅ *"I was in board prep **all day**."* One Tuesday, entire → duration → *all day*. *Every day* would
   tell the CEO you're in board prep **daily, on repeat** — a very different (and alarming) message.
4. ✅ *"**The whole team** needs a reset, because **all the projects** slipped."* *Team* is one singular
   unit → *whole*; *projects* is plural → *all* (*whole* can't take a plural noun).

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

*All*, *every* e *whole* dividem entre si o trabalho que em português cabe na palavra *todo*. Use
**all** para o grupo como **massa** — substantivo no plural ou incontável (*all the data*, *all
pilots*), com verbo no plural; e lembre do *of* obrigatório antes de *the/this* ou pronome (*all of
them*). Use **every** para percorrer o grupo **um a um** — sempre com substantivo **singular** e verbo
**singular** (*every unit is*, nunca *every days*); soa mais minucioso, ideal para tranquilizar o
board. Use **whole** para **uma coisa inteira e única** (*the whole quarter*, *the whole team*), sempre
singular. Para pessoas, prefira *everyone / everybody* a *all the people*.

A cilada é o *todo*. *Todo dia* = **every day** (frequência); *o dia todo/inteiro* = **all day / the
whole day** (duração — um único dia inteiro); *todos os X* = **all the X**; *cada* = **each/every**.
Então *"trabalhei o dia todo"* → *"I worked **all day**"*, mas *"trabalho todo dia"* → *"I work **every
day**"* — a mesma palavrinha em português, estruturas opostas em inglês. Cuidado também com a ordem:
não existe *"all the day"* nem *"the all afternoon"*; é *the whole day* ou *all day*.
