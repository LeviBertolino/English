### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Can we talk before the board?
> **You:** Sure — let's **have a quick call** at four. I'll **have a look** at the deck
> beforehand.
> **CEO:** Good. I want to **have a serious discussion** about the AI budget.
> **You:** Understood. Let's **have lunch** first, then take it to the room. And honestly,
> after last week, the team should **have a short break** too.
> **CEO:** Agreed. **Have a good flight** tonight, by the way.

Notice something. *Have a call*, *have a look*, *have a discussion*, *have lunch*, *have a
break*, *have a good flight* — none of these is about **owning** anything. Here **have**
doesn't mean *possess*; it means **do or experience** the thing named. A Portuguese speaker
reaches for *fazer* (*fazer uma reunião*), *tomar* (*tomar café*) or *dar* (*dar uma
olhada*) — and each one, translated straight, breaks. That gap is today's whole lesson.

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

**have** does two completely different jobs. Today's job is the **action / experience**
one:

| Use of *have* | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **have** = **possession / relationship** (the *other* use) | *own, hold, be related to* | have a car, have two sisters, have time, have a headache |
| **have** = **do / experience an action or event** (today) | *do it, take it, go through it* | have a meeting, have a look, have a chat, have breakfast, have a shower, have a good time |

**Key facts about the action-*have*:**
- The noun names an **activity or event**; *have* just means *do/experience it*.
  *have a meeting* = *hold a meeting*; *have a look* = *take a look*; *have lunch* = *eat
  lunch*; *have a rest* = *rest*.
- Common partners: **have** breakfast / lunch / dinner / a coffee · a meeting / a call / a
  chat / a conversation / a discussion / a word · a look / a try / a go · a break / a rest /
  a holiday / a day off · a shower / a bath · a party · a baby · an accident · a good time /
  a nice trip / a bad day / trouble / a problem / difficulty.
- **This *have* can be continuous.** *"I'm **having** lunch."* / *"We're **having** a
  meeting right now."* — because it's an action in progress. Possession-*have* cannot:
  *"I'm having a car"* is impossible.
- **This *have* never uses *have got*.** *have got* only means possession. *"I **have** a
  shower every morning"* ✅ — *"I've **got** a shower every morning"* means you *own* a
  shower unit, not that you take one.
- To make it negative or a question, use **do/does/did** (never *have got*): *"Did you
  **have** a good flight?"*, *"We didn't **have** a meeting."*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this *have* exist?** English uses **have + [activity noun]** as its default,
neutral way to say *do/experience* an event — a meeting, a look, a meal, a break, a trip.
It's lighter and more idiomatic than a heavy single verb: native speakers say *have a
look* far more than *look*, *have a chat* more than *chat*, *have a meeting* more than
*meet*. The noun carries the content; *have* just delivers it. Miss this pattern and your
English sounds translated even when every word is "correct".

**Decision rule — action-*have* vs possession-*have* (and *have got*).** Ask: *am I
naming something you DO, or something you OWN?*

| | **action / experience** *have* (today) | **possession** *have* / *have got* |
|---|---|---|
| Test: can you say *"having"*? | **Yes** — *"We're having a meeting."* | **No** — *"I'm having a car"* ✗ |
| Can you swap in *have got*? | **No** — *have got* is wrong here | **Yes** — *"I've got a car / two sisters"* |
| Executive | *"Let's **have a call** at four."* (do it) | *"We **have** three AI vendors shortlisted."* (own the list) |
| Executive | *"I'll **have a look** at the deck."* (do it) | *"I **have** full visibility on the budget."* (possess it) |

Minimal pair — same verb, different job, different meaning:
- *"We **have a meeting** at three."* = an event we'll **hold** (action). You can say
  *"We're **having** a meeting."*
- *"We **have** a strong team."* = something we **possess**. You **cannot** say *"We're
  having a strong team."* — and here *have got* works: *"We've **got** a strong team."*

**PT→EN mapping — where the translation betrays Levi.** Portuguese doesn't use *ter* for
most of these; it reaches for *fazer*, *tomar*, or *dar* — and each is a trap:
- *"**fazer** uma reunião / uma ligação / uma pausa / uma festa"* → ✅ **have** a meeting /
  a call / a break / a party — **not** *"make a meeting"*.
- *"**tomar** café da manhã / um banho / um café"* → ✅ **have** breakfast / a shower / a
  coffee — **not** *"take breakfast"* (though *"take a shower"* is fine in American English,
  *have* is safest and always correct).
- *"**dar** uma olhada / uma passada"* → ✅ **have** a look / **drop** by — **not** *"give a
  look"* in the *"let me check"* sense.
- Where *ter* **does** map cleanly, it's the **possession** use: *ter um carro* → *have a
  car*, *ter tempo* → *have time*, *ter dor de cabeça* → *have a headache*. That's the
  other job — and there you may use *have got*.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"Let's **make a meeting** tomorrow."* → ✅ *"Let's **have a meeting** tomorrow."* —
  straight from *fazer uma reunião*. English **holds/has** meetings; it doesn't *make* them.
- ❌ *"I **take breakfast** at seven, then I **take a coffee** with the CFO."* → ✅ *"I
  **have breakfast** at seven, then I **have a coffee** with the CFO."* — *tomar* is not
  *take* here.
- ❌ *"I've **got** a shower every morning."* / *"We're **having** a great strategy."* → ✅
  *"I **have** a shower every morning."* / *"We **have** a great strategy."* — *have got*
  and the *-ing* form belong to opposite jobs; don't cross the wires.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"Let's **have a call** at four before the board meeting."*
   *— action-have: a call is an event you hold; "make a call" exists but means "phone someone / decide", not "schedule a talk". Portuguese fazer uma ligação tempts "make".*
2. *"I'll **have a look** at the AI budget deck tonight."*
   *— have a look = take a quick look (an action). Far more natural than bare "look at"; never "give a look" (dar uma olhada).*
3. *"We need to **have a serious discussion** about the 2027 roadmap."*
   *— have a discussion = hold one; "make a discussion" ✗. The noun carries the weight; have just delivers it.*
4. *"The team should **have a short break** after the quarter close."*
   *— have a break = rest (experience it). "Do a break" / "make a break" are both wrong.*
5. *"Did the CFO **have** a good flight to the summit?"*
   *— question uses did + have, never "had she got a good flight". This is the experience-have, so do-support builds the question.*
6. *"We're **having a meeting** right now — can I call you back?"*
   *— continuous form proves it's action-have (an event in progress). You could never say "we're having a strong team".*
7. *"Let me **have a word** with the board chair before we present."*
   *— have a word = a short private talk. A fixed action-have block; "make a word" is meaningless.*
8. *"I **have breakfast** with my daughter every morning before the strategy calls."*
   *— everyday: have breakfast, not "take breakfast" (tomar café). Present simple = a routine; note no "have got" here.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 053 — do and make):**
1. ***make*** — *"…has to **make a decision**."* A decision is a **result you produce**, so
   it takes *make*. *"Do a decision"* is the number-one *fazer uma decisão* slip.
2. ***did*** — *"…the team **did** three weeks of analysis."* Analysis is an
   **activity/task** (the work of analysing), not a product you hold → *do*. *"Made
   analysis"* is the mirror error.
3. ***made … done*** — *"We finally **made** a deal with the vendor we'd **done** business
   with for years."* *Make a deal* = **produce/close** an agreement (a result); *do
   business* = **carry on** a working relationship (an activity). Same *fazer* in Portuguese,
   two different verbs in English.

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 047)** ***hard to predict*** — *"The market is **hard to predict** that far
   out."* The pattern is **adjective + to-infinitive** (*hard to predict, easy to scale,
   ready to move*). English never says *"difficult for predict"*: after these adjectives the
   verb is the **plain to-infinitive**, not a preposition + verb.
- **(Unit 021)** ***was approved*** — *"The budget **was approved** at the last board
   meeting."* Passive = **was/were + past participle**. It fits because the point is **what
   happened to the budget**, not *who* approved it — the agent (*by the board*) is obvious or
   irrelevant, so English fronts the thing affected.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ❌ *"**make** a meeting"* → ✅ *"Let's **have a meeting** on Thursday to review the
   portfolio."* — English **has/holds** meetings, it doesn't *make* them. *"Make a meeting"*
   comes straight from *fazer uma reunião*.
2. ***"We have a strong AI team"*** can take *have got* (*"We've **got** a strong team"*) —
   it's **possession**. ***"We have a call at four"*** **cannot** (*"we've got a call"* is
   wrong for a scheduled event) — it's the **action-have**, so it uses do-support and can go
   continuous (*"we're **having** a call"*).
3. ***"I'm having lunch with the CEO"*** is possible; ***"I'm having a company car"*** is
   not. The continuous form works **only** with action-*have* (lunch is an event you're in
   the middle of). A car is **possessed**, and possession-*have* never takes *-ing* — you'd
   say *"I **have** a company car"*.
4. ***have … have*** — *"Every morning I **have** breakfast, then the team **has** a quick
   stand-up."* Portuguese *tomar café* pushes you toward *"take breakfast"* and *fazer uma
   reunião* toward *"make a stand-up"* — both wrong. English uses the neutral **have** for
   meals and meetings alike.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** O verbo **have** faz dois trabalhos totalmente diferentes. O de
hoje é o **have de ação / experiência**: *have* significa **fazer ou vivenciar** a coisa
nomeada pelo substantivo — *have a meeting* (fazer/realizar uma reunião), *have a look* (dar
uma olhada), *have lunch* (almoçar), *have a break* (fazer uma pausa), *have a shower* (tomar
banho), *have a good time* (se divertir). O substantivo carrega o conteúdo; o *have* só o
entrega. Dois testes separam esse uso do outro (posse): (1) esse *have* **aceita -ing** —
*"we're **having** a meeting"* — a posse não (*"I'm having a car"* é impossível); (2) esse
*have* **nunca** usa *have got*, que é só para posse (*"I **have** a shower every morning"*,
nunca *"I've got a shower"*). Perguntas e negativas usam **do/does/did**: *"**Did** you
**have** a good flight?"*.

**As armadilhas.** O português quase nunca usa *ter* aqui — usa *fazer*, *tomar* ou *dar*, e
cada um trai a tradução direta. (1) *Fazer uma reunião / ligação / pausa* → ✅ *"**have** a
meeting / call / break"*, nunca *"make a meeting"* (erro nº 1). (2) *Tomar café / banho* → ✅
*"**have** breakfast / a shower"*, nunca *"take breakfast"* (*"take a shower"* passa em inglês
americano, mas *have* é sempre seguro). (3) *Dar uma olhada* → ✅ *"**have** a look"*, não
*"give a look"*. Onde *ter* funciona limpo, é o **outro** *have*, o de **posse** (*ter um
carro* → *have a car*, e aí *have got* vale). Não cruze os fios: *-ing* e *have got* pertencem
a lados opostos. Se ainda confundir com *do/make* (a decisão vs a atividade), releia
`lessons/2026-09-03-unit-053.md`.
