### 1. 📘 Unit 084 — comparatives 1: *-er* vs *more* + *than*

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** In-house or license the platform?
> **You:** Building in-house is **cheaper than** licensing — but **far slower**, and the vendor's model is
> **more scalable than** anything we'd ship this year.
> **CFO:** And the risk?
> **You:** The second scenario is **riskier**, honestly. The upside is **much bigger**, but the payback is
> **less predictable**.
> **CEO:** So which is the **better** bet?
> **You:** License it. It gets us to market **faster than** the board expects — and our roadmap is already
> **far more ambitious than** last year's.

Watch how every option gets measured against another. *Cheaper than*, *more scalable than*, *faster than* —
each one ranks **this** against **that**. And notice two shapes doing the same job: short words add **-er**
(*cheap → cheaper*, *big → bigger*, *risky → riskier*) while longer words take **more** in front (*more
scalable*, *more ambitious*, *more predictable*). A few refuse both and change shape entirely — *good →
**better***, *bad → **worse***. The little word gluing the two sides together is always **than**, never
*that*, never *of*.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

A comparative says *X is \_\_\_\_er / more \_\_\_\_ **than** Y*. How you build it depends on the **length** of
the adjective/adverb.

| Type | Rule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1 syllable** | add **-er** | *cheap → **cheaper**, fast → **faster**, slow → **slower**, high → **higher*** |
| 1 syllable, consonant-vowel-consonant | **double** the last letter + **-er** | *big → **bigger**, hot → **hotter**, thin → **thinner*** |
| ends in **-y** | drop *-y*, add **-ier** | *risky → **riskier**, easy → **easier**, early → **earlier**, healthy → **healthier*** |
| **2+ syllables** (most) | **more** + word | *more **scalable**, more **efficient**, more **ambitious**, more **expensive*** |
| **irregular** | memorise | *good / well → **better** · bad / badly → **worse** · far → **further / farther** · much/many → **more** · little → **less*** |

**Key facts:**
- **Never mix the two.** It's *-er* **or** *more*, not both: *cheaper* ✅ / *~~more cheap~~* ❌ / *~~more
  cheaper~~* ❌. The double marker is the most audible non-native slip.
- **Irregulars never take *more*:** *better*, not *~~more good~~* / *~~more better~~*; *worse*, not
  *~~more bad~~* / *~~more worse~~*.
- **The connector is *than*** — one word, always: *cheaper **than** X*, *more scalable **than** ours*. Not
  *that*, not *of*, not *then*.
- **Adverbs follow the same split:** short ones take *-er* (*work **harder**, arrive **earlier**, move
  **faster***); longer *-ly* adverbs take *more* (*more **carefully**, more **efficiently**, more
  **slowly***).
- **Boosters go in front of the comparative:** *much*, *far*, *a lot*, *a bit*, *slightly*, *even* →
  ***much** bigger*, ***far** more ambitious*, ***a bit** slower*, ***even** cheaper*. (Never *~~very
  bigger~~* — *very* doesn't modify comparatives.)
- **Going the other way: *less* + adjective** = the opposite direction — *less predictable*, *less
  expensive*, *less risky*. *less* works with any length, no *-er* ever.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (the core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** A comparative ranks **two** things on one scale — faster/slower,
cheaper/dearer, safer/riskier. It's the native grammar of **choosing between options**, which is most of
what an executive does out loud: vendor A vs vendor B, build vs buy, this quarter vs last. Every time you
say *"this one is \_\_\_\_er than that one"* you're not describing — you're **recommending**, putting a bet
above its rival.

**Decision rule #1 — comparative vs superlative (the one that matters most):**

| | **comparative** (*-er / more … than*) | **superlative** (*the …est / the most …*) |
|---|---|---|
| ranks | **two** items against each other | **one** item against a **whole group (3+)** |
| connector | **than** | **of / in** (*the cheapest **of** the three*) |
| executive | *"Vendor A is **cheaper than** Vendor B."* | *"Vendor A is **the cheapest of** the four."* |

- Two options on the table → **comparative**: *"The SaaS route is **more scalable than** building it."*
- One winner out of many → **superlative**: *"The SaaS route is **the most scalable** option we reviewed."*
- (Superlatives are their own unit — the point here: *than* = exactly two; *the …est / of* = the field.)

**Decision rule #2 — *-er* vs *more* (length, not meaning):** the choice is purely mechanical — count
syllables. One syllable → *-er* (*cheaper, faster*). Three+ → *more* (*more ambitious, more predictable*).
Two-syllable words ending in *-y* take *-ier* (*riskier, easier*); other two-syllable words usually take
*more* (*more careful, more modern*). Meaning never enters into it — only the shape of the word.

**PT→EN mapping — two interference points:**
- **Portuguese has no *-er*.** It builds *every* comparative with *mais* + adjective: *mais barato*, *mais
  rápido*, *mais fácil*. So the instinct is to say *~~more cheap~~*, *~~more fast~~*, *~~more easy~~* —
  slapping *more* onto short words that actually need *-er* (*cheaper, faster, easier*). This is the #1 slip
  below. Long words (*more expensive*, *more ambitious*) happen to match *mais* and feel easy — the trap is
  the short ones.
- **"do que / que" → than, not *that*.** *"mais barato **do que** a licença"* → *"cheaper **than** the
  licence."* The sound-alike *that* is wrong, and so is dropping the word entirely.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"Building is **more cheap** and **more fast** than licensing."* → ✅ *"Building is **cheaper** and
   **faster** than licensing."* (short words take **-er**, never *more* — *mais* misleads you.)
2. ❌ *"Our roadmap is **cheaper that** last year's."* / *"**more scalable of** the old one."* → ✅
   *"**cheaper than** / **more scalable than** …"* (*do que* = **than**, one word, every time.)
3. ❌ *"The new numbers are **more good**"* / *"**more better** than Q3."* → ✅ *"The new numbers are
   **better** than Q3."* (irregular — *good → better*, and **never** stack *more* on it.)

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **Building in-house is cheaper than licensing the platform — but far slower.**
   *— two one-syllable adjectives → `-er` (`cheaper`, `slower`); `than` glues the two options; `far` boosts.*
2. **The vendor's model is more scalable than anything we'd ship this year.**
   *— `scalable` is long → `more`, never `-er`; `than` + the rival. Pure build-vs-buy ranking.*
3. **The second scenario is riskier, but the upside is much bigger.**
   *— `risky → riskier` (-y → -ier); `big → bigger` (double the g); `much` boosts the comparative, not `very`.*
4. **Our 2027 roadmap is far more ambitious than last year's.**
   *— long adjective → `more ambitious`; `far` intensifies; `than last year's` compares to a possessive.*
5. **License it — the new numbers are better, not more better, than the old plan.**
   *— irregular `good → better`; the inline ❌ `more better` flags the double-marker trap directly.*
6. **Cloud costs are rising faster than we forecast.**
   *— adverb `fast → faster` (short → -er, like adjectives); `than` can introduce a whole clause, not just a noun.*
7. **This route is a bit longer but much safer in the rain.** *(everyday)*
   *— `long → longer`, `safe → safer`; two boosters (`a bit`, `much`) sizing the gap on each scale.*
8. **My phone's battery lasts longer than my laptop's.** *(everyday)*
   *— `long → longer`; `than my laptop's` compares two possessions — the `'s` avoids repeating "battery".*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **build-vs-buy ranking drill**: they name two options from your world (*"in-house
  vs vendor," "cloud vs on-prem," "hire vs upskill," "pilot now vs wait a quarter"*) and you must deliver a
  one-line recommendation using **three comparatives** — one short (*-er*: *cheaper, faster, riskier*), one
  long (*more …*: *more scalable, more predictable*), and one irregular (*better / worse*) — each closed with
  *than*. This burns in the *-er* / *more* split under real decision pressure.
- Then have the tutor **bait the *more cheap* reflex**: they fire short adjectives at you fast (*cheap,
  fast, big, easy, high, safe, close*) and you snap back the comparative instantly (*cheaper, faster,
  bigger, easier, higher, safer, closer*) — no *more*, no hesitation. The speed is the point: the Portuguese
  *mais* has to stop being your first move for short words.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 083 — *enough* and *too*):**
1. *"The team isn't **senior enough** to run it"* / *"we'd have **enough runway** to fund one bet."*
   *enough* goes **after** an adjective (*senior **enough***) but **before** a noun (***enough** runway*).
   The word order literally flips depending on what follows.
2. **very** — *"very impressive"* = praise, which matches *"the board loved it."* *too impressive* would
   accidentally mean *impressive to the point of being a problem* — the opposite of a compliment.
3. *"too **much** pressure"* / *"too **many** competing priorities."* *pressure* is **uncountable** → *much*;
   *priorities* is **countable plural** → *many*. Countability decides, same as *so much / so many*.

**Spaced review:**
- **~1 week (Unit 077):** **interested** — *"I'm very **interested** in the AI bet."* You **have the
   feeling** → **-ed**. The wrong *interesting* describes a **thing's quality**, so *"I'm interesting"*
   accidentally says **you are a fascinating object** — not that you're keen.
- **~1 month (Unit 054):** **have** — *"Let's **have a meeting** on Thursday."* English **holds/has**
   meetings; it doesn't *make* them. The slip comes straight from *fazer uma reunião* → *"make a meeting."*

**Recognition drill:**
1. **"cheaper" and "faster":** *"The SaaS option is **cheaper** and **faster** than building it."* Both are
   one-syllable words → *-er*; *more cheap / more fast* double-marks and copies the Portuguese *mais*.
2. **the cheapest** — the clue is *"Of the three vendors"*: a field of **3+** → **superlative** (*the
   cheapest*), not the two-way comparative *cheaper*. *than* would be needed only for exactly two.
3. **than** — *"Our platform is more reliable **than** the old one."* The connector is always *than*, never
   the sound-alike *that*.
4. **better** — *"The Q4 numbers are **better** than Q3."* *good* is irregular → *better*; it never takes
   *more* (*more good / more better* are both wrong).

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

O comparativo coloca **duas** coisas numa mesma escala — mais rápido/mais lento, mais barato/mais caro — e é
a gramática natural de **escolher entre opções**, que é quase tudo que um executivo faz em voz alta. A
construção é *X é \_\_\_\_er / more \_\_\_\_ **than** Y*, e a escolha entre *-er* e *more* é **mecânica, não de
sentido**: palavra de **1 sílaba** leva **-er** (*cheap → cheaper, fast → faster*); terminada em **-y** vira
**-ier** (*risky → riskier, easy → easier*); palavra **longa (2+ sílabas)** leva **more** na frente (*more
scalable, more ambitious*); e os **irregulares** mudam de forma — *good → **better***, *bad → **worse***,
*far → **further***. Boosters vão **antes**: *much bigger, far more ambitious, a bit slower* (nunca *very
bigger*). Para o lado oposto, use *less* (*less predictable*).

As ciladas do português: (1) o português constrói **todo** comparativo com *mais* + adjetivo, então a mão
vai para *~~more cheap~~, ~~more fast~~, ~~more easy~~* — mas palavra curta pede **-er** (*cheaper, faster,
easier*); esse é o erro nº 1. (2) *do que* é **than**, uma palavra só — nunca *that* nem *of* (*cheaper
**than** X*). (3) nunca empilhe *more* em irregular: é *better*, jamais *~~more good~~* ou *~~more
better~~*. E não confunda com o superlativo: *than* = exatamente **dois**; *the …est / of* = o grupo inteiro
(3+). Se a dupla *so/such* × *enough/too* ainda embolar, releia `lessons/2026-10-03-unit-083.md`.
