### 1. 📘 Unit 046 — Preposition + -ing (interested in doing / committed to investing / before making)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Where's your head on the AI budget?
> **You:** We're **committed to investing** over three years, and I'm **focused on
> scaling** the platform — not on **chasing** every trend.
> **CEO:** And the vendor risk?
> **You:** **Before committing** the budget, we benchmarked two more. **Instead of
> waiting** for full consensus, I'd rather stage it.
> **CEO:** Good. I **look forward to seeing** the roadmap.
> **You:** And I **look forward to presenting** it next week.

Notice the pattern before any rule. Every verb after a little linking word ends in
**-ing**: *committed to **investing***, *focused on **scaling***, *on **chasing***,
*before **committing***, *instead of **waiting***, *look forward to **seeing/
presenting***. Those linking words — *to, on, before, instead of* — are all
**prepositions**, and English has one iron rule: **after a preposition, a verb
takes -ing, never the infinitive.** Portuguese uses the plain infinitive here
(*comprometido a investir*, *antes de decidir*), which is exactly the trap.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

When a verb comes **right after a preposition**, it must be in the **-ing** form.

| Frame | Preposition | Verb → -ing |
|---|---|---|
| adjective + prep | interested **in** | invest → **investing** |
| adjective + prep | committed **to** | invest → **investing** |
| adjective + prep | good **at** / bad **at** | align → **aligning** |
| verb + prep | focus **on** | scale → **scaling** |
| verb + prep | look forward **to** | present → **presenting** |
| time / manner prep | before / after / without / by / instead of | commit → **committing** |

**Key facts:**
- **Preposition + verb → always -ing.** *in, on, at, of, for, about, before,
  after, without, by, instead of, in addition to* — every one forces the *-ing*.
- **The killer *to*.** Some fixed phrases end in *to* that is a **preposition**,
  not an infinitive marker: **look forward to**, **committed to**, **in addition
  to**, **object to**, **used to (an idea)**, **when it comes to**. After *these*,
  the verb is **-ing**: *"look forward to **meeting** you"*, *"committed to
  **investing**"* — never *"to meet" / "to invest"*.
- **Common adjective + preposition pairs:** *interested **in**, good/bad **at**,
  responsible **for**, focused **on**, committed **to**, worried **about**, keen
  **on**, capable **of**, involved **in***.
- **Common verb + preposition pairs:** *think **about**, insist **on**, succeed
  **in**, believe **in**, apologise **for**, give up, focus **on**, look forward
  **to***.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** English treats *"invest"* differently depending
on what sits in front of it. After a **preposition**, the language needs a **noun**
— and the *-ing* form is the verb dressed up as a noun (*investing* = "the act of
investing"). So *"committed **to** investing"* literally means "committed to the
act of investing". This is the grammar of everything a leader says about
**stance, focus, and sequence**: *committed to…, focused on…, responsible for…,
before deciding…, instead of waiting…, without asking…*. Get the *-ing* automatic
and you sound fluent exactly where executives speak most — describing priorities
and process.

**Decision rule — preposition + -ing vs the rival, verb + to-infinitive.** This is
*the* confusion, because both patterns can appear one word apart:

| | Verb of intention + **to** (infinitive) | Preposition + **-ing** |
|---|---|---|
| Example | *"We **decided to** invest."* | *"We're **committed to** investing."* |
| The word before | a plain verb: *want, plan, decide, hope, need, agree* | a preposition, often in a fixed phrase: *committed **to**, focused **on**, interested **in**, before, without* |
| Verb form | base verb (*invest*) | **-ing** (*investing*) |

Minimal pairs — same idea, the trigger flips the form:
- *"We **plan to** expand into Europe."* (verb *plan* + to → base verb) vs *"We're
  **focused on** expanding into Europe."* (prep *on* → **-ing**). ✅ both
- *"I **want to** meet the board."* (verb *want* + to) vs *"I **look forward to**
  meeting the board."* (fixed prep *to* → **-ing**). ✅ both

**The *to* test.** When you hit *to*, ask: is it glued to the word before as a
**fixed phrase** (*look forward **to**, committed **to**, in addition **to**, when
it comes **to***)? → *to* is a **preposition** → **-ing**. Is it just *to* after a
plain intention verb (*want to, plan to, decide to, need to*)? → **infinitive** →
base verb. This one test kills the *"look forward to meet"* error for good.

**PT→EN mapping — where Portuguese betrays Levi.** Portuguese puts the **plain
infinitive** after every preposition. English refuses:
- *"interessado **em investir**"* → ✅ *"interested **in investing**"*, ❌
  *"interested in to invest"* / *"interested in invest"*.
- *"**antes de** decidir"* → ✅ *"**before deciding**"*, ❌ *"before to decide"*.
- *"**sem** perguntar"* → ✅ *"**without asking**"*, ❌ *"without to ask" / "without
  ask"*.
- *"**em vez de** esperar"* → ✅ *"**instead of waiting**"*, ❌ *"instead of to
  wait"*.
- *"comprometido **a investir**"* → ✅ *"committed **to investing**"*, ❌ *"committed
  to invest"* (the *to* looks like an infinitive, but here it's a preposition).

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"We're **interested in to invest** in AI infrastructure."* → ✅ *"We're
  **interested in investing** in AI infrastructure."* — after a preposition (*in*),
  the verb is **-ing**, never *to* + verb.
- ❌ *"**Before to make** the final call, let's align."* → ✅ *"**Before making** the
  final call, let's align."* — *before/after/without* + **-ing**, never + *to*.
- ❌ *"I **look forward to meet** you next week."* → ✅ *"I **look forward to meeting**
  you next week."* — the *to* in *look forward to* is a **preposition**, so the
  verb takes **-ing**.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"We're **committed to investing** in AI over the next three years."*
   *— committed to + -ing; the "to" is a preposition, not an infinitive, so it's "investing", never "committed to invest".*
2. *"I'm **focused on scaling** the platform, not on **chasing** every trend."*
   *— focus on + -ing (twice); the second "on chasing" reuses the same preposition, so the -ing repeats.*
3. *"I **look forward to presenting** the 2027 vision to the CEO next week."*
   *— the classic trap: "look forward to" ends in a preposition "to", so it's "presenting", not "to present".*
4. *"**Before committing** the budget, we benchmarked two more vendors."*
   *— before + -ing marks the earlier action; "before to commit" is the direct-from-Portuguese error.*
5. *"We **succeeded in cutting** the pilot time in half."*
   *— succeed in + -ing; the result-verb "cut" becomes "cutting" after the preposition "in".*
6. *"**Instead of waiting** for full consensus, I made the call."*
   *— instead of + -ing; "instead of to wait" copies the Portuguese "em vez de esperar" and breaks.*
7. *"I'm **good at reading** a room, but **bad at keeping** in touch afterwards."*
   *— everyday life; good/bad at + -ing — a very frequent adjective+preposition frame.*
8. *"She left the meeting **without saying** a word to anyone."*
   *— everyday; without + -ing = the action that didn't happen; never "without to say" / "without say".*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **"stance frame" drill**: they fire an adjective+preposition
  frame plus a topic from your world — *"interested in… / committed to… / focused
  on… / responsible for… / worried about… / good at… (the AI budget, the vendor,
  the 2027 vision)"* — and you must complete it instantly with **-ing**
  (*"committed to investing", "focused on scaling", "worried about overpromising"*).
  Goal: make the *-ing*-after-preposition reflex automatic so *"interested in to
  invest"* never surfaces in a real room.
- Then a **"to-trap" bait round**: have your tutor deliberately mix intention verbs
  (*plan to, decide to, want to*) with fixed-*to* phrases (*look forward to,
  committed to, in addition to, when it comes to*) and you supply the right form
  live — base verb after the first group, **-ing** after the second. This is the
  one distinction that most often trips up senior Brazilian speakers in English.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 045 — to for purpose):**
1. ***to*** — *"I flew to Boston **to close** the vendor deal."* The purpose of an
   **action** is *to* + verb (= "in order to"); *"for close"* is impossible —
   there is no *for* + verb in English.
2. ***to*** — *"…a consultant **to review** the AI strategy."* Both *for* + verb
   and *for to* + verb are wrong; plain *to* alone carries "in order to."
3. ***for*** — *"I'm here **for the board meeting**."* Before a **noun** the word is
   *for*, not *to*; *to* + verb is only for a **purpose action**.

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 039)** ***It's*** — *"**It's** important to move now on the AI budget."*
  English requires a subject in front of the verb; Portuguese *"É importante…"*
  hides it, but English uses the empty subject *it* and can never drop it
  (*"Is important…"* is the classic slip).
- **(Unit 018)** ***I've been*** — *"**I've been** leading the transformation for
  eight months."* A situation that started in the past and **continues to now**
  takes the present perfect (*have been + -ing*), not the present simple; *"I lead
  it for eight months"* can't carry a span reaching the present.

**Recognition drill:**
1. First blank → ***to invest*** (*"We **decided to** invest…"* — the intention verb
   *decide* + **to**-infinitive). Second → ***to investing*** (*"we're **committed
   to** investing…"* — *committed to* ends in a **preposition** *to*, so **-ing**).
   Same word "invest", but *decide* triggers the infinitive and *committed to*
   triggers *-ing*.
2. ***leading*** — *"interested **in leading** the industry."* After the preposition
   *in*, the verb is **-ing**; *"in to lead" / "in lead"* are both wrong.
3. ***to meeting*** — *"I **look forward to meeting** the board."* Here *to* is a
   **preposition** (part of the fixed phrase *look forward to*), so the verb takes
   **-ing**, never *"to meet"*.
4. **Wrong: *before to make*.** Fix → *"**Before making** the final call, let's
   stress-test the numbers."* *before* is a preposition, so the verb is **-ing**;
   there is no *before* + *to* + verb.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** Em inglês, quando um verbo vem **logo depois de uma
preposição**, ele fica na forma **-ing** — nunca no infinitivo. A forma *-ing* é o
verbo funcionando como substantivo (*investing* = "o ato de investir"), e é por
isso que cabe depois de *in, on, at, of, for, about, before, after, without, by,
instead of*. É a gramática de tudo que um executivo diz sobre **postura, foco e
sequência**: *committed **to investing***, *focused **on scaling***, *responsible
**for driving** the AI agenda*, *before **deciding***, *instead of **waiting***.
Contraste com o rival **verbo de intenção + to** (*want/plan/decide **to** + verbo
na base*): *"We **decided to** invest"* (infinitivo) × *"We're **committed to**
investing"* (preposição → -ing).

**As armadilhas.** (1) O português usa o **infinitivo** depois de toda preposição
(*interessado em investir, antes de decidir, sem perguntar*), e o inglês **recusa**:
diga *"interested **in investing**", "**before deciding**", "**without asking**"* —
jamais *"in to invest", "before to decide", "without to ask"*. (2) A cilada do
***to***: em frases fixas como **look forward to**, **committed to**, **in addition
to**, **when it comes to**, o *to* é uma **preposição**, não marca de infinitivo —
então o verbo é **-ing**: *"I **look forward to meeting** you"*, nunca *"to meet"*.
O teste: o *to* está colado numa expressão fixa? → preposição → **-ing**. É só *to*
depois de *want/plan/decide/need*? → infinitivo → verbo na base. Se o par -ing ×
infinitivo depois de verbos ainda embolar, releia `lessons/2026-08-23-unit-042.md`
(verb + -ing) e `lessons/2026-08-25-unit-044.md` (verb + object + to).
