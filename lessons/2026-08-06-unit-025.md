### 1. 📘 Unit 025 — I used to… (past habits and states that are no longer true)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Remember when every AI decision **used to** land on my desk?
> **You:** I do. We **used to** approve each pilot one by one, and the portfolio
> **used to** be a spreadsheet nobody trusted. There **didn't use to** be a real
> governance layer.
> **CEO:** And now?
> **You:** Now the framework decides most of it. I **used to** spend Fridays
> chasing sign-offs — I don't anymore. That whole ritual is gone.

Notice the pattern *before* any rule. Every *used to* here paints a **past
habit or state that is no longer true**: the decisions no longer land on the CEO's
desk, the portfolio is no longer a spreadsheet, you no longer chase sign-offs.
Portuguese would reach for the **imperfeito** — *"eu costumava…"*, *"tudo passava
pela minha mesa"*, *"eu passava as sextas…"*. That contrast — *then, repeatedly;
not anymore* — is the entire job of this structure.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

*used to +* **base verb**. It exists **only in the past** — there is no present
form.

| Form | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | used to + base | *We **used to** approve each pilot separately.* |
| **Negative** | didn't use to + base | *There **didn't use to** be a governance layer.* |
| **Question** | Did … use to + base? | ***Did** the board **use to** meet weekly?* |

The load-bearing detail: after **did / didn't**, it drops the **-d** — *use to*,
not *used to*. Just like any verb after *did* goes back to its base (*did you
go?*, not *did you went?*), *used* loses its ending: **didn't use to**, **did you
use to**. In the positive it keeps the *-d*: **used to**.

*used to* covers both **repeated actions** (*I used to spend Fridays chasing
sign-offs*) and **past states** (*the portfolio used to be a spreadsheet*). That
second one — states — is where it beats the rivals, as section 4 shows.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** The plain past simple (*I did*) tells you
something happened, but says **nothing** about whether it was a one-off or a habit,
or whether it's still true. *used to* carries one specific extra meaning: **this
was true regularly/continuously in the past, and it isn't anymore**. The contrast
with *now* is baked in. When you say *"AI decisions used to land on my desk"*, the
listener already knows they **don't** anymore — you didn't have to add it.

**Decision rule vs the rival structures.** There are two different rivals, and
mixing them up is the classic Brazilian trap.

**Rival 1 — used to do vs the past simple (I did).** Both are past. The difference
is *repetition + discontinuation*.

| Meaning | ✅ Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A **single** finished event | **past simple**: *We **approved** the pilot in March.* | one occasion, a point in time |
| A **repeated** past habit, now over | **used to**: *We **used to approve** each pilot one by one.* | many times, and no longer the case |
| A **past state**, now over | **used to**: *The portfolio **used to be** a spreadsheet.* | *be* is a state → *used to be*, not "was being" |

Decision rule: **one time → past simple; a habit or state that has since changed →
used to.** If you can naturally add *"…but not anymore"*, *used to* fits.

**Rival 2 — used to do vs be used to doing (the dangerous one).** These *look*
alike and mean **completely different** things:
- **used to do** = a past habit that stopped. *I **used to** work from home.* (I
  don't now.)
- **be used to doing** = to be accustomed to something. *I **am used to** working
  from home.* (It's normal for me — present, ongoing.)

The tells: *used to* + **base verb** and always past; *be used to* + **-ing (or a
noun)** and can be any tense. *I'm used to long board meetings* ≠ *I used to have
long board meetings*.

**PT→EN mapping.** *used to do* maps cleanly onto the **imperfeito de hábito**:
*costumava fazer / fazia habitualmente*. *"Eu costumava aprovar cada piloto"* =
*"I used to approve each pilot."* So far, friendly. Two places it betrays you:
1. **The present-habit trap (the #1 Brazilian error).** *used to* has **no present
   form**. For a *current* habit, English uses the **present simple**, often with
   *usually*: *"Eu costumo trabalhar de casa"* → *"I **usually** work from home"*,
   **never** *"I use to work from home."* Brazilians say *"I use to…"* for present
   habits constantly — it is wrong in every case.
2. ***be used to* = "estar acostumado".** *"Estou acostumado a reuniões longas"* →
   *"I'm **used to** long meetings"*, not *"I used to…"*.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *I **use to** run the AI review every Monday.* (meaning: I do it now) → ✅ *I
  **usually** run the AI review every Monday.* — *for a **present** habit use the
  present simple;* used to *is past-only.*
- ❌ *Did the board **used to** meet weekly?* → ✅ *Did the board **use to** meet
  weekly?* — *after* did *it drops the* -d *→* use to.
- ❌ *I **used to working** with a small team* / *I'm **use to** long meetings* → ✅
  *I'm **used to working** with a small team.* — ***be** used to +* -ing *means
  "accustomed"; don't fuse it with the past-habit* used to.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **AI decisions used to land on my desk; now the framework handles most of them.**
   *Past habit, explicitly no longer true — the* now *clause is the contrast* used to *sets up.*
2. **The innovation portfolio used to be a spreadsheet nobody trusted.**
   *A past **state** (*be*) that has changed;* used to be *is the natural form — "was being" would be wrong.*
3. **We used to approve each pilot one by one, but the governance layer replaced that.**
   *Repeated past habit, now discontinued — many occasions, so* used to*, not the single-event past simple.*
4. **There didn't use to be a clear budget owner for AI.**
   *Negative → **didn't use to** (base, no* -d*); describes a past absence that has since changed.*
5. **Did the CFO use to sit in on the technology reviews?**
   *Question → **did … use to** (no* -d*); asking about a former habit that may have stopped.*
6. **I approved the vendor contract last quarter.**
   *A **single** finished event → **past simple**, not* used to*; it happened once, with no "habit" or "not anymore" meaning.*
7. **I'm used to defending my roadmap in front of a skeptical board.**
   ***be** used to +* -ing *= accustomed (present, ongoing) — a different structure from the past-habit* used to*.*
8. **We used to lose whole afternoons to email; these days I usually clear it in twenty minutes.**
   *Everyday contrast: past habit (**used to lose**) vs current habit (**usually clear**) — exactly the past-only vs present-simple split.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Ask your tutor to run a **"then vs now" contrast drill** anchored in your real
   work: they name a topic — *the approval process, the data team, board reporting,
   your Friday schedule* — and you produce two sentences, *"We used to…"* (the old
   way) and *"Now we…"* (present simple). This trains the exact meaning *used to*
   carries and forces you off the present-habit trap.
2. Then a **minimal-pair speaking drill** on the dangerous rival: your tutor throws
   *"used to do"* vs *"be used to doing"* prompts about your routine (*"presenting
   to the CFO", "long strategy offsites", "working across time zones"*) and you say
   whether the meaning is *a habit that stopped* or *something you're accustomed
   to*, using the right structure out loud.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 024)**
1. **taken** — *"The board has **taken** a final position."* The word **has**
   forces the **participle**; *took* is the bare past simple and can't follow a
   perfect helper.
2. ❌ *was wrote* → ✅ *"The strategy memo **was written** before the offsite."* —
   the passive is *be* + **participle** (*written*); *wrote* is the past simple and
   can't follow *was*.
3. *buy* → **bought / bought**; *choose* → **chose / chosen**. The tempting wrong
   *-ed* is *"buyed"* (and *"choosed"*) — both are irregular, so **never** add *-ed*.

**Spaced review**
- **(Unit 018)** **been** — *"I've **been** leading the AI transformation **for**
  eight months."* The action started in the past and **continues now**, so English
  uses the present perfect continuous (*have been + -ing*), **not** the present
  tense; Portuguese *"faz oito meses que lidero…"* uses a present verb, which
  English refuses for something running from the past up to now.

**Recognition drill**
1. **I usually** — *"**I usually** chair the weekly AI steering meeting."* The
   meaning is a **present** routine, and *used to* has **no present form**; for a
   current habit English uses the present simple (often with *usually*). *"I use
   to…"* is the classic interference error.
2. ❌ *Did the board **used to** review…* → ✅ *"**Did** the board **use to** review
   every pilot separately?"* — after *did*, the *-d* drops: **use to**, not *used
   to*.
3. **am used to** — *"…I **am used to** presenting to the board."* *be used to +
   -ing* = **accustomed** (present, ongoing). *"I used to present"* would mean a
   past habit that **stopped** — the opposite of what a year of experience implies.
4. **approved** — *"We **approved** the AI budget last March."* A **single**
   finished event on one occasion → **past simple**; *used to* would wrongly signal
   a repeated habit that's no longer true.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** *used to + verbo na base* descreve um **hábito ou
estado no passado que não é mais verdade hoje** — o contraste com o *agora* já vem
embutido. Mapeia direto no **imperfeito de hábito** do português: *"eu costumava
aprovar cada piloto"* = *"I used to approve each pilot."* Serve tanto para ações
repetidas (*used to spend*) quanto para estados (*used to be*). Use *used to*
quando puder acrescentar naturalmente *"…mas não mais"*; se foi um evento **único**,
use o **passado simples** (*We approved the budget last March*). Atenção à forma:
no positivo é *used to*; na negativa e na pergunta o *-d* cai — *didn't use to*,
*did you use to*.

**As armadilhas.** Duas, e são as que mais pegam brasileiro. (1) *used to* **não
tem presente**: para um hábito **atual** use o presente simples com *usually* —
*"eu costumo trabalhar de casa"* → *"I **usually** work from home"*, **nunca** *"I
use to work…"*. Esse *"I use to"* para o presente está sempre errado. (2) Não
confunda *used to do* (hábito passado que acabou) com *be used to doing* (estar
**acostumado** a algo — presente, com *-ing*): *"estou acostumado a reuniões
longas"* → *"I'm **used to** long meetings"*, não *"I used to…"*. Se a diferença
entre hábito passado e evento único embolar, releia `lessons/2026-08-01-unit-020.md`
(present perfect vs past simple).
