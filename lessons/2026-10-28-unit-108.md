### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** How did the pilot actually perform?
> **Levi:** The results are **fairly strong** — not spectacular, but solid enough to continue.
> **CFO:** And the cost side?
> **Levi:** That's where it gets **rather expensive**. The infra bill came in higher than we modelled.
> **CEO:** High enough to kill it?
> **Levi:** No — I'm **quite sure** the unit economics still work. It's a **pretty promising** bet. I'd just call it **quite a risky** one until we see Q3.

Notice the grading. *fairly strong* = good, but deliberately modest. *rather expensive* carries a small frown — more than I'd like. *pretty promising* is warm but casual. And watch *quite* do two completely different jobs: *quite a risky bet* = **moderately** risky (partial), but *quite sure* = **completely** sure (total). Same word, opposite strength — the whole lesson turns on that split. One more thing: *quite **a** risky one* — *quite* jumps **in front of** the article, never *"a quite risky one."*

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

These are **degree adverbs** — they sit before an adjective or adverb and say *how much*, landing **below** *very* (except non-gradable *quite*, below). Think of a ladder:

> a little → **fairly** → **quite** / **pretty** → **rather** → **very** → **too**

| Modifier | Strength | Tone | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **fairly** | moderate, clearly below *very* | neutral, often a touch modest / disappointing | *fairly strong · fairly quickly* |
| **quite** (+ gradable adj) | moderate — more than *fairly*, less than *very* | neutral | *quite good · quite risky* |
| **pretty** | ≈ *quite* / *fairly* | informal, spoken only — warm | *pretty promising · pretty sure* |
| **rather** | ≈ *quite* in strength | **critical / negative**, or **surprised** | *rather expensive · rather good (= surprisingly)* |
| **very** | high | neutral, strong | *very strong* |

**Word order with *a / an*:**

| Modifier | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **fairly / pretty** | normal — after the article | *a **fairly** good plan · a **pretty** bold move* |
| **quite / rather** | **jump before** the article | ***quite** a good plan · **rather** a mess* |

Never *"a quite good plan."* It's either *"a fairly good plan"* or *"quite a good plan."*

**The one that splits — *quite*:** its meaning flips depending on the adjective.

| *quite* + … | Means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| a **gradable** adjective (*good, big, cold, risky, slow*) | **fairly / moderately** (partial) | *quite good · quite risky · quite cold* |
| a **non-gradable / absolute** adjective (*sure, certain, right, true, impossible, perfect, finished, amazing*) | **completely / absolutely** (total) | *quite sure · quite right · quite impossible · quite perfect* |

Absolute adjectives are already "100%" by nature — you can't be *more* sure than *sure*. So *quite* with them means "all the way," not "moderately."

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these exist?** English has *very* (strong) and *too* (excess, a problem) — but a leader spends most of the day in the **middle**: calibrating a claim, hedging a forecast, softening a verdict. *fairly / quite / rather / pretty* are the dials for that middle ground. They let you say "good, but I'm not overselling it" without committing to *very*. For an executive, that precision is the message: *"fairly confident"* and *"very confident"* authorise very different decisions.

**Decision rule — which dial?**

| You want to… | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| sound **modest / measured**, below *very* | **fairly** | signals "decent, not remarkable" — even mildly underwhelmed |
| say "more than fairly, solidly so" (neutral) | **quite** (gradable) | the calm middle — stronger than *fairly*, softer than *very* |
| the same, but **informally** (speech, chat) | **pretty** | warm and casual — keep it out of formal memos |
| flag that it's **more than you'd like**, or a **surprise** | **rather** | carries judgement: *rather expensive* = too pricey for my taste |
| give **genuine, strong praise** | **very / really** | *fairly / quite* would *undersell* it |

Minimal pairs — same adjective, different message:
- *"The plan is **fairly** good"* (faint — "it'll do") ≠ *"The plan is **very** good"* (real praise). In British English, even *"quite good"* is a mild, almost cautious compliment — never your word for a plan you love.
- *"The quote is **rather** high"* (I'm unhappy with it) ≠ *"The quote is **fairly** high"* (just a neutral observation).
- *"I'm **quite sure**"* (100% — absolute adjective) ≠ *"I'm **fairly sure**"* (maybe 70%). Here *quite* and *fairly* are **not** close — because *sure* is non-gradable, *quite* means *completely*.

**PT→EN mapping:**
- *bastante* → **quite / pretty / fairly** depending on strength (*bastante bom* = *quite good / pretty good*). ⚠️ *bastante* also means "a lot / enough" — don't let it pull you to *enough*.
- *razoavelmente* → **fairly** (*razoavelmente sólido* = *fairly solid*).
- *meio / um tanto* → **rather / a bit** (*meio arriscado* = *rather risky / a bit risky*) — note the slight negativity matches *rather*.
- The trap with **no clean Portuguese twin:** the *quite*-split. Portuguese *bastante certo* doesn't warn you that *quite certain* means **absolutely** certain while *quite good* means only **moderately** good. You have to learn it by the adjective type.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"It was **a quite risky** decision."* → ✅ *"It was **quite a risky** decision"* (or *"a **fairly** risky decision"*). *quite / rather* go **before** the article, never between *a* and the adjective.
2. ❌ Hearing *"quite good"* as strong praise and using it to compliment something you love → ✅ use ***very / really* good**. In British English *quite good* is modest, even faintly damning — the opposite of what you intend.
3. ❌ *"I'm **very sure** / the deadline is **very impossible**."* → ✅ *"I'm **quite sure** / **absolutely** sure"*, *"the deadline is **quite impossible**."* Absolute adjectives (*sure, impossible, perfect, right*) don't take *very* — reach for *quite / absolutely / completely*.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. The early numbers are **fairly encouraging**, though I wouldn't call it a breakthrough yet.
   *— fairly = moderate and deliberately modest; it sits below "very" and signals "decent, not remarkable."*
2. Rolling AI out firm-wide this year would be **quite ambitious** — doable, but a stretch.
   *— quite + gradable (ambitious) = moderately; stronger than "fairly," softer than "too." Neutral tone.*
3. I'm **quite certain** the board will back the raise.
   *— quite + non-gradable (certain) = completely. The split's second face: here quite means 100%, not "moderately."*
4. The vendor's quote came in **rather high** for what they're offering.
   *— rather = moderately high with a frown; "rather" tells the listener you're not pleased, where "fairly" would stay neutral.*
5. It's **quite a bold bet**, but the downside is capped.
   *— quite jumps before the article: "quite a bold bet," never "a quite bold bet." Gradable → moderate strength.*
6. The CFO's pushback was **pretty sharp**, so we softened the ask.
   *— pretty (informal) = quite; natural in speech, but keep it out of a written board memo — use "quite/fairly" there.*
7. You're **quite right** — we should pause before scaling.
   *— quite + non-gradable (right) = absolutely right. You're agreeing fully; "fairly right" would sound like a half-agreement.*
8. The restaurant was **rather good**, actually — better than the reviews suggested.
   *— everyday life: "rather good" carries pleasant surprise. That surprise is exactly what separates it from a flat "quite good."*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 107 — *too* / *enough*):**
1. **very** — *"The 2027 vision is **very** ambitious — exactly the stretch we need."* *too* means "so ambitious it will fail," which would flatly contradict the praise that follows. *too* always hides a "…and that's a problem."
2. *"aren't **prepared enough**"* — *enough* goes **after** the adjective (*prepared enough*), though **before** a noun (*enough people*). *"enough prepared"* breaks that rule.
3. *"There's **too much** risk (uncountable noun), **too many** dependencies (countable noun), and the whole thing is **too** fragile (adjective)."* Noun → *too much / too many*; adjective → plain *too*.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 101:** *"the fund **we invested in**"* — in natural spoken English the preposition drops to the **end** and the pronoun (*which / that*) **disappears**. The formal *"in which we invested"* is correct but sounds like a contract in a live meeting.
- **Unit 079:** **good** — *"The new roadmap sounds **good**."* *sound* is a linking verb describing the roadmap, so it needs an **adjective**. *well* would mean "in good health," which makes no sense for a roadmap (the *soa bem* → *sounds well* trap).

**Recognition drill:**
1. **"quite impossible"** — *quite* means *completely* there, because *impossible* is a **non-gradable / absolute** adjective (you can't be "a bit impossible"). *"quite good"* uses a **gradable** adjective, so *quite* means only *moderately*. The adjective type is the whole decision.
2. *"It was **quite a risky** decision"* **or** *"It was **a fairly risky** decision."* *quite* must jump **before** the article (*quite a risky*); you can't place it between *a* and the adjective. *"a quite risky"* is wrong.
3. **very** — *"The plan is **very** strong."* *fairly strong* sounds modest/underwhelmed, and British *quite strong* is a mild, cautious compliment — both **undersell** a plan you want to champion. Genuine strong praise needs *very* (or *really*).
4. **rather** adds a note of **disapproval / mild complaint** — "longer than it should have." *fairly long* would be a flat, neutral measurement; *rather long* tells the listener you're not happy about it.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra:** *fairly, quite, rather, pretty* são advérbios de grau que ficam **abaixo** de *very* e servem para você calibrar o meio-termo — nem fraco, nem forte demais. *fairly* = moderado e modesto (*fairly good* = bom, mas sem empolgação); *quite* (com adjetivo gradável) = um pouco mais que *fairly*, neutro (*quite risky*); *pretty* = igual a *quite/fairly*, mas **só na fala**, informal; *rather* = mesma força, porém com um tom de **crítica ou surpresa** (*rather expensive* = caro mais do que eu gostaria). Ordem com artigo: *fairly/pretty* ficam depois do *a* (*a fairly good plan*), mas *quite/rather* pulam para **antes** do artigo (*quite a good plan*) — nunca *"a quite good plan."*

**As armadilhas (interferência do português):** a maior é o **split do *quite***. Com adjetivo comum (gradável), *quite* = "moderadamente" (*quite good* = razoavelmente bom); mas com adjetivo **absoluto** (*sure, right, impossible, perfect, certain*), *quite* = "completamente" (*quite sure* = absolutamente certo). O português *bastante* não avisa essa diferença. Além disso: no inglês britânico, *quite good* é um elogio **fraco** — para elogiar de verdade use *very / really*, não *quite*. E nunca ponha *very* antes de um absoluto (*~~very sure~~, ~~very perfect~~*) — o certo é *quite sure* ou *absolutely sure*. Mapeamento rápido: *bastante* → *quite/pretty*; *razoavelmente* → *fairly*; *meio / um tanto* → *rather*.
