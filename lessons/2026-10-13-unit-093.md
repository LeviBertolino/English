### 1. 📘 Unit 093 — time conjunctions (+ present, not *will*)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** When do we take the AI investment to the board?
> **You:** We'll move **as soon as the board approves** the budget — not before.
> **CFO:** And the vendor? Have you picked one?
> **You:** I'll bring you the numbers **when I have** them, not before. And **before we commit**, let's run a ninety-day pilot.
> **CEO:** What about timing on the market?
> **You:** **While the market is still forming**, we should move fast. **Once the window closes**, the cost of entry triples.
> **CEO:** Good. Keep me posted.

Read the verbs after *as soon as*, *when*, *before*, *while*, *once*. Every one of them is **present tense** — *approves, have, commit, is forming, closes* — even though the whole conversation is about the **future**. The *will* lives only in the **other** half of the sentence (*we'll move*, *we should*). That split is the entire lesson: in English, the "time" half stays in the present and the "result" half carries the future.

In Portuguese you'd reach for the future subjunctive here — *"assim que o board **aprovar**", "quando eu **tiver**", "antes de **nos comprometermos**"*. That verb form does not exist in English, and the trap is to translate it as *will*. It isn't *will* — it's the plain present.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

When a sentence has two halves joined by a **time word**, and you're talking about the **future**:

| Half of the sentence | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Time clause** (*when / after / before / while / until / as soon as / once*) | **present** (never *will*) | *when the budget **is** approved* |
| **Main clause** (the result) | *will* / *going to* / imperative | *we **will** move* |

- **One sentence, two halves:** *We'll start **as soon as** the data **arrives**.*
- **Order is free, comma is not:** if the time clause comes **first**, put a comma; if it comes **second**, no comma.
  - *When the budget clears, we'll hire.* (comma)
  - *We'll hire when the budget clears.* (no comma)
- **Completion → present perfect:** use *have + -ed* in the time clause to stress that the action is **finished** before the next one starts.
  - *We'll move to rollout when we **have finished** the pilot.* (= pilot fully done first)
- **The conjunctions and what they mean:**

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *when / once / as soon as* | at the point that (*as soon as* = the very moment) |
| *before / after* | sequence in time |
| *while* | during, at the same time (two things ongoing) |
| *until* | up to that point, then it stops |

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (the core)

**Why does this structure exist?** English keeps the future marker (*will*) in **one place only** — the half that reports the result. The time half just names *when*, so it stays neutral and present. Doubling *will* into both halves (*"when the board will approve, we will move"*) sounds, to a native ear, like a child learning the language. One *will*, in the result half, is the mark of a fluent executive.

**Decision rule vs the rival structure(s):**

- **Present vs *will* inside the time clause.** This is *the* rival, and it's not a style choice — the *will* version is simply wrong.
  - ✅ *I'll present it **when the board is ready**.*
  - ❌ *I'll present it **when the board will be ready**.*
  - The test: is there a **time word** (*when, after, before, while, until, as soon as, once*) in front of the verb, and are you talking about the future? → that verb goes **present**.

- ***while* (+ clause) vs *during* (+ noun).** Both are *durante* in Portuguese, but English splits them by what follows.
  - ✅ *We raised the risk **while we were presenting**.* (a verb clause → *while*)
  - ✅ *We raised the risk **during the presentation**.* (a noun → *during*)
  - ❌ *~~During we were presenting~~* / ❌ *~~While the presentation~~.*

- ***until* vs *by*.** *Until* = continuously up to a point; *by* = at or before a deadline (a single moment).
  - ✅ *We'll hold the decision **until** Q3 results land.* (we keep holding the whole time)
  - ✅ *Send me the deck **by** Friday.* (deadline, one point — not *until Friday*)

**PT→EN mapping.** The danger is the **future subjunctive**, which Portuguese uses after these words and English does not have:

| Portuguese | ❌ Literal trap | ✅ English |
|---|---|---|
| *quando eu **tiver** os números* | *when I **will have** the numbers* | *when I **have** the numbers* |
| *assim que o board **aprovar*** | *as soon as the board **will approve*** | *as soon as the board **approves*** |
| *até que o CFO **responda*** | *until the CFO **will reply*** | *until the CFO **replies*** |

The *-ar/-er* subjunctive ending (*tiver, aprovar, responda*) feels like the future, so the hand reaches for *will*. Override it: after a time word, think **present**.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *As soon as we **will have** the data, I'll brief the CEO.* → ✅ *As soon as we **have** the data, I'll brief the CEO.*
2. ❌ *I'll wait **until** you **will finish**.* → ✅ *I'll wait **until** you **finish**.*
3. ❌ ***During** we discuss the roadmap, let's park the budget question.* → ✅ ***While** we discuss the roadmap…* (a clause follows → *while*, not *during*).

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **I'll share the AI roadmap with the board as soon as the budget is approved.**
   *Future result (*I'll share*) + time clause in the present (*is approved*) — never *will be approved*.*
2. **We'll hold the vendor decision until we've seen Q3 results.**
   *Present perfect (*we've seen*) stresses the results are fully in before we decide; *until* = we keep holding the whole time.*
3. **When the CEO asks about ROI, keep the answer to one number.**
   *Present (*asks*) for a future/whenever situation; the result half is an imperative (*keep*), which is fine in place of *will*.*
4. **Before we commit to the platform, let's run a ninety-day pilot.**
   *Time clause first → comma after it; *before we commit* stays present, not *will commit*.*
5. **While the market is still forming, we should move fast.**
   ***While** + a clause (two things ongoing at once); *during* would be wrong here because a verb, not a noun, follows.*
6. **Once we have board sign-off, the rest is execution.**
   ***Once** = the moment that; same present-tense rule as *when / as soon as*, slightly more emphatic on "the instant it happens".*
7. **Call me when you get home.**
   *Everyday: future meaning, present verb (*get*) after *when* — the exact pattern as the executive cases.*
8. **I'll start cooking after the kids are in bed.**
   *Everyday: *after* + present (*are*), future result (*I'll start*) — one *will*, in the result half only.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- **Kill the double-*will*.** Ask your tutor to throw contingency questions at you fast — *"When will you tell the board? What happens once the pilot ends? What do you do until the budget clears?"* — and answer in full sentences. Your tutor's only job is to buzz the instant a *will* sneaks into the time half (*"when the board **will**…"*). Drill until the present tense after *when / as soon as / until* feels automatic.
- **Role-play a timeline for the 2027 AI vision.** Lay out the sequence aloud — *"Before we commit, we'll pilot. As soon as the pilot delivers, we'll present to the board. Once they approve, we scale. We won't announce anything until the deal closes."* This strings five time conjunctions together in one breath, which is exactly how these clauses show up in a real strategy discussion.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Review Week 013):**
1. **as … than … the** — level → *as … **as***; one beats the other → *-er/more … **than***; the whole group → ***the** …-est / the most*. (Two things use *as*/*than*; the whole field uses *the*.)
2. **"Send them to the CFO." → "Send them to her."** — once the *thing* becomes a pronoun (*them*) it jumps to the front, and the person moves behind *to*.
3. **no longer** — the situation **changed** (contract dropped last quarter); *still* would mean it continues. The choice is continuation vs. change, not the Portuguese word (*ainda / não mais*).

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 086:** **the same *as*** — *the same* is joined by **as**, and a **noun** follows (*the same **budget** as*). The wrong reaches are *the same **than*** and *the same **like***.
- **Unit 063:** **"___ AI"** takes **no article** — general concepts (*AI, innovation*) drop the article in English, where Portuguese keeps it (*a IA*). *Our industry* already has *our*, so no *the* there either.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **delivers** — *once* is a time word → present tense, even though the vote is future. *will deliver* is the classic double-*will* error.
2. **During … while** — *the offsite* is a **noun** → *during*; *we were debating it* is a **clause** → *while*.
3. **"As soon as we close the round…"** — drop *will* inside the time clause; the *will* belongs only in *we'll scale*.
4. **Yes — a comma after "clears": *"When the budget clears, we will hire."*** The time clause comes **first**, so it needs a comma; if it came second (*"We will hire when the budget clears"*) there'd be none.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar:** quando você junta duas metades de uma frase com uma **palavra de tempo** — *when, after, before, while, until, as soon as, once* — e está falando do **futuro**, a metade do tempo fica no **presente**, e só a outra metade (o resultado) leva *will*: *"We'll move **as soon as** the board **approves** the budget."* Um *will* só, do lado do resultado. Se a frase de tempo vem primeiro, use vírgula (*"When the budget clears, we'll hire"*); se vem depois, sem vírgula. Para enfatizar que a ação terminou antes da próxima, use o present perfect na metade do tempo (*"when we **have finished** the pilot"*).

**As armadilhas:** a maior é o **futuro do subjuntivo** português (*quando eu **tiver**, assim que **aprovar**, até que **responda***), que **não existe** em inglês e faz a mão buscar *will* — mas o certo é o **presente simples** (*when I **have**, as soon as it **approves**, until he **replies***). Nunca ponha *will* depois de *when / until / as soon as* para o futuro. Segundo: *durante* se divide em dois — ***while*** antes de uma oração com verbo (*while we were presenting*) e ***during*** antes de um substantivo (*during the presentation*). Terceiro: *until* é "continuamente até um ponto"; para um prazo único use *by* (*send it **by** Friday*, não *until Friday*).
