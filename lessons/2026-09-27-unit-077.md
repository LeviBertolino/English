### 1. 📘 Unit 077 — -ing vs -ed adjectives

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** How did the board take the AI roadmap?
> **You:** They were genuinely **excited** — the growth numbers are **exciting**, and nobody expected
> them.
> **CEO:** Anyone unconvinced?
> **You:** The CFO was a bit **worried**. The funding gap is **worrying**, I'll admit.
> **CEO:** And you? You look **tired**.
> **You:** Long week. The negotiation was **exhausting** — but I'm not **bored** by any of it. This is
> the most **interesting** bet we've made in years, and I'm fully **interested** in leading it.

Look at the pairs. **Exciting / excited**, **worrying / worried**, **exhausting / tired**, **interesting
/ interested** — same root, two endings, and they are *not* interchangeable. The **-ing** word describes
the **thing that causes the feeling**: the numbers are *exciting*, the gap is *worrying*, the week is
*exhausting*. The **-ed** word describes the **person who has the feeling**: the board is *excited*,
the CFO is *worried*, you are *tired*. Say *"the board is exciting"* and you've just told the CEO the
board is a thrilling spectacle — not that they're keen.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Most feeling-verbs (*interest, excite, bore, worry, surprise, tire, confuse, disappoint*) give you
**two adjectives**:

| Ending | Describes | Points at | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **-ing** | what **causes** the feeling | a thing, situation, person-as-cause | *an **exciting** plan · the news is **worrying*** |
| **-ed** | how someone **feels** | a person (or group) receiving the feeling | *the board is **excited** · I'm **worried*** |

**Key facts:**
- **This is the same -ing / -ed logic as yesterday.** -ing = **active / the source** (the thing *does*
  something to you); -ed = **passive / the receiver** (you are *affected*). *The results **excite** the
  board* → the results are **exciting** (-ing, the doer) and the board is **excited** (-ed, the receiver).
- **A thing is almost always -ing; a person can be either** — depending on whether you mean their effect
  on others or their own feeling: *"She's **boring**"* (she bores people) vs *"She's **bored**"* (she
  feels bored). Both grammatical, opposite meanings.
- **-ed adjectives take a preposition + often a cause:** *interested **in**, worried **about**, excited
  **about**, surprised **at/by**, bored **with/by**, disappointed **with/in**.* The preposition is fixed,
  not free choice.
- **Common pairs to own:** interesting/interested · exciting/excited · boring/bored · tiring/tired ·
  worrying/worried · surprising/surprised · confusing/confused · disappointing/disappointed ·
  exhausting/exhausted · frustrating/frustrated · encouraging/encouraged.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (a seção central)

**Why do both forms exist?** Because English splits the **cause** of an emotion from the **experience**
of it, and forces you to say which one you mean every time. One ending points outward (the presentation
was *boring* — blame the presentation); the other points inward (I was *bored* — that's my state). No
single word can do both jobs, so the choice is not decoration — it's who you're talking about.

**Decision rule — one question: am I describing the THING (its effect) or the PERSON (their feeling)?**

| I'm describing… | Ending | Example | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| the **thing / situation** and its effect on people | **-ing** | *The Q3 numbers are **disappointing**.* | *the numbers cause the feeling* |
| the **person** and the feeling they have | **-ed** | *The board was **disappointed**.* | *the board has the feeling* |

Minimal pair — swap the ending, swap who you mean:
- *"The CFO is **frustrating**."* → the CFO **causes** frustration in others (he's the problem).
- *"The CFO is **frustrated**."* → the CFO **feels** frustration (something is bothering him).
Two very different things to report to your CEO. Pick the wrong ending and you insult the wrong person.

**PT→EN mapping — where this helps Levi and where it trips him.**
- Portuguese usually keeps the same split, which mostly protects you: **-ing** ≈ *-ante / -ivo / -dor*
  (*interess**ante**, cansat**ivo**, anima**dor***) and **-ed** ≈ *-ado / -ido* (*interess**ado**,
  cans**ado**, entedi**ado**, surpres**o***). Trust that: *"resultado empolg**ante**"* → *exciting*,
  *"estou empolg**ado**"* → *excited*.
- **The trap is where Portuguese uses ONE word for both.** *"Confuso"* covers both: *"estou confuso"*
  (I feel confused) **and** *"isso é confuso"* (that is confusing). English does **not** — ❌ *"I'm
  **confusing** about the roadmap"* → ✅ *"I'm **confused** about the roadmap."* Same slip with a person:
  say *confused* for your feeling, *confusing* only when you (or a doc) puzzle other people.
- **The famous one:** *"interessado"* → **interested** (a person's feeling), *"interessante"* →
  **interesting** (a thing's quality). ❌ *"I am very **interesting** in AI"* means *"I am a fascinating
  object"* — the opposite of humble. ✅ *"I am very **interested** in AI."*

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"I am very **interesting** in the partnership."* → ✅ *"I am very **interested** in the partnership."*
  *(you have the feeling → -ed; "-ing" would call yourself fascinating.)*
- ❌ *"The board was **surprising** with the payback numbers."* → ✅ *"The board was **surprised** by the
  numbers"* / *"The numbers were **surprising**."* *(the board receives the surprise → -ed; the numbers
  cause it → -ing.)*
- ❌ *"I'm **boring** in these long reviews."* → ✅ *"I'm **bored** in these long reviews."* *("I'm boring"
  = I bore other people; you mean you feel bored → -ed.)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"The growth numbers are **exciting**, and the board is **excited**."*
   *— the numbers cause it (-ing); the board feels it (-ed). The single sentence shows both halves of the rule.*
2. *"The funding gap is **worrying**; the CFO is **worried** about it."*
   *— gap = cause (-ing); CFO = feeler (-ed), with its fixed preposition "worried about."*
3. *"This is the most **interesting** bet we've made — and I'm fully **interested** in leading it."*
   *— the bet is interesting (its quality); Levi is interested in it (his stance) — never "I'm interesting."*
4. *"The Q3 results were **disappointing**, so the leadership team felt **disappointed**."*
   *— results cause the letdown (-ing); the team receives it (-ed).*
5. *"That vendor demo was **confusing** — half the room left **confused**."*
   *— the demo puzzles people (-ing); the people end up puzzled (-ed); the exact pair Portuguese "confuso" blurs.*
6. *"The all-day board prep was **exhausting**; by 6 p.m. we were all **exhausted**."*
   *— the prep drains you (-ing); you are drained (-ed).*
7. *"I was **surprised** by how fast the pilot scaled — the speed was genuinely **surprising**."*
   *— "I" receive the surprise (-ed, surprised BY); the speed causes it (-ing).*
8. *"The kids were **bored** on the long flight, so I downloaded a couple of **boring** documentaries to knock them out."*
   *— everyday life: the kids feel bored (-ed); the documentaries produce boredom (-ing).*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Ask your tutor to run a **cause-vs-feeler flip drill**: they give you a feeling-verb (*excite, worry,
   confuse, disappoint, surprise, frustrate*) and you must produce **both** adjectives in an executive
   sentence — the thing (*"the numbers are **surprising**"*) and the person (*"the board was **surprised**
   by them"*), adding the right preposition on the -ed one. This kills the *"I'm interesting"* reflex,
   which is the single most exposing version of this error.
2. Role-play a **stakeholder read-out to the CEO**: describe how three groups reacted to a decision
   (*the board, the CFO, your team*) using only -ed adjectives for their feelings (*excited, worried,
   disappointed, encouraged*) and -ing adjectives for the causes (*the exciting upside, the worrying
   gap*). Have the tutor stop you every time you attach a *-ing* adjective to a person or a *-ed* one to
   a thing — the goal is to hear the ending as automatic.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 076):**
1. **approved** — *"the budget **approved** by the board."* The deciding question: *does the noun DO the
   action or RECEIVE it?* A budget is approved (it receives the action) → passive → **-ed**. *"approving"*
   would mean the budget is doing the approving, which is nonsense.
2. **-ing fixes it:** *"The consultant **advising** the board is very expensive."* The wrong *advised*
   reads as a full past-tense verb (*"the consultant advised the board"* = a complete sentence), so the
   reader loses the thread. You mean the consultant who **gives** advice → active → **-ing**.
3. *"the roadmap **presented** on Monday."* The two words that disappear are **that + was** (*that was
   presented* → *presented*). Passive meaning, so the participle stays *presented* (-ed), not *-ing*.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 070:** **all week** / **every week** → *"They worked **all week** on it — not **every week**,
   this one."* *all week* = the **entire single week**, start to end. *every week* = **each week,
   repeatedly**. Same trap as *all day* vs *every day*.
- **Unit 047:** missing word **It** → *"**It's** important to align early on the AI bet."* English needs
   a subject, so use empty *it*. The slip comes from Portuguese *"É importante…"*, which drops the subject
   — English never does.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **excited** — *"The board was **excited** by the roadmap."* The deciding question: *thing or person?*
   The board is a **person/group with the feeling** → **-ed**. *"exciting"* would mean the board itself
   is a thrilling spectacle.
2. Wrong: *interesting* describes a **thing's quality**, so *"I'm interesting"* accidentally says **you
   are a fascinating object**. You mean you **have the feeling** → **-ed**: *"I'm very **interested** in
   the AI budget discussion"* (interested **in**).
3. *"My manager is **frustrating**"* = the manager **causes** frustration in others (the manager is the
   problem). *"My manager is **frustrated**"* = the manager **feels** frustration (something is bothering
   them). -ing points at the cause, -ed at the feeler.
4. **worried about** — *"The CFO is **worried about** the numbers."* Feeling → **-ed** (*worried*), and
   its fixed preposition is **about**.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Muitos verbos de emoção (*interest, excite, bore, worry, surprise, confuse*) geram **dois adjetivos**. O
de **-ing** descreve **o que causa** o sentimento — a coisa, a situação: *"the numbers are **exciting**"*
(os números empolgam). O de **-ed** descreve **quem sente** — a pessoa: *"the board is **excited**"* (a
diretoria está empolgada). É a mesma lógica de ontem: **-ing = ativo/fonte**, **-ed = passivo/quem
recebe**. A pergunta única: *estou falando da COISA (o efeito dela) ou da PESSOA (o sentimento dela)?*

As ciladas: (1) a mais famosa — *"interessado"* é **interested**, *"interessante"* é **interesting**;
❌ *"I am interesting in AI"* diz que **você** é fascinante, não que tem interesse → use **interested
in**. (2) onde o português usa **uma só palavra**: *"confuso"* serve para os dois em português, mas em
inglês *"I'm **confused**"* (eu sinto) ≠ *"it's **confusing**"* (aquilo confunde os outros) — nunca
*"I'm confusing"* para o seu sentimento. (3) os adjetivos -ed pedem **preposição fixa**: *interested
**in**, worried **about**, surprised **by**, excited **about**.* Se a base de -ing/-ed em orações
reduzidas ainda embolar, releia `lessons/2026-09-26-unit-076.md`.
