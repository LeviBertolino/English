### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** You're still recommending we scale the pilot — even after that infra bill?
> **Levi:** Yes. **Although the costs came in high**, the retention numbers justify it.
> **CFO:** The board was nervous, though.
> **Levi:** They were. But **despite their concerns**, they approved the first tranche. And **in spite of the delay**, we're still ahead of the market.
> **CEO:** So the thesis holds?
> **Levi:** It does. **Even though** two competitors launched first, we're the only one profitable on this line. We're the smaller player, **though** — so speed matters.

Every bold phrase does the same move: it **concedes** something that points one way, then pushes to a conclusion that points the *other* way. *Although the costs came in high* (bad) → *we should scale* (good anyway). Now watch the grammar split: **although / even though** are followed by a whole **clause** — a subject and a verb (*although the costs **came** in high* · *even though two competitors **launched**first*). But **despite / in spite of** are followed by a **noun** — no verb (*despite their **concerns*** · *in spite of the **delay***). Same meaning, two different shapes. And **though** at the very end (*the board was nervous, **though*** · *we're the smaller player, **though***) is a casual *"however"* tacked on in speech.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

All of these signal **contrast / concession**: *this is true, but here's what matters despite it.* What changes is **what comes after**:

| Linker | What follows it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **although** | a **clause** (subject + verb) | *Although **the pilot worked**, we paused it.* |
| **even though** | a **clause** — but stronger/more emphatic | *Even though **it worked**, we paused it.* |
| **though** (front) | a **clause** — informal *although* | *Though **it worked**, we paused it.* |
| **though** (end) | nothing — a trailing *however* | *It worked. We paused it, **though**.* |
| **in spite of** | a **noun / -ing / the fact that** | *In spite of **the strong results**, we paused.* |
| **despite** | a **noun / -ing / the fact that** (**no "of"**) | *Despite **the strong results**, we paused.* |

**Two shapes, concretely:**
- **Clause linkers** (*although, even though, though*) take a full mini-sentence: *although **we won** the deal* (subject *we* + verb *won*).
- **Noun linkers** (*despite, in spite of*) take a thing, not a sentence: *despite **the win*** · *despite **winning** the deal* (the *-ing* form turns the verb into a noun-like chunk) · *despite **the fact that** we won* (the escape hatch when you really need a clause).

**Position & punctuation:** either half can come first. Front position takes a comma: *Although it's risky, we'll do it.* / *Despite the risk, we'll do it.* Back position usually doesn't: *We'll do it although it's risky.* / *We'll do it despite the risk.* End-position **though** is set off by a comma: *It's risky, we'll do it **though**.*

**One rule to burn in:** *despite* has **no "of"** (❌ *despite of*); *in spite* **always** has it (✅ *in spite **of***).

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** *still / yet / already* placed an event on a **timeline**. These linkers do something only they can do — they let you **put two opposing facts in one breath and tell the listener which one wins**. A leader lives in this move: *the data is weak, but we still back the bet* · *the board hesitated, yet we got the tranche*. Concession is the grammar of persuasion — you show you've seen the counter-argument (*although the costs are high…*) and then override it (*…the retention justifies it*). It signals confidence and fairness at once. Get the shape wrong and a crisp, executive sentence collapses into a learner error in front of the C-level.

**Decision rule #1 — clause vs noun (THE split that decides everything):**

Ask one question: **is a verb coming, or just a thing?**

| | **although / even though / though** | **despite / in spite of** |
|---|---|---|
| followed by | a **clause** (subject + **verb**) | a **noun** or **-ing** (no verb of its own) |
| *the pilot succeeded* | *Although the pilot **succeeded**, …* ✅ | *Despite the pilot **succeeded**, …* ❌ |
| *the success* | *Although the success, …* ❌ | *Despite the **success**, …* ✅ |

Same idea, two machines. If you want to say a full sentence (*the pilot succeeded*), you **must** use *although*. If you want to name a thing (*the success / the delay / the costs*), you use *despite / in spite of*. You cannot mix the shapes.

Need a clause but prefer *despite*? Use the bridge **despite the fact that** / **despite** + **-ing**: *Despite **the fact that** the pilot succeeded, …* · *Despite **succeeding**, …*.

**Decision rule #2 — `although` vs `but` (one sentence vs two balanced halves):**
- *"**Although** we lost the deal, we learned a lot."* → one sentence; *although* **subordinates** the first fact (it's the concession, the minor point) and the main clause carries the weight.
- *"We lost the deal, **but** we learned a lot."* → two **balanced** halves joined by *but*; neither is subordinated.

They're not interchangeable in form: **use one, not both.** Portuguese lets you say *"embora tenhamos perdido, **mas** aprendemos"* — the *mas* is natural there. In English that doubles the marker and is wrong (see error #3).

**Decision rule #3 — `despite` vs `because of` (contrary-to-expectation vs cause):**
- *"We grew **despite** the downturn."* → we grew **against** what the downturn would predict (surprise).
- *"We grew **because of** the downturn."* → the downturn **caused** the growth (we sell cost-cutting tools).

Opposite logic on the same noun. *despite* = the thing worked **against** you and you won anyway; *because of* = the thing is the **reason**.

**PT→EN mapping (where translation betrays you):**
- *embora · ainda que · mesmo que* (+ verbo) → **although / even though** (+ clause): *"embora os custos tenham subido"* → *"**although** the costs went up."*
- *apesar de* (+ substantivo) → **despite / in spite of** (+ noun): *"apesar dos resultados"* → *"**despite** the results."*
- *apesar de* (+ verbo, no português) → NOT a clause after *despite*; use *although* **or** *despite + -ing / the fact that*: *"apesar de termos ganhado"* → *"**although** we won"* / *"**despite** winning"* / *"**despite the fact that** we won."*
- *apesar disso · mesmo assim* → **despite that · even so · still**: *"apesar disso, aprovaram"* → *"**even so**, they approved"* / *"they approved it **anyway**."*
- The two faces of *de*: Portuguese *apesar **de*** tricks you into *despite **of*** — but English *despite* takes **no** *of*; only *in spite **of*** does.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"**Despite of** the risk, we'll invest."* → ✅ *"**Despite** the risk, we'll invest"* **or** *"**In spite of** the risk, we'll invest."* *despite* never carries *of*; *in spite* always does. (The Portuguese *apesar **de*** is what plants the stray *of*.)
2. ❌ *"**Despite** the board approved the budget, finance is nervous."* (a clause after *despite*) → ✅ *"**Although** the board approved the budget, …"* **or** *"**Despite** the board's **approval**, …"* / *"**Despite** the fact that the board approved it, …"*. After *despite* comes a **noun**, never a bare subject + verb.
3. ❌ *"**Although** we hit the target, **but** margins were thin."* (double marker) → ✅ *"**Although** we hit the target, margins were thin"* **or** *"We hit the target, **but** margins were thin."* Portuguese pairs *embora … mas*; English picks **one**.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **Although the AI budget isn't approved yet, we've already briefed the delivery team.**
   *— `although` + clause (the budget **isn't** approved) concedes a blocker; the main clause overrides it. Note "yet" (end) and "already" (mid) carried over from Unit 114 — the concession lets both facts coexist.*
2. **Despite the strong pilot numbers, the board wants another quarter of data.**
   *— `despite` + noun phrase (the strong pilot **numbers** — no verb). Contrary-to-expectation: good numbers would predict a yes, yet they're cautious. Swap to "Although the pilot numbers were strong" if you want a verb.*
3. **In spite of the delay, we're still ahead of every competitor on this line.**
   *— `in spite of` + noun (the **delay**), the full "of" present. Equivalent to "despite the delay." The concession (delay = bad) sets up the win (still ahead).*
4. **Even though two competitors launched first, we're the only profitable player.**
   *— `even though` = emphatic `although`; the real, surprising contrast (they beat us to market, yet we win on economics). Clause after it (two competitors **launched**).*
5. **The board approved the first tranche despite the CFO's concerns.**
   *— back-position `despite` + noun (the CFO's **concerns**), no comma. "Despite the CFO was concerned" would be wrong — turn the clause into a noun ("the CFO's concerns") to keep `despite`.*
6. **We should scale it. The timing is tight, though.**
   *— end-position `though` = a casual "however" added in speech; set off by a comma. Natural in a meeting, too informal for a written board memo (use "however" there).*
7. **Although the restaurant had terrible reviews, the food was excellent.** *(everyday)*
   *— `although` + clause (the restaurant **had** terrible reviews). Concession (bad reviews) → surprise result (great food). "Despite the terrible reviews" would say the same with a noun.*
8. **We went for a long walk despite the rain.** *(everyday)*
   *— `despite` + noun (the **rain**), no "of". Contrary-to-expectation. "Because of the rain" would flip it to a reason — we'd have stayed in.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Run a **concession round** with your tutor: they name an obstacle (*the budget was cut · the pilot slipped a quarter · a competitor launched first · the CFO pushed back · adoption was slow*) and you must counter each one **twice** — once with a **clause linker** (*although / even though* + subject + verb) and once with a **noun linker** (*despite / in spite of* + noun or *-ing*). The rule to enforce out loud: if a verb follows, it's *although*; if a thing follows, it's *despite*. Ask them to buzz you the instant you say *"despite of"* or bolt a clause onto *despite*.
2. Practise a **90-second "defend the bet" pitch**: present an investment the data argues *against*, conceding the weak point up front and overriding it — *"Although the early ROI is thin, the strategic option value is the real prize…"*. Have the tutor play a skeptical CFO who keeps raising objections; you answer each with a fresh *although / despite* opener so the concession-then-override move becomes automatic under pressure.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 114 — *still / yet / already / no longer*):**
1. **still** (middle) + **yet** (end): *"We're **still** waiting on finance, and the board hasn't signed off **yet**."* *still* = the waiting **continues** (mid-position, after *are*); *yet* = expected but **not done**, at the **end** of the clause.
2. **yet** — *"Have you approved the forecast **yet**?"* A neutral question wants *yet*. *"…approved it **already**?"* would instead mean *"you've approved it so fast?!"* — surprise, not a plain status check.
3. **not … any more / no longer:** *"We don't use that vendor **any more**"* **or** *"We **no longer** use that vendor."* *não … mais* is never a bare *more*.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 108 (quite-split):** *"quite **certain**"* = **completely** certain — before a *non-gradable* adjective (certain/right/sure), *quite* means 100%. *"quite **a risky** bet"* = **moderately** risky — before a *gradable* adjective, *quite* means "fairly, middling." Same word, opposite strength; the adjective type decides.
- **Unit 087 (superlatives):** *"the biggest player **in** the market"* and *"the best call **in** the company's history."* A group/place (*the market*) takes **in**, and a span treated as a group (*the company's history*) also takes **in**, never *of* — *of* is the Portuguese *do mercado / da história* bleeding through.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **although** — *"**Although** the board approved the budget, finance is still cautious."* The part after it is a full clause (subject *the board* + verb *approved*), which only a clause linker allows. To make *despite* legal, turn the clause into a **noun**: *"**Despite** the board's **approval**, …"* (or *"Despite the fact that the board approved it, …"*).
2. **in spite **of** the vendor outage, despite a tight budget:** *"We launched on time **in spite of** the vendor outage, **despite** a tight budget."* *in spite* **needs** the *of*; *despite* **rejects** it.
3. **drop *but*:** *"**Even though** the results were strong, the board hesitated."* (or *"The results were strong, **but** the board hesitated."*) English uses **one** contrast marker, not both — *even though … but* is the Portuguese *embora … mas* leaking in.
4. **despite** — *"Revenue grew **despite** the recession…"*: it grew **against** what a recession predicts (surprise). *"**because of** the recession"* would flip the story — the recession would be the **cause** of the growth (which, for a cost-cutting product, is actually plausible — hence the meaning hinges entirely on the linker).

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra:** estes conectivos todos marcam **contraste / concessão** — *isto é verdade, mas o que importa é o contrário*. A escolha depende do que vem **depois**. **although / even though / though** (= *embora, ainda que, mesmo que*) pedem uma **oração completa** (sujeito + verbo): *"**although** the costs **went** up."* Já **despite / in spite of** (= *apesar de*) pedem um **substantivo** (ou *-ing*): *"**despite** the **cost increase**."* Mesmo sentido, duas formas — você não pode misturá-las. Precisa de verbo depois de *despite*? Use a ponte *despite **the fact that*** ou *despite + -ing*. E **though** no **fim** da frase é um *"porém"* informal da fala (*we're profitable, **though***).

**As armadilhas (interferência do português):** (1) *apesar **de*** faz você escrever *despite **of*** — mas *despite* **não** leva *of*; só *in spite **of*** leva. (2) Em português *apesar de* aceita verbo (*apesar de termos ganhado*), mas em inglês **não** se põe oração depois de *despite* — troque por *although we won* ou *despite winning / despite the fact that we won*. (3) O português casa *embora … mas*; o inglês usa **um** marcador só — *"although we won, **but** margins were thin"* está errado: escolha *although* **ou** *but*. Na dúvida entre *although* e *despite*, pergunte: vem um **verbo** depois (→ *although*) ou só uma **coisa** (→ *despite*)?
