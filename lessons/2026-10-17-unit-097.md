### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Remind me who's actually driving the AI roadmap day to day.
> **You:** Mariana — she's the leader **who turned our first pilot into revenue**. She's the one I trust with this.
> **CEO:** And the vendor issue?
> **You:** We dropped the vendor **that kept missing the security deadlines**. The contract **we signed last year** had no exit clause, so it cost us a quarter to unwind.
> **CFO:** What about the budget line the board flagged?
> **You:** That's the number **(that) we revised twice**. The version **the board rejected** assumed a bigger team than we have.
> **CEO:** So what's the single thing that unlocks Q1?
> **You:** A decision **which only the board can make** — the go/no-go on the platform spend.

Notice what the relative clauses are doing. Each one **points at a noun and adds exactly the detail that identifies it**: not *a leader*, but the leader *who turned the pilot into revenue*; not *the contract*, but the contract *we signed last year*. And look closely at two of them — *the contract **we signed*** and *the number **(that) we revised*** — there's no *who/that/which* before *we* at all. That's not an error; it's the rule you'll meet today.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

A relative clause is a mini-sentence glued **onto a noun** to say *which one* you mean. The glue is a relative pronoun:

| Pronoun | Points at | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | people | *the CFO **who** challenged the budget* |
| **which** | things / ideas | *a decision **which** only the board can make* |
| **that** | people **or** things | *the vendor **that** missed the deadline* · *the deck **that** we sent* |

**The one structural rule that matters most — subject vs object:**

- The relative pronoun is the **subject** of its clause (nothing else does the verb) → you **must keep** it:
  *the leader **who** drives change* — drop *who* and *drives* has no subject. ❌ *the leader drives change* means something else entirely.
- The relative pronoun is the **object** (there's already another subject doing the verb) → you **can drop** it:
  *the deal **(that) we** closed* — *we* is the subject, so *that* is optional. Both *the deal that we closed* and *the deal we closed* are correct.

A quick test: **look at the word right after the pronoun.** If it's a verb (*who **drives***, *that **missed***), the pronoun is the subject — **keep it**. If it's a new subject (*that **we** closed*, *which **the board** rejected*), it's the object — **you may drop it**.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?**
A relative clause lets you **identify a noun with precision in a single breath**, instead of two choppy sentences. Compare *"We dropped the vendor. The vendor kept missing deadlines."* with *"We dropped the vendor **that kept missing deadlines**."* The second tells the listener instantly *which* vendor and *why it matters* — the relative clause carries the identifying detail without a restart. For an executive this is the difference between *"a decision"* (vague) and *"a decision **which only the board can make**"* (precise, unarguable). It's the grammar of pointing.

**Decision rule #1 — *who* vs *which* vs *that*:**
This is the one Portuguese flattens, because Portuguese uses **one word — *que* — for all three**.

| Refers to | Use | Never use |
|---|---|---|
| a **person** | *who* or *that* | ❌ *which* |
| a **thing / idea** | *which* or *that* | — |
| **either**, in a defining clause | *that* (safe default) | — |

Side-by-side minimal pairs:
- *the CFO **who** challenged the budget* ✅ / *the CFO **which** challenged the budget* ❌ (people are never *which*)
- *the strategy **which** we chose* ✅ / *the strategy **that** we chose* ✅ (things take either)
- *the leader **that** drives change* ✅ (*that* works for people too — the universal safe choice)

**Decision rule #2 — keep the pronoun or drop it? (the core skill):**
Ask: **is there already a subject after the pronoun?**
- **No other subject → keep it (it's the subject):** *the roadmap **that unlocks** Q1.* (nothing else does *unlocks*)
- **Yes, another subject → you may drop it (it's the object):** *the roadmap **(that) the board approved**.* (*the board* does *approved*)

Dropping the object pronoun is what fluent, natural English does: *the deck we sent*, *the number we revised*, *the version the board rejected*. Keeping *that* isn't wrong, just slightly heavier.

**PT→EN mapping:**
Portuguese says everything with ***que*** (and *o qual / a qual* in formal writing), and — crucially — Portuguese **cannot drop it** and often **repeats the noun as a pronoun** inside the clause:
- *"a líder **que** transformou o piloto"* → *the leader **who** turned the pilot…* (person → *who*, not *which*)
- *"o contrato **que** assinamos"* → *the contract **(that) we signed*** — English **drops** the *que*; Portuguese keeps it.
- *"o cliente **que** falamos com **ele**"* / *"o relatório **que** enviei **ele** ontem"* → here Portuguese smuggles in an extra pronoun (*ele*). English **forbids** it: *the client **we spoke to*** (no *him*), *the report **I sent** yesterday* (no *it*).

Where it betrays you — **three traps**: the *que*→*which* reflex for people, the inability to drop the pronoun, and the extra *ele/it/him* copied from Portuguese.

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. **Double subject / resumptive pronoun** (the most common and most invisible):
   - ❌ *The vendor **that he** missed the deadline cost us a quarter.*
   - ✅ *The vendor **that** missed the deadline cost us a quarter.* (*that* **is** the subject — no second *he*)
   - ❌ *The report **that I sent it** yesterday had the final numbers.*
   - ✅ *The report **(that) I sent** yesterday had the final numbers.* (object dropped, no *it*)
2. ***which* for a person:**
   - ❌ *She's the leader **which** drives our AI strategy.*
   - ✅ *She's the leader **who** drives our AI strategy.* (people → *who / that*, never *which*)
3. ***what* used as a relative pronoun** (from *"o que"*):
   - ❌ *This is the strategy **what** we chose.*
   - ✅ *This is the strategy **(that / which)** we chose.* (*what* is never the glue onto a noun)

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **Mariana is the leader who turned our first AI pilot into revenue.**
   *Executive. *who* is the **subject** of *turned* — keep it, and use *who* (not *which*) because Mariana is a person.*
2. **We dropped the vendor that kept missing the security deadlines.**
   *Executive. *that* for a thing/company, as **subject** of *kept* → cannot be dropped. No second pronoun: not "that it kept".*
3. **The contract we signed last year had no exit clause.**
   *Executive. Object relative with no pronoun at all — *we* is the subject, so *(that)* is dropped. This is the natural, fluent shape.*
4. **A decision which only the board can make is holding up Q1.**
   *Executive. *which* for a thing, **object** of *make* (subject is *the board*) — keeping *which* is fine and sounds crisp here; you could also say "that only the board can make".*
5. **The version the board rejected assumed a bigger team than we have.**
   *Executive. Dropped object pronoun again: *the board* is the subject of *rejected*. Compare #1, where *who* can't go — there's no other subject.*
6. **Investors back founders who think three moves ahead.**
   *Executive. *who* = subject of *think* → mandatory. Drop it and "founders think three moves ahead" becomes a different sentence.*
7. **The restaurant we booked for the offsite cancelled on us.** *(everyday / work blend)*
   *Everyday. Object relative, pronoun dropped (*we* booked it). Not "the restaurant that we booked it" — no extra *it*.*
8. **That's the colleague who lent me her charger at the airport.** *(everyday)*
   *Everyday. Person + subject of *lent* → *who*, kept. The thing she lent (*her charger*) stays inside the clause; don't double it with "who she lent it".*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 096):**
1. ***"I **hope** we **win** it."*** The deal is still open, so *hope* + present. Using *wish* ("I wish we win") is doubly wrong — *wish* never takes the present tense, and *wish* about a **live** opportunity sounds **defeatist**, as if you'd already given up on it.
2. ***"I wish she **would stop** moving it."*** → *wish* + **would** + base verb. The tell is *"keeps pushing… every week"* — a **repeated behaviour** you want changed (you could add "…but she won't"). The plain past (*had*) is only for a **state**, not a behaviour.
3. ***were*** — *"I wish I **were** the one presenting…"*. After *wish*, the verb *be* takes **were for every subject** in careful/professional English (same rule as *If I were you*); *was* is informal spoken only.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 090:** First sentence: ❌ *already* → ✅ ***yet*** — *"Has the CFO signed off on the spend **yet**?"* A neutral status **question** takes *yet* at the end; *already* here would mean "signed off *so soon*?!". Second: *no longer* goes in the **middle**, before the plain verb → *"We **no longer invest** in that legacy platform."* (not after the verb).
- **Unit 068:** *"**Most** companies… but **most of** our business units…"*. Bare *most* + general plural noun (*most companies*) for the industry-wide claim; *most **of*** when the group is **specified** by a possessive/*the* (*our business units*) — the *of* is forced by that specific, localised group.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ❌ *The CFO **which**…* → ✅ *The CFO **who** (or that) challenged…*. A person is never *which* — use *who* (or *that*).
2. You can drop *that* in the **second** blank: *"The roadmap **(that) the board approved**…"* — the next word is a new subject (*the board*), so *that* is the **object** and droppable. In the first, *"The roadmap **that unlocks** Q1,"* the next word is a **verb** (*unlocks*), so *that* is the **subject** — **keep it**.
3. ❌ *…that I sent **it** to the board…* → ✅ *The report **(that) I sent** to the board yesterday…*. *I* is already the subject, so the pronoun is the object — drop *that* if you like, but **never** add *it* (the Portuguese resumptive-pronoun trap).
4. *"She's the investor **(who) we met** at the summit"* — object of *met* (subject *we*), so *who* is **droppable**. *"She's the investor **who backed** our seed round"* — *who* is the **subject** of *backed*, so it **must stay**. Both are people → *who* (not *which*) throughout.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

A oração relativa cola uma mini-frase a um substantivo para dizer **qual** você quer: não "a líder", mas a líder *who turned the pilot into revenue*. Use **who** para pessoas, **which** para coisas, e **that** para os dois (é o coringa seguro). A regra estrutural central é **sujeito × objeto**: olhe a palavra logo depois do pronome. Se vier um **verbo** (*who **drives***, *that **missed***), o pronome é o **sujeito** e **não pode sair**. Se vier um **novo sujeito** (*that **we** signed*, *which **the board** rejected*), o pronome é o **objeto** e **pode ser omitido** — e omitir (*the contract we signed*, *the version the board rejected*) é justamente o que soa natural e fluente em inglês.

Três armadilhas vêm direto do português, que usa só ***que*** para tudo. Primeira: **nunca repita o pronome dentro da oração** — português enfia um *ele/isso* (*"o relatório que eu enviei **ele**"*), mas inglês proíbe: *the report I sent* (sem *it*), *the client we spoke to* (sem *him*). Segunda: **pessoa nunca é *which*** — é *who* ou *that* (*the CFO **who** challenged the budget*). Terceira: **não use *what* como relativo** (do "o que") — ❌ *the strategy **what** we chose* → ✅ *the strategy **(that) we chose***. Treine sentir a frase sem o pronome como completa, não truncada.
