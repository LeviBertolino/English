### 1. 📘 Unit 094 — *if* + present (real conditions)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** So when does the AI program actually start?
> **You:** **If the board approves** the budget next week, **we'll kick off** in Q1.
> **CFO:** And if they don't?
> **You:** **If they push back**, **we'll scale** the pilot down and revisit in March. Either way we move.
> **CEO:** Good. And the vendor contract?
> **You:** **We'll sign** it **as soon as** legal clears it — but only **if the terms hold**. If the price **changes**, we renegotiate.

Yesterday you locked in *when / until / as soon as* for events you're **sure** will happen. Today one word opens a second door: **if**. Notice the shape — *if the board **approves*** (present, not *will approve*), then *we'**ll** kick off* (future in the other half). Same tense rule as yesterday, but a different **meaning**: *if* marks the event as **possible, not certain**. *When they approve* = they will, it's just a matter of timing. *If they approve* = maybe yes, maybe no.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

A real-condition sentence has **two halves**: the **if-part** (the condition) and the **result-part** (what follows). The tense split is fixed:

| Half | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **if** + condition | **present simple** (never *will*) | *If the board **approves** …* |
| result | **will / can / may / imperative** | *… we**'ll** move.* / *… we **can** scale.* / *… **tell** the CFO.* |

Either half can come first — the meaning is identical:

- *If the price changes, we renegotiate.*
- *We renegotiate if the price changes.*

**Comma rule:** if the *if*-part comes **first**, put a comma after it. If it comes **second**, no comma.

The one unbreakable rule: **no *will* in the *if*-part.** ✅ *If it **rains*** — ❌ *If it **will rain***.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?**
It lets you talk about a future that **depends on something** — a plan with a hinge in it. Business life runs on these hinges: *if the numbers hold, if the board signs off, if the pilot works*. *If* says "this outcome is real, but conditional" — it hasn't happened and it **might not**.

**Decision rule vs the rival — *if* vs *when*:**
Both take the present tense in their own half (that's yesterday's rule, unchanged). The difference is **certainty**:

| | Meaning | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| **when** | it **will** happen — only the timing is open | the event is **certain** |
| **if** | it **might** happen — maybe yes, maybe no | the event is **uncertain** |

Side-by-side minimal pairs:

- ***When** the board approves the budget, we start.* → they **will** approve; you're just waiting for the date.
- ***If** the board approves the budget, we start.* → they **might** approve, they might reject it.

- *I'll call you **when** I land.* → I **am** landing (the flight will arrive).
- *I'll call you **if** I land on time.* → maybe on time, maybe delayed.

Flip it and the sentence lies: saying *"when the board approves"* to a CEO quietly promises the approval is a done deal. If it's genuinely in doubt, that's an *if*.

**PT→EN mapping:**
Portuguese splits the same way — **se** = *if*, **quando** = *when* — so the **choice** between them is easy for you. The trap is the **verb**. Portuguese uses the **future subjunctive** after both: *"Se o conselho **aprovar**"*, *"Quando o conselho **aprovar**"*. That *aprovar* **feels** future, so your instinct is to reach for *will approve* in English. Don't. English flattens that future subjunctive into **plain present**: *If the board **approves***. The direct word-for-word betrays you exactly here.

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. ***will* smuggled into the *if*-part** (the future-subjunctive echo):
   - ❌ *If the board **will approve** the budget, we'll move.*
   - ✅ *If the board **approves** the budget, we'll move.*
2. ***if* where the event is certain** (should be *when*):
   - ❌ *If I **get** to the office tomorrow, I'll send it.* (you **are** going to the office)
   - ✅ *When I get to the office tomorrow, I'll send it.*
3. **both halves in the present** (forgetting the result needs *will*):
   - ❌ *If the price changes, we **renegotiate it** next quarter.* (sounds like a fixed routine, not a plan)
   - ✅ *If the price changes, **we'll renegotiate** it next quarter.*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **If the pilot succeeds, we'll scale it across all three regions.**
   *Executive. The scaling is real but hinges on the pilot — classic conditional plan. Present in the if-part, will in the result.*
2. **We won't commit the full budget if the vendor can't guarantee uptime.**
   *Executive. Result-part first, so no comma. The spending depends on a guarantee that may not come — uncertain, so* if*, not* when.
3. **If the CFO raises the cost question, tell her the ROI model is attached.**
   *Executive. Result is an imperative (*tell*) — perfectly valid after* if*. The CFO might or might not raise it.*
4. **If the board approves next Tuesday, we can kick off onboarding the same week.**
   *Executive. Compare with* when *here: using* if *signals the vote is genuinely contested, not a rubber stamp.*
5. **We'll position the company as an AI-first player if the market keeps moving this way.**
   *Executive. The strategy is conditional on a trend that might reverse — so* if*, and* keeps *stays present.*
6. **If it rains tomorrow, the offsite moves indoors.**
   *Everyday. Rain is genuinely uncertain, so* if*. Note the result can even be present-simple for a fixed arrangement — but a plan usually takes* will.
7. **Call me if you need anything before the flight.**
   *Everyday. Result-first imperative, no comma. The need may or may not arise — uncertain — so* if*, not* when.
8. **If I finish the deck early, I'll send it tonight; if not, tomorrow morning.**
   *Everyday. "Finishing early" is in doubt, so* if*. "I'll send" carries the* will *in the result half.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 093):**
1. *when the board **will be** ready* → *when the board **is** ready*. After *when* about the future, use the **present simple**, never *will*.
2. *We'll hold the hire **until** the budget clears.* *Until* = "up to that moment"; and no, *will* never follows it — present (*clears*), not *will clear*.
3. The present *clears/approves* covers Portuguese *aprovar*; you must **not** add *will*. (*When the board **approves**…*)

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 087:** *the biggest risk*. Short adjective → *the + -est*; the article *the* is never dropped from a superlative.
- **Unit 065:** *more **information*** (uncountable — no plural) and *any **budget*** (here mass/uncountable sense). Portuguese pluralizes *informações*, so you over-pluralize *information*, which has no plural in English.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ❌ *will sign* → ✅ *If the board **signs** the budget…*. No *will* in the *if*-part.
2. ***when*** — the quarter-end is certain (a fixed date); only the timing is pending.
3. ***If*** — the funding is explicitly not guaranteed, so the event is uncertain.
4. *we **renegotiate*** → *we'**ll** renegotiate*. A one-off plan needs *will* in the result half; the bare present sounds like a standing policy.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Condições reais usam duas metades: **if + presente** na condição (*If the board **approves**…*) e **will / can / imperativo** no resultado (*… we'**ll** move*). A regra inquebrável: **nunca use *will* depois de *if*** — o inglês achata o futuro do subjuntivo português (*"Se o conselho **aprovar**"*) em presente simples. Se a parte do *if* vem primeiro, use vírgula; se vem depois, não.

A escolha entre **if** e **when** é a mesma do português (*se* × *quando*): use **when** quando o evento **vai** acontecer (só falta a data) e **if** quando ele **pode ou não** acontecer. A armadilha não é escolher a palavra — é o verbo: aquele *aprovar* parece futuro e puxa você para o *will approve*, que é o erro número um. Também cuidado com deixar as duas metades no presente (*"If the price changes, we renegotiate"*) — o plano pontual pede *we'll* no resultado.
