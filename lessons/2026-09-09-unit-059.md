### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** The board wants one owner for the AI bet. **You** and **I** should present it
> together, but **you** lead.
> **You:** Works for **me**. Sofia built the model, so let's bring **her** in for the
> numbers — the board trusts **her**.
> **CEO:** Good. **She** and **the CFO** already aligned on the assumptions. **They**
> reviewed **them** last night.
> **You:** Then it's clear. **The CFO** and **I** will frame the strategy; **she** and
> Sofia handle the figures. Send the final deck to **Sofia** and **me** by Wednesday.

Notice the switch before any rule. The **same person** shows up as two different words:
*I* / *me*, *she* / *her*, *they* / *them*. When the person **does** the action (*I should
present*, *they reviewed*) English uses one form; when the person **receives** it or sits
after a small word like *to / for / with* (*works for **me***, *bring **her** in*, *send it
to **me***) it flips to the other. And English never drops the little word — *"Works for
me,"* never just *"Works."*

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

Every personal pronoun has **two shapes** — a subject shape and an object shape.

| Subject (does the action) | Object (receives it / after a preposition) |
|---|---|
| **I** | **me** |
| **you** | **you** *(same)* |
| **he** | **him** |
| **she** | **her** |
| **it** | **it** *(same)* |
| **we** | **us** |
| **they** | **them** |

**The rule in one line:** subject form goes **before the verb** (it's the doer);
object form goes **after the verb** or **after any preposition** (*to, for, with,
between, about…*).

- Doer → subject: *"**She** approved it." / "**They** decided."*
- Receiver → object: *"I called **her**." / "Send it to **them**."*
- After a preposition → **always** object: *"between you and **me**", "for **us**",
  "with **him**".*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do two shapes exist?** English word order is fixed, but the pronoun form carries
extra insurance about **who is doing what to whom**. *I* announces a doer; *me* announces a
receiver. The form itself tells you the role — so *"I saw her"* and *"She saw me"* can't be
confused even though the words are the same three people.

**Decision rule #1 — subject vs object.** Ask: is this pronoun the **doer of the verb**, or
is it **receiving the action / sitting after a preposition**?

| The pronoun is… | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| the **doer**, before the verb | subject (*I, he, she, we, they*) | *"**They** approved the budget."* |
| **receiving** the verb's action | object (*me, him, her, us, them*) | *"The board trusts **her**."* |
| **after a preposition** (to/for/with/between) | object | *"Send it to **me**."* |

Minimal pair — the roles flip, the form flips with them:

- *"**She** briefed **me**."* (she = doer → subject; me = receiver → object)
- *"**I** briefed **her**."* (now I do the briefing → subject; her receives → object)

**Decision rule #2 — the double subject/object trap ("X and I" vs "X and me").** When the
pronoun is paired with a name, the form is exactly what it would be **alone** — the name
doesn't change it. Test: drop the other person and see which sounds right.

- *"The CFO and **I** will present."* → drop it → *"**I** will present"* ✅ (doer → subject)
- *"Send the deck to Sofia and **me**."* → drop it → *"Send it to **me**"* ✅ (after *to* → object)
- ❌ *"between you and **I**"* → drop → *"between **I**"* ✗ → so it must be *"between you and **me**"*.

**PT→EN mapping.** Portuguese is a **pro-drop** language: the verb ending already shows the
subject, so you say *"Está pronto"* / *"Aprovaram o orçamento"* with **no pronoun**. English
is **not** pro-drop — it needs a subject in almost every sentence: *"**It's** ready," "**They**
approved the budget."* Also, Portuguese *"entre você e eu"* maps word-for-word to the wrong
English *"between you and I"* — the polite-sounding *I* is a false friend after a preposition;
English wants **me**.

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. ❌ *"Is ready." / "Approved it yesterday."* → ✅ *"**It's** ready." / "**They** approved
   it yesterday."* *(English can't drop the subject the way Portuguese does — the pronoun is
   obligatory.)*
2. ❌ *"between you and **I**", "to Sofia and **I**"* → ✅ *"between you and **me**", "to
   Sofia and **me**"* *(after a preposition, always the object form — the "and I" habit
   overcorrects.)*
3. ❌ *"Me and the CEO decided."* → ✅ *"The CEO and **I** decided."* *(as the doer, use the
   subject form — and by courtesy the other person comes first.)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"**The CFO** and **I** will frame the strategy for the board."*
   *— doers of* will frame *→ subject* I*; test: "I will frame" ✅. Never "the CFO and me will".*
2. *"The board trusts **her** on the numbers, so let's bring **her** in."*
   *— both* her *receive the action (trusts / bring) → object form.*
3. *"Send the final deck to Sofia and **me** by Wednesday."*
   *— after the preposition* to *→ object; test: "send it to me" ✅, not "to I".*
4. *"**They** reviewed the assumptions last night and signed off on **them**."*
   *— they = doer (subject); them = the assumptions receiving the action (object). Two roles, two forms.*
5. *"This is between the CEO and **me** — we'll decide before the offsite."*
   *— after* between *→ object* me*; the tempting "between the CEO and I" is the classic overcorrection.*
6. *"**We** own the AI roadmap, but the budget call isn't **ours** — it's the board's."*
   *— we = doer of* own *(subject); ties back to yesterday's* ours *(possessive pronoun).*
7. *"**She** and I met the investors; **they** were impressed with **us**."*
   *— everyday-into-work: she/they = doers (subject); us = receivers after* with *(object).*
8. *"Can you call **him** back? **He** left a message for **us** this morning."*
   *— everyday: him receives* call *(object); He is the doer of* left *(subject); us after* for *(object).*

   overcorrection where it actually shows up: in executive email and meetings.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 058):**
1. *"The roadmap is **mine**."* — nothing follows, so the possessive **pronoun** replaces
   the adjective; *"is my"* is incomplete because *my* must sit before a noun.
2. **Whose** budget model is this? — it asks about ownership. Test: expand to *who is* →
   *"Who is budget model…"* ✗, so it's *whose*, not *who's*.
3. *"It's **hers**."* — ownership → possessive pronoun. *"It's her"* points at a person
   (identifies her), a different meaning.

**Spaced review:**
- *(Unit 052)* *"Let's **get** aligned before Thursday."* — *get + adjective* = **become**
  (Portuguese *ficar*): move into a state you're not in yet. *Be aligned* would claim you
  already agree.
- *(Unit 035)* *"Do we **have to** wait for the full board?"* — it's an **external
  requirement** (a process/rule), which is *have to*; *must* would sound like your own
  personal insistence, not the process.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **I** — it's the doer of *will present*; test: *"I will present"* ✅ (*"me will present"* ✗).
2. ❌ → ✅ *"Send the updated model to Sofia and **me**."* — after the preposition *to* →
   object; test: *"send it to me"* ✅.
3. *"**She** trusts **him** on the numbers."* — *she* = doer (subject) before the verb; *him*
   = receiver (object) after the verb.
4. *"**The CFO and I** decided…"* — as doers, use the subject form *I*, with the other person
   first by courtesy; test: *"I decided"* ✅.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Cada pronome pessoal em inglês tem **duas formas**: uma de **sujeito** (quem faz a ação —
*I, he, she, we, they*) e uma de **objeto** (quem recebe a ação ou vem depois de preposição
— *me, him, her, us, them*). A regra prática: antes do verbo, forma de sujeito (*They
approved it*); depois do verbo ou depois de *to / for / with / between*, forma de objeto
(*Send it to **me***). Quando o pronome vem junto de um nome, teste tirando o nome: *"The CFO
and I will present"* → *"I will present"* ✅.

As armadilhas nascem do português. Primeiro, o português **omite o sujeito** (*"Está
pronto"*), mas o inglês **exige** o pronome: *"**It's** ready."* Segundo, *"entre você e eu"*
vira, por transferência direta, o errado *"between you and I"* — depois de preposição é
**sempre** objeto: *"between you and **me**."* E, ao ser o sujeito, diga *"The CEO and
**I**"*, nunca *"Me and the CEO"*.
