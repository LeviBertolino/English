### 1. 📘 Unit 026 — What are you doing tomorrow? (present tenses for the future)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** What's your week looking like?
> **You:** Full. I**'m presenting** the AI roadmap to the board on Thursday, and
> we**'re meeting** the CFO tomorrow to defend the budget.
> **CEO:** And the offsite?
> **You:** It **starts** at nine sharp on Monday. I**'m flying** to São Paulo on
> Sunday night so I'm there in time.
> **CEO:** Good. What **are** you **doing** after today's sync?
> **You:** Nothing fixed yet.

Notice the pattern *before* any rule. Almost everything future here is a
**present continuous** — *I'm presenting, we're meeting, I'm flying* — because
each one is **already arranged**: a decision made, often with a day and a place.
Only the offsite uses the **present simple** (*it starts at nine*), because that
time is fixed by a **schedule**, not by you. Portuguese would say all of it in
the plain present — *"quinta eu apresento…", "amanhã a gente reúne…"* — and that
reflex is exactly what English does **not** do for personal plans.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

English uses **two present tenses to talk about the future**, and each has its
own job.

| Use | Tense | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| A **personal arrangement / plan** (already decided) | **present continuous** | am/is/are + *-ing* | *I**'m presenting** on Thursday.* |
| A **timetable / schedule** (fixed by clock or calendar) | **present simple** | base verb (+ *-s*) | *The offsite **starts** at nine.* |

**Key facts:**
- The **present continuous for the future** almost always carries a **future time
  marker** — *tomorrow, on Thursday, next week, tonight* — which is what tells the
  listener you mean the future, not right now. *"I'm meeting the CFO"* (now, in
  progress) vs *"I'm meeting the CFO **at three**"* (a future arrangement).
- The **present simple for the future** is narrow: it's for things run by a
  **timetable** — meetings on an agenda, flights, trains, film times, opening
  hours. *"The board meeting **starts** at 9", "My flight **leaves** at 6:40."*
- Ask *"Have I arranged this, or does a schedule decide it?"* Arranged by me →
  continuous. Set by the clock/agenda → simple.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** Because English wants to show that a future
plan is **already settled** — not a guess, not a decision made this second, but
something on the calendar. The present continuous does that: it takes a form you
know for *"happening now"* and points it forward, so the plan feels **already in
motion**. *"I'm presenting to the board on Thursday"* means it's arranged, the
slot is booked, it's real.

**Decision rule vs the rival structures.** Two rivals matter here.

**Rival 1 — present continuous vs present simple (the unit's own core).** Both can
point to the future, but they answer different questions.

| Meaning | ✅ Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Something **I / we arranged** | **present continuous**: *I**'m chairing** the review at nine.* | a personal plan, decided |
| Something a **schedule** dictates | **present simple**: *The review **starts** at nine.* | fixed by the agenda, not by me |

Minimal pair — same nine o'clock, different tense because the *meaning* differs:
- *"The strategy review **starts** at nine."* → the timetable says so. ✅
- *"I**'m chairing** the strategy review at nine."* → my arrangement. ✅
- ❌ *"I **chair** the review at nine tomorrow."* → *a personal plan needs the
  continuous: **I'm chairing**.*

Decision rule: **my plan → present continuous; the clock's schedule → present
simple.** If *you* decided it, it's continuous.

**Rival 2 — present continuous vs *will* (the one Brazilians reach for).** For a
future that's **already arranged**, English prefers the **present continuous**,
not *will*. *will* is for decisions made **at the moment of speaking**, offers,
and predictions (its own unit is coming). So:
- *"It's in my calendar — I**'m meeting** the CFO at four."* (arranged) ✅
- *"The phone's ringing — I**'ll get** it."* (decided right now) ✅ *will*
- ❌ *"It's already booked, so I **will meet** the CFO at four."* → *already
  arranged, so use the present continuous: **I'm meeting**.*

**PT→EN mapping.** Portuguese uses the **presente do indicativo** for near-future
plans — *"Quinta eu **apresento** ao board", "Semana que vem eu **viajo** pra São
Paulo."* Translated word-for-word that becomes *"Thursday I present…", "Next week
I travel…"* — grammatically present, and to an English ear it sounds like a
timetable or a general truth, **not** a plan. English needs the **present
continuous**: *"I**'m presenting** to the board on Thursday", "I**'m travelling**
to São Paulo next week."* Where the direct translation *does* work: **timetables**
— *"o voo **sai** às sete"* → *"the flight **leaves** at seven"* (present simple in
both). (Portuguese *"vou apresentar"* maps to *going to*, a later unit — for now,
for arrangements, reach for the continuous.)

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *Tomorrow I **present** the 2027 vision to the C-Level.* → ✅ *I**'m
  presenting** the 2027 vision to the C-Level tomorrow.* — *a personal
  arrangement takes the **present continuous**; the plain present simple reads as
  a timetable and sounds off for your own plan.*
- ❌ *What **do** you **do** this weekend?* (meaning: what are your plans) → ✅
  *What **are** you **doing** this weekend?* — *"what do you do?" asks about a
  **habit/job**; for future plans use the present continuous.*
- ❌ *It's already booked — I **will meet** the CFO at four.* → ✅ *It's already
  booked — I**'m meeting** the CFO at four.* — *already arranged → present
  continuous; *will* is for a decision made **right now**, not a fixed plan.*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **I'm presenting the AI roadmap to the board on Thursday.**
   *A fixed personal arrangement → present continuous; "I present … on Thursday"
   would sound like a recurring timetable, not this week's plan.*
2. **We're meeting the CFO tomorrow to defend the AI budget.**
   *An arranged meeting with a set day → present continuous; the plan is already
   in motion, so not *will meet*.*
3. **The strategy offsite starts at nine sharp on Monday.**
   *A time fixed by the **schedule** → present simple; nobody's "arranging" the
   start time in the moment — the agenda dictates it.*
4. **I'm flying to São Paulo next week for the innovation review.**
   *A booked trip → present continuous; the ticket makes it an arrangement, even
   though the flying happens later.*
5. **My flight leaves at 6:40, so I'll be at HQ by nine.**
   *A **timetable** event → present simple (*leaves*); airlines, not you, set
   departure times.*
6. **What are you doing after the CEO sync?**
   *Asking about someone's **plans**, not their habits → present continuous;
   "what do you do after…?" would ask about a routine.*
7. **The vendor demo is at two, and the contract review runs right after.**
   *Both are fixed agenda slots → present simple (*is*, *runs*); a scheduled
   sequence behaves like a timetable.*
8. **We're having dinner with the new AI partner on Friday.**
   *An everyday social **arrangement** with a set day → present continuous; it's
   agreed and on the calendar.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

1. Ask your tutor to run a **"week ahead" drill**: they ask *"What are you doing
   on Monday? And Thursday? What are you doing after the board dinner?"* and you
   answer every arrangement in the **present continuous** — *"I'm presenting…",
   "I'm flying to…", "We're meeting…"*. Have them deliberately bait you with a
   Portuguese-style prompt (*"So Thursday you present to the board?"*) so you
   practise correcting *"I present"* → *"I'm presenting"* out loud.
2. Then a **timetable vs plan sorting drill**: your tutor throws mixed items
   about your real week — *"the flight", "the offsite start time", "your meeting
   with the CFO", "the keynote", "dinner with the partner"* — and you decide fast
   whether it's a **schedule** (present simple: *"the flight leaves at 6:40"*) or
   **your arrangement** (present continuous: *"I'm meeting the CFO at four"*),
   saying each one aloud.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 025)**
1. ✅ *"**Did** the CFO **use to** sit in on the technology reviews?"* — *after
   **did**, the verb drops the *-d*: **use to**, not *used to*; the auxiliary
   already carries the past.*
2. **am used to** — *"…I **am used to** presenting to the board."* *be used to +
   -ing* = **accustomed** (present, ongoing). *"I used to present"* would mean a
   past habit that **stopped** — the opposite of what a year of experience implies.
3. ✅ *"I **usually** work from home."* — *used to* has **no present form**; for a
   **current** habit English uses the **present simple**, often with *usually*.
   *"I use to work from home"* is the classic Brazilian interference error.

**Spaced review**
- **(Unit 019)** *"Assinei… **há** dois anos"* → ✅ *"I **signed** with the vendor
  two years **ago**."* (finished, dated event → **ago + past simple**). *"Trabalho
  com eles **há** dois anos"* → ✅ *"I've **worked** with them **for** two years."*
  (still going, span up to now → **for + present perfect**). *The one Portuguese
  word* há *splits in two: **ago** if it's over, **for** if it continues.*

**Recognition drill**
1. ✅ *"What **are** you **doing** after the board dinner?"* — *you're asking about
   a **plan/arrangement**, so the **present continuous**; "what do you do…?" would
   ask about a habit or job.*
2. ❌ *"Next Tuesday I **present**…"* → ✅ *"Next Tuesday I**'m presenting** the
   2027 vision to the C-Level."* — *a specific one-off **arrangement** takes the
   present continuous; the present simple reads as a timetable/routine.*
3. **starts / 'm rehearsing** — ✅ *"The keynote **starts** at ten, but I**'m
   rehearsing** with the team at eight."* — *the keynote time is set by the
   **schedule** → present simple; the rehearsal is **your arrangement** → present
   continuous.*
4. **'m meeting** — ✅ *"It's already in my calendar — I**'m meeting** the CFO at
   four."* — *already **arranged** → present continuous; *will* is for a decision
   made **at the moment of speaking**, not a booked plan.*

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** O inglês usa **dois presentes para falar do futuro**.
Para um **plano ou compromisso que você já marcou**, use o **present continuous**
(*am/is/are + -ing*): *"I**'m presenting** to the board on Thursday", "We**'re
meeting** the CFO tomorrow."* Quase sempre vem com um marcador de tempo futuro
(*tomorrow, on Thursday, next week*), que é o que sinaliza o futuro. Para algo
fixado por um **horário/agenda** — voos, trens, reuniões pautadas, palestras —
use o **present simple**: *"The offsite **starts** at nine", "My flight **leaves**
at 6:40."* A pergunta-chave: *eu marquei isso, ou um horário decide?* Eu marquei →
continuous; a agenda decide → simple.

**As armadilhas.** (1) A interferência número um: o português usa o **presente do
indicativo** para plano futuro (*"quinta eu apresento ao board"*), e traduzir
direto vira *"Thursday I present…"*, que soa como rotina/horário, não como plano —
o certo é *"I**'m presenting**…"*. (2) *"What do you do this weekend?"* pergunta
sobre **hábito/profissão**; para **planos** é *"What **are** you **doing** this
weekend?"*. (3) Para algo **já combinado**, prefira o present continuous a *will*:
*"It's booked — I**'m meeting** the CFO at four"*, não *"I will meet"* (*will* fica
para decisão tomada **na hora**, unidade que vem a seguir). Se a diferença entre
plano e horário embolar, releia `lessons/2026-08-06-unit-025.md` (o contraste
passado-vs-presente ajuda a fixar qual tempo carrega qual sentido).
