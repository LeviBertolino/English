### 1. 📘 Unit 023 — be / have / do in present and past tenses

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** **Does** the board **have** everything it needs for Thursday?
> **You:** Almost. The deck **is** ready, and the CFO **has** reviewed the
> numbers. We **don't have** the legal sign-off yet — but I **didn't** want to
> send it half-finished.
> **CEO:** **Do** you **need** anything from me?
> **You:** No. I **have** one open question for the vendor, and I**'m** chasing
> it now.

Notice, before any rule, that the same three little verbs keep reappearing — but
in two completely different jobs. *is ready*, *have one open question* are **main
verbs** (they carry the meaning themselves). *Does the board have…?*, *don't
have*, *didn't want*, *Do you need?* use **do / does / did** as a **helper** to
build the question or the negative — the helper carries no meaning of its own. And
*has reviewed*, *I'm chasing* use **have** and **be** as helpers too, for the
perfect and the continuous. Three verbs, two lives each: this unit is about
telling those lives apart.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

**be, have, do** are the three most important verbs in English because each one
works **both as a main verb and as an auxiliary (a helper)**.

| Verb | Forms (present / past) | As a **main verb** (own meaning) | As an **auxiliary** (helper) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **be** | am/is/are — was/were | *I **am** tired. The plan **is** ready.* | continuous: *I **am** doing* · passive: *it **is** done* |
| **have** | have/has — had | *I **have** a meeting.* (possession) | perfect: *I **have** done* |
| **do** | do/does — did | *I **do** my job well.* | negatives & questions in the **simple** tenses: *I **don't**… · **Did** you…?* |

**The one rule that governs `do`:** you use **do / does / did** to build a
**negative or a question in the present simple or past simple — and nowhere else.**
The moment another helper is already present (*be, have, can, will…*), that helper
does the job and **do disappears**.

| | Statement | Question | Negative |
|---|---|---|---|
| present simple | *She **leads** it.* | ***Does** she **lead** it?* | *She **doesn't lead** it.* |
| past simple | *They **approved** it.* | ***Did** they **approve** it?* | *They **didn't approve** it.* |
| already has a helper | *We **are** ready. / We **have** decided.* | ***Are** we ready? / **Have** we decided?* | *We **aren't** ready. / We **haven't** decided.* |

After **does / did**, the main verb goes back to its **base form**: *Does she
**lead*** (not *leads*), *Did they **approve*** (not *approved*). The helper holds
the tense so the main verb doesn't have to.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these three verbs matter so much?** Because almost every English
negative, question, continuous, perfect, and passive is built from one of them.
Get *be / have / do* right and the whole tense system holds together; get the
helper wrong and even a simple question collapses. This unit isn't new grammar —
it's the wiring behind everything from Units 1–21.

**Decision rule — do I add `do`, or is there already a helper?**
Ask one question before every negative or question: *is there already an auxiliary
in this sentence?*
- **Yes** (a form of *be*, *have* [perfect], *can/will/should*…) → use **that**
  helper, no *do*.
- **No** (a plain present simple or past simple main verb) → you **must** bring in
  **do / does / did**.

Minimal pairs — same idea, the helper decides everything:

| You mean | ✅ Correct | ❌ Wrong | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| ask about a plan (present simple) | ***Does** the roadmap **cover** 2027?* | *Is the roadmap cover 2027?* | present simple → needs **does**, not *is* |
| ask about a modal | ***Can** you **present** tomorrow?* | *Do you can present tomorrow?* | *can* is already the helper → **no** *do* |
| ask about a finished result | ***Have** you **finished** the deck?* | *Do you have finished the deck?* | *have* (perfect) is the helper → **no** *do* |
| ask about possession | ***Does** the CFO **have** the numbers?* | *Has the CFO the numbers?* | here *have* is a **main** verb → needs **does** |

**PT→EN mapping — the trap for a Brazilian speaker.** Portuguese has **no
do-support at all**. *"Você trabalha aqui?"* is just verb + subject, no helper
word. So the instinct is to translate it with no helper (*"You work here?"*) or to
grab the wrong helper (*"Is you work here?"*). English **forces** an empty helper
into the plain question or negative: *"**Do** you work here?"* Train the reflex:
*plain verb + question/negative → insert do/does/did.*
A second *ter* trap: Portuguese *ter* is *have*, but existence — *"**Tem** três
riscos abertos"* — is **there is / there are** in English, not *have*: *"**There
are** three open risks,"* not *"Have three open risks."*

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *Do you can join the strategy sync?* → ✅ ***Can** you join the strategy
  sync?* — never put *do* in front of a modal; the modal is already the helper.
- ❌ *Does the CFO **has** the final numbers?* → ✅ *Does the CFO **have** the
  final numbers?* — after *does/did*, the main verb drops back to its **base
  form**; the helper already carries the tense.
- ❌ *I **didn't approved** the budget* / *Did the board **decided**?* → ✅ *I
  **didn't approve** the budget* / *Did the board **decide**?* — *did* holds the
  past; the main verb stays base.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **Does the board have the final AI budget in front of them?**
   *Present-simple question about possession — main verb* have *needs* does; *the
   helper carries the tense, so it's* have*, not* has.
2. **The CFO doesn't support the vendor choice yet.**
   *Present-simple negative, no other helper present → we bring in* doesn't; *the
   main verb* support *stays base.*
3. **We didn't launch the pilot last quarter — the funding wasn't approved.**
   did *carries the past for the active clause;* wasn't approved *is passive (*be*
   + participle), so it needs no* do.
4. **Have you already aligned the leadership team on the 2027 vision?**
   *A finished-result question → the perfect helper* have *builds it;* do *would be
   double-helping and is wrong.*
5. **Can the innovation portfolio absorb one more bet this year?**
   *A modal question — *can* is already the auxiliary, so no* do *appears; the main
   verb stays base.*
6. **I have a hard stop at four, so let's keep the board prep tight.**
   have *as a plain main verb (possession/appointment) — a statement, so no helper
   at all.*
7. **Do you want the risk section before or after the numbers?**
   *Present-simple question with a plain main verb (*want*) and no other helper →*
   do *is obligatory; Portuguese would have none.*
8. **The results are reviewed every fortnight, and the summary is sent to the CEO.**
   be *+ participle = passive (two of them); the focus is on the results and the
   summary, not who reviews or sends them.*

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**be, have e do** são os três verbos-chave do inglês porque cada um vive duas
vidas: como **verbo principal** (com significado próprio — *I **am** ready*, *I
**have** a meeting*, *I **do** my job*) e como **auxiliar** (ajudante que monta
tempos verbais — contínuo *am doing*, perfect *have done*, passiva *is done*, e as
perguntas/negativas do simple com *do/does/did*). A regra que resolve quase tudo:
use **do / does / did** só para fazer **pergunta ou negativa no present simple ou
past simple**. Se já existe outro auxiliar na frase (*be, have, can, will*), é ele
que faz o trabalho e o *do* **some**. Depois de *does/did*, o verbo principal volta
à **forma base** (*Does she lead?*, *Did they approve?*), porque o auxiliar já
segura o tempo.

As armadilhas para o brasileiro vêm de o português **não ter** o *do*: *"Você
trabalha aqui?"* não tem palavra-ajudante, então a tendência é esquecer o *do*
(*"You work here?"*) ou usar o auxiliar errado (*"Is you work?"*) — o inglês
**obriga** o *do/does/did* na pergunta/negativa simples. Evite os três erros
clássicos: **nunca** *do* na frente de modal (*"Do you can…"* → **Can you…?**); depois
de *does/did* o verbo fica na base (*"Does he has…"* → **Does he have…?**, *"didn't
approved"* → **didn't approve**); e *ter* de existência é **there is/are**, não
*have* (*"Tem três riscos"* → **There are three risks**).
