### 1. 📘 Unit 068 — all / all of · most / most of · some / none of

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Do our people actually believe in the AI strategy?
> **You:** **Most leaders** do, in general. But **most of our leaders** here still wait for proof —
> **some of them** openly, **some** quietly.
> **CEO:** And the pilots?
> **You:** **None of the pilots** failed, but **none** are ready to scale either. **All of them**
> need more data.
> **CFO:** So where did the quarter's budget go?
> **You:** **All the budget** went to talent. **All of us** agreed that was the right bet — you
> included.

Notice one word doing quiet work: **of**. *Most **leaders*** talks about leaders everywhere;
*most **of** our leaders* narrows to **this** company. *None* and *all* stand alone when the group is
obvious, but grab **of** the moment they point at *our leaders*, *the pilots*, *us*. That little
*of* is today's whole lesson.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

These words — **all, most, some, any, no / none, half, much, many, (a) little, (a) few** — come in
two shapes:

| Talking about… | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| people/things **in general** | word **+ noun** (no *of*, no *the*) | *most **companies***, *some **money***, *all **strategy** carries risk* |
| a **specific** group (the / this / my / these …) | word **+ of + the/my/these + noun** | *most **of the** companies*, *some **of my** budget*, *none **of the** pilots* |
| a **pronoun** (us / them / it / you) | word **+ of +** pronoun (always *of*) | *most **of us***, *all **of them***, *none **of it*** |

Two special cases to memorise:

- **all** and **half** may **drop** *of* before *the / my / this*: *all (of) the budget*, *half (of)
  the team* — both correct. But *of* is **obligatory** before a pronoun: *all **of** us*, not
  *all us*.
- **no** vs **none**: *no* is the general shape (*no leaders agreed*); *none* is the *of* / stand-alone
  shape (*none **of the** leaders agreed*, or just *— How many agreed? — **None**.*).

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (the core section)

**Why does *of* exist here?** It's the switch between **the whole category** and **a defined subset
in front of you**. *Most executives resist change* is a claim about executives as a class. *Most of
our executives resist change* is a claim about **your** leadership team — a very different sentence
to say to a CEO. Dropping or adding *of* silently changes who you're talking about.

**Decision rule — is there a "pointing" word after it?**
Look at what follows the quantifier:
- Followed by a **bare plural/uncountable noun** (*leaders, budget, data*) → **no *of***: *most
  leaders*, *some budget*.
- Followed by **the / this / that / these / my / our / your + noun** → **use *of*** (optional only
  after *all* / *half*): *most **of the** leaders*, *some **of our** budget*, *none **of these**
  pilots*.
- Followed by a **pronoun** → **always *of***: *most **of them***, *all **of us***, *none **of it***.

Minimal pairs — same words, the meaning turns on *of + the*:

| General (category) | Specific (this group) |
|---|---|
| *Most **AI projects** overrun.* (industry-wide) | *Most **of our AI projects** overran.* (ours, this year) |
| *Some **investors** are cautious.* (investors at large) | *Some **of the investors** in the room are cautious.* (these ones) |
| *No **strategy** is risk-free.* (any strategy anywhere) | *None **of the** three strategies is risk-free.* (these three) |

**PT→EN mapping.** Portuguese almost always carries the article: *a maioria **dos** líderes*,
*todos **os** projetos*, *alguns **dos** investidores*. That *dos / os* is glued into the phrase, so
Brazilians import it into English and say *"most **of the** leaders"* even when they mean leaders in
general. English does the opposite: **general = strip everything** (*most leaders*), **specific =
add *of the*** (*most of the leaders*). When in doubt, ask yourself *"whose?"* — if you can't name a
group, drop the *of the*.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"Most of companies are adopting AI."* → ✅ *"**Most companies** are adopting AI."* Speaking
   generally = no *of*, no *the*. (*Most of companies* is impossible in any reading — *of* needs
   *the/my/these* or a pronoun after it.)
2. ❌ *"All of leaders should upskill."* → ✅ *"**All leaders** should upskill."* (general) — or ✅
   *"**All of our leaders** should upskill."* (this team). Never *all of* + bare noun.
3. ❌ *"Most of us **agrees**."* → ✅ *"**Most of us agree**."* / ✅ *"**None of the pilots are**
   ready."* When *of + plural* group follows, the verb is usually **plural** — agree with the people,
   not with *most / none*.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **Most companies** underestimate the cost of AI adoption.
   *→ Bare plural noun, industry-wide claim — no *of*, no *the*. This is the general shape.*
2. **Most of our business units** have already piloted at least one model.
   *→ *our business units* is a specific group → *most **of** our*. Compare #1: the *of* is what
   localises it to us.*
3. **None of the three vendors** met the security bar.
   *→ A defined set (*the three vendors*) → *none of the*. Not *no vendors* — we're judging **these**,
   not vendors in general.*
4. **All of us** signed off on the roadmap, board included.
   *→ Pronoun *us* → *of* is obligatory. *All us* is ungrammatical; only *all of us* works.*
5. **All the budget** went to talent this quarter.
   *→ *all* may drop *of* before *the*: *all (of) the budget* both fine. Specific pot of money, so
   *the* stays.*
6. **Some leaders** resist AI; **some of the loudest** sit on my own team.
   *→ First *some* is general (*some leaders*); second points at a subset → *some of the*. Same word,
   two shapes, one sentence.*
7. Cancel the meeting — I've had **no time** to prepare, and **none of the slides** are final.
   *→ *no + bare noun* (*no time*) for the general lack; *none of the + specific noun* (*the slides*)
   for this deck. This is the *no / none* split.*
8. **Half the team** is remote, so **most of them** join by video.
   *→ *half* drops *of* before *the* (*half the team*); *most* + pronoun *them* forces *of*
   (*most of them*).*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 067 — much / many / little / few):**
1. ✅ *"We don't have **much** time or **many** options left before Q4."* *time* is **uncountable**
   → *much*; *options* is a **countable plural** → *many*. The count test decides.
2. ✅ *"**A few** board members support…"* = *you have some allies* (positive base). *"**Few** board
   members support…"* = *almost nobody does* (a warning). The *a* flips it from a problem to an
   asset.
3. ✅ *"We collected **a lot of** feedback…"* *much* is fine in negatives/questions but sounds wrong
   in a plain positive statement — use *a lot of* (or *plenty of*) there.

**Spaced review:**
- *(Unit 061 — reflexives / each other)* ✅ *"The CEO and the CFO don't always **agree with each
  other** on the pace."* A **two-way** relationship (A ↔ B) uses **each other**, not a reflexive.
  *agree with themselves* would mean each one agrees internally — not the meaning.
- *(Unit 040 — question tags)* ✅ *"…hasn't signed off on the budget yet, **has it?**"* A **negative**
  statement (*hasn't*) takes a **positive** tag (*has it?*) — opposite polarity — reusing the same
  auxiliary (*has*) and subject-pronoun (*it*).

**Recognition drill:**
1. ✅ *"**Most** startups fail…, but **most of** the ones we backed survived."* First is general
   (bare plural → no *of*); second points at *the ones we backed* (specific → *most of*).
2. ✅ General: *"**All leaders** in the room supported…"* is still wrong here — *in the room* makes it
   specific, so the two real fixes are **"All the leaders in the room…"** or **"All of the leaders in
   the room…"** (*all* allows *of* to drop before *the*). The only impossible version is *all of
   leaders* — *of* can't sit before a bare noun.
3. ✅ *"None of the pilots **are** ready…"* Standard modern usage takes a **plural** verb when *none
   of* + plural noun follows (agree with *pilots*). (*is* is used only in very formal writing.)
4. ✅ *"**Most of our flagship projects** are over budget."* You mean **your** specific three, so you
   need *of our* to localise it. *"Most projects are over budget"* is a claim about projects
   **everywhere** — it hides your problem and sounds like an industry excuse.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Palavras como *all, most, some, no/none* têm **duas formas**. Para falar em **geral**, use a palavra
**+ substantivo puro**, sem *of* e sem *the*: *most companies*, *some budget*, *no strategy is
risk-free*. Para falar de um **grupo específico** (o seu time, estes pilotos), acrescente **of +
the/our/these**: *most of our leaders*, *none of the pilots*, *some of my budget*. Antes de
**pronome** (*us, them, it*), o *of* é **obrigatório**: *all of us*, *most of them*. Só *all* e
*half* podem largar o *of* antes de *the* (*all (of) the budget*).

A cilada é o português quase sempre carregar o artigo — *a maioria **dos** líderes*, *todos **os**
projetos* — e você importar esse *of the* para o inglês geral: *"most of the companies use AI"*
quando quer dizer empresas em geral. Regra de bolso: pergunte *"de quem? quais?"* — se **não
consegue nomear o grupo**, tire o *of the* (*most companies*). E cuidado com o verbo: *most of us
**agree*** e *none of the pilots **are*** ficam no **plural**, concordando com as pessoas/coisas, não
com *most/none*.
