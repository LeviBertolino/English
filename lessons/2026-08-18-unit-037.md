### 1. 📘 Unit 037 — would like (I'd like… / Would you like…?)

---

### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** We need a stronger story for the board on AI. Where do you land?
> **You:** I **'d like to** propose that we position the company as an AI-first
> player. **Would you like me to** draft the narrative this week?
> **CEO:** Yes, please. And the numbers?
> **You:** The CFO **would like** a cleaner budget breakdown first — I **'d like to**
> sit with her before Thursday. **Would you like to** review it before the board?
> **CEO:** Good. Send it over.

Notice the pattern before any rule. Every *would like* here is a **polite want** —
a wish, an offer, an invitation — softened for the room. *"I'd like to propose"* is
not weaker than *"I want to propose"*; it's the **diplomatic** version, the one you
use with a CEO or a board. Portuguese has the exact same move — *"gostaria de"* —
and that clean mapping is mostly a gift, with two sharp traps we'll fix below.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

*would like* = a polite way to say *want*. *would* is a modal, so the form is the
**same for every subject**, and it almost always contracts to **'d**.

| | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Statement (+ verb) | **'d like to** + base verb | *I **'d like to** propose a pilot.* |
| Statement (+ noun) | **'d like** + noun | *The CFO **'d like** a clearer breakdown.* |
| Offer / invitation | **Would** + subject + **like to** + base? | ***Would** you **like to** join the offsite?* |
| Offer to *do it for them* | **Would** + subject + **like** + **me/us to** + base? | ***Would** you **like me to** present it?* |
| Negative (rare) | **wouldn't like** | *I **wouldn't like** to be in his seat.* |

**Key facts:**
- Contractions carry it: **I'd, you'd, he'd, she'd, we'd, they'd**. In speech you'll
  almost never hear the full *"I would like"* — it's *"I'd like."*
- Two things can follow: a **to-infinitive** (*I'd like **to** review it*) or a
  **noun** (*I'd like **your** feedback*). Never drop the *to* before a verb.
- To offer to **do something for someone**, use **would like + [person] + to**:
  *"Would you like **me to** open the meeting?"* — the person comes **before** *to*.
- Questions invert *would*, like any modal: ***Would** you like…?* — no *do/does*.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does *would like* exist?** English needs a **polite, softened** way to state
a want, make an offer, or extend an invitation — a register you can safely use with
a CEO, a CFO, or a board. Bare *want* is grammatical but **blunt**; in a senior
room *"I want the forecast by Friday"* can land as a demand. *would like* keeps the
same meaning and adds **courtesy and distance**: *"I'd like the forecast by
Friday."* For a leader whose whole job is influence, not command, this is the
default register for proposing, requesting, and offering.

**Decision rule #1 — *would like* vs *like* (this specific wish vs a general
truth). This is the single biggest trap, because the two look almost identical:**

| | *would like* | *like* |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | **want — now / this time** (a specific wish or offer) | **enjoy — in general** (a permanent preference) |
| Time | this occasion | always true |
| Example | *"I **'d like** a coffee."* (bring me one now) | *"I **like** coffee."* (in general, I enjoy it) |
| As a question | *"**Would** you **like** to present?"* (offer — this time) | *"**Do** you **like** presenting?"* (do you enjoy it?) |

Minimal pair — same verb, opposite job:
- *"**Would you like to** join the board dinner?"* → an **invitation** to this
  event. ✅
- *"**Do you like to** join board dinners?"* → asking whether he **enjoys** them in
  general — not an invitation at all. ❌ (wrong meaning if you meant to invite)

Decision rule: **am I naming a specific wish/offer for now, or a general taste?**
Now / this time → *would like*. Always-true preference → *like*.

**Decision rule #2 — *would like* vs *want* (polite vs blunt).** Same meaning,
different **register**. Both are correct English; the difference is social. With
the C-Level, in writing, in any careful room, *would like* is the safe default.
*want* is fine among peers or when you deliberately want directness.
- *"I **want** to see the updated numbers."* → direct, can sound like an order. ✅
  (register: blunt)
- *"I **'d like** to see the updated numbers."* → the same request, made diplomatic.
  ✅ (register: courteous)

**PT→EN mapping — where *gostaria* helps and where it betrays.** The core mapping is
clean and reliable: *"gostaria de"* → **would like to** (+ verb) or **would like**
(+ noun). *"Gostaria de propor um piloto"* → *"I'**d like to** propose a pilot."*
Two places it betrays Levi:
- (1) **The *"gostaria que eu"* trap.** Portuguese offers with *"Gostaria que eu
  apresentasse?"* (que + subjunctive). English does **not** use *"that I…"* — it
  uses **would like + me to**: *"**Would you like me to** present?"*, never *"Would
  you like that I present?"*
- (2) **The *gostaria* → *like* collapse.** When Levi simplifies *"gostaria"* down
  to plain *like*, the meaning flips from *this specific wish* to *general habit*:
  *"I like to propose a pilot"* says he **enjoys proposing pilots in general** — not
  that he wants to propose **this** one. Keep the *'d*.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"**Would you like that I** open the board meeting?"* → ✅ *"**Would you like me
  to** open the board meeting?"* — the *"gostaria que eu"* structure maps to *would
  like + me to*, with **no** *that*.
- ❌ *"**Do you like to** join the strategy offsite next month?"* (as an invitation)
  → ✅ *"**Would you like to** join the strategy offsite next month?"* — *Do you
  like…?* asks about a general taste; only *Would you like…?* actually invites.
- ❌ *"I'd like **propose** a partnership."* → ✅ *"I'd like **to propose** a
  partnership."* — before a verb, *would like* always needs **to**.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"I **'d like to** propose that we position the company as an AI-first player."*
   *— A polite proposal to the room; softer and more board-appropriate than "I want to propose."*
2. *"**Would you like me to** walk the board through the AI roadmap, or will the CEO?"*
   *— Offer to do it for them: "me to," never "that I."*
3. *"The CFO **would like** a clearer breakdown of the AI budget before Thursday."*
   *— Polite want + a noun; reports a wish without sounding like a demand.*
4. *"**Would you like to** join the strategy offsite next month?"*
   *— An invitation to a specific event; "Do you like to join…?" would only ask if he enjoys offsites.*
5. *"We **'d like to** explore a partnership, but the board hasn't approved it yet."*
   *— A tentative, diplomatic wish; "would like" keeps it a proposal, not a done decision.*
6. *"I **'d like** your honest read on the acquisition — no filter, please."*
   *— Polite request + noun; "I want your read" would sound blunt to a peer you're leaning on.*
7. *"**Would you like** some coffee before we start the review?"*
   *— Everyday offer for now; "Do you like coffee?" would ask about her general taste instead.*
8. *"I **'d like** a table by the window, please."*
   *— Everyday specific request; "I like a table by the window" would state a general preference, not order one.*

### 6. 🧪 Recognition drill (4 items, ~2 minutes)

Answers in the Gabarito — decide first.

1. **Rival choice (the core one):** You're **inviting** the CFO to next month's

   you like to**? What does the wrong one actually ask her?
2. **Rival choice (form of the offer):** You want to offer to present the roadmap
   yourself. *"___ present the AI roadmap to the board?"* — **Would you like that I**

3. **Register (blunt vs polite):** Writing to the board chair, which lands better —
   *"I want the updated forecast before Thursday"* or *"I'd like the updated forecast
   before Thursday"*? What does *would like* add that *want* doesn't?
4. **Form fix:** *"I'd like propose a new pilot to the innovation committee."* One
   small word is missing — which, and where?

### 7. 🔊 Shadowing (ouça e repita)

Listen once, then repeat 2–3 times per sentence, copying the rhythm. In natural
speech *I'd like* is one quick beat — *"I'd like"*, not *"I … would … like"* — and
*would you* often blurs to *"wouldya"*. Keep the weight on the **main verb**
(*"I'd like to **propose**", "would you like me to **walk**"*). Phrase 1 has a slow

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run an **"offer drill"**: they give you a situation — *"the CEO
  needs the narrative drafted," "the board wants a demo set up," "the CFO could use
  the numbers reworked"* — and you offer fast with *"**Would you like me to**…?"*
  every time. The goal is to kill the *"Would you like that I…?"* reflex until *me
  to* comes out automatically.
- Then a **"polite proposal" role-play**: your tutor plays a skeptical board chair,
  and you open each pitch with *"I**'d like to** propose…"* / *"We**'d like to**
  explore…"*, then invite them in with *"**Would you like to** review the details
  first?"* — practising the diplomatic register you'd actually use with the
  C-Level, instead of the blunter *"I want."*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 035 — have to / have got to):**
1. ***mustn't*** — *"You **mustn't** share these figures."* Sharing is **forbidden**;
   *don't have to* would only say it's *not necessary* (optional) — the opposite
   meaning, and a dangerous one in a board room.
2. ***had to*** — *"Last quarter we **had to** cut two pilots."* *must* has **no past
   form** at all; past obligation is always *had to*.
3. ***Does … have to*** — *"**Does** the CFO **have to** sign the budget…?"* The
   third-person *-s* rides on **does**, so *have to* stays **base**; *does … has to*
   would double the *-s*.

**Spaced review:**
- **(Unit 030 — might)** ***might*** — *"The vendor **might** renew on the current
  terms."* *might* names a **possibility**, not a plan, and like every modal it takes
  the **base verb** — *might renew*, never *might **to** renew*.
- **(Unit 013 — past continuous)** ***was presenting*** — *"At four yesterday I **was
  presenting** the AI roadmap when the CFO called."* The **past continuous**
  (*was/were + -ing*) freezes a past moment and shows the action **in progress**; the
  short *called* lands inside it.

**Recognition drill:**
1. ***Would you like to*** — *"**Would you like to** join us at the offsite?"* That's
   an **invitation** to this event; *Do you like to join…?* would merely ask whether
   she **enjoys** offsites in general — not an invitation at all.
2. ***Would you like me to*** — *"**Would you like me to** present the AI roadmap?"*
   English offers with *would like + me to*; *"Would you like that I…"* is a direct
   translation of *"gostaria que eu…"* and isn't English.
3. ***"I'd like…"*** — same request, but *would like* adds **courtesy and distance**,
   which is the right register with a board chair; *"I want…"* can read as a blunt
   order in writing.
4. **Add *to*** — *"I'd like **to** propose a new pilot."* Before a verb, *would
   like* always needs **to**; *"I'd like propose"* drops the required infinitive
   marker.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

**A regra e quando usar.** *would like* é o jeito **educado** de dizer *want* — um
desejo, uma oferta ou um convite, suavizado para a sala. Equivale ao *"gostaria
de"*: *"Gostaria de propor um piloto"* → *"I**'d like to** propose a pilot."* Use
com o C-Level, por escrito, em qualquer situação diplomática: *"I want the
forecast"* pode soar como ordem; *"I'd like the forecast"* mantém o mesmo pedido,
mas com cortesia. Forma: **'d like to + verbo base** (*I'd like to review*) ou **'d
like + substantivo** (*I'd like your feedback*); pergunta/oferta invertendo o
*would* (*Would you like to…?*), sem *do/does*.

**As armadilhas.** (1) *would like* ≠ *like*. *would like* = desejo **agora / desta
vez**; *like* = gosto **em geral**. *"Would you like to join?"* é um **convite**;
*"Do you like to join?"* pergunta se a pessoa **gosta** disso — sentido trocado.
(2) Oferecer-se para fazer algo é *would like + me to*, nunca *"that I"*: *"gostaria
que eu apresentasse?"* → *"**Would you like me to** present?"*, jamais *"Would you
like that I present?"*. (3) Antes de verbo, sempre *to*: *"I'd like **to**
propose"*, nunca *"I'd like propose"*. Se obrigação vs pedido/oferta ainda embolar,
releia `lessons/2026-08-16-unit-035.md` (have to) e
`lessons/2026-08-15-unit-034.md` (should).
