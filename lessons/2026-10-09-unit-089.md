### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Do you present to the board often?
> **You:** I **usually present** the AI roadmap myself. The CFO **is always** in the room, and she **always
> asks** about ROI first.
> **CEO:** And this quarter's budget?
> **You:** We **have already submitted** it. The board **will probably approve** it next week — they **have
> never rejected** a proposal we've prepped this carefully.
> **CFO:** I **also want** the risk section expanded before then.
> **You:** Noted. I **can also add** a scenario view by Friday.

Listen to *where* the little adverbs land before any rule. They don't go at the end and they don't go after the
object — they sit **in the middle, glued to the verb**. But the exact slot shifts with the verb: before a
**plain verb** (*usually **present***, *always **asks***), but **after** *is / are / was* (*is **always***), and
**after the first auxiliary** when there's more than one verb (*have **already** submitted*, *will **probably**
approve*, *can **also** add*). Yesterday's adverbs (*carefully, well*) went to the **end**; today's (*always,
never, usually, probably, also, already*) go to the **middle**. That seam is the whole lesson.

---

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

These "middle" adverbs — **always, never, usually, often, sometimes, also, probably, already, still, just** (and
**all / both**) — tell you *how often*, *how certain*, or *in addition*. They take a **fixed middle slot**, and
the slot moves by one simple test: **how many verbs are there?**

| The verb is… | Adverb goes… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **one plain verb** | **before** the verb | *I **always** check the numbers.* |
| **be** (am/is/are/was/were) | **after** *be* | *She **is always** prepared.* |
| **two+ verbs** (auxiliary + main) | **after the first** (auxiliary) | *We **have already** approved it.* · *It **will probably** pass.* |

- ✅ *I **always** go to the C-level sync.* — ❌ *I go always to the C-level sync.* (plain verb → adverb before)
- ✅ *She **is never** late.* — ❌ *She **never is** late.* (with *be* → adverb after)
- ✅ *We **have never** missed a deadline.* — ❌ *We never have missed… / We have missed never…* (aux + main →
  after the auxiliary, before the main verb)

**all / both follow the same slots:** *We **are all** tired.* · *They **have both** left.* · *We **all** agree.*

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these adverbs sit in the middle?** Because they modify the **whole action**, not the object — they
colour *the verb itself* (how often it happens, how sure you are, whether it's added on). English reserves a
**fixed mid-sentence slot** for exactly this meaning. Put them at the end and you either change the meaning or
sound translated. Mastering this slot is what lets you say *"the board **will probably** approve"* or *"she
**always** asks about ROI"* the way a native does — effortlessly, without the adverb drifting.

**Decision rule vs the rival — yesterday's manner adverbs (Unit 088).** This is the key contrast. Two families
of adverb, two different homes:

| Adverb type | Examples | Home | Sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Manner** (*how?*) | carefully, well, quickly, clearly | **END** (after the object) | *We review the budget **carefully**.* |
| **Frequency / certainty / adding** (*how often / how sure / also?*) | always, never, usually, probably, also | **MIDDLE** (before main verb / after *be*/aux) | *We **always** review the budget.* |

Minimal set — watch both land in one sentence, each in its own slot:
- *We review the budget **carefully**.* (manner → end)
- *We **always** review the budget.* (frequency → middle)
- *We **always** review the budget **carefully**.* ✅ (middle **and** end — both at once, never swapped)

**The *also* vs *too* rival.** Both mean "in addition," but they live in different slots:
- **also** → the middle slot: *"I **also** lead the innovation portfolio."*
- **too** → the end: *"I lead the innovation portfolio **too**."*
- ❌ *"I lead **also** the innovation portfolio."* (also can't sit after the verb like that) and don't double it:
  pick *also* **or** *too*, not both.

**PT→EN mapping — where Portuguese loosens what English fixes:**
- *sempre / nunca*: Portuguese is flexible (*"vou sempre"* / *"sempre vou"*). English is **not** — plain verb →
  *always* **before** it: *"I **always** go,"* never *"I go always."*
- *também*: in Portuguese it usually trails at the end (*"eu quero isso também"*). In English, *also* goes
  **middle** (*"I **also** want that"*) or you switch to *too* at the end (*"I want that **too**"*) — but not
  *"I want also that."*
- *provavelmente*: *"provavelmente vamos aprovar"* → *"we'**ll probably** approve"* — *probably* **after** the
  auxiliary *will*.
- *já* (already): *"já aprovamos o orçamento"* → *"we **have already** approved the budget"* — *already* **after**
  the auxiliary *have*, before the main verb.

**Top Brazilian-speaker errors:**
1. ❌ *"I go always to the board meetings."* → ✅ *"I **always** go to the board meetings."* A plain verb takes
   the adverb **before** it — *always go*, never *go always* (Portuguese *vou sempre* leaks through here).
2. ❌ *"She always is prepared for tough questions."* → ✅ *"She **is always** prepared…"* With *be*, the adverb
   goes **after** it — *is always*, not *always is*. (The *be* verb flips the slot.)
3. ❌ *"We have approved already the AI budget."* → ✅ *"We **have already** approved the AI budget."* With two
   verbs, the adverb slots **between** them — after the auxiliary *have*, before the main verb *approved*. Same
   trap with *also*: ❌ *"I want also the risk section"* → ✅ *"I **also** want the risk section."*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **The CFO is always in the room when we present the AI budget.**
   *— with `be` (`is`), the adverb goes right **after** it: `is always`. "always is" is the classic flip.*
2. **We have already submitted the proposal to the board.**
   *— two verbs (`have` + `submitted`) → `already` slots **between** them, after the auxiliary. Not "submitted
   already."*
3. **The board will probably approve the roadmap next week.**
   *— `probably` (certainty) after the auxiliary `will`, before the main verb `approve`. Place ("the roadmap")
   and time ("next week") still follow yesterday's rule at the end.*
4. **I usually present the strategy to the C-level myself.**
   *— a single plain verb (`present`) → the frequency adverb goes **before** it: `usually present`, never
   "present usually."*
5. **They have never rejected a proposal we prepped this carefully.**
   *— aux + main (`have` + `rejected`) → `never` after the auxiliary. (And `carefully` sits at the END — the
   manner adverb from Unit 088, different slot.)*
6. **I can also add a scenario view before Friday.**
   *— modal auxiliary `can` + `add` → `also` in between: `can also add`. "I want also" / "add also" would be
   wrong; `too` would go at the end instead.*
7. **We are all convinced the market is moving fast.** *(strategy / everyday blend)*
   *— `all` behaves like the middle adverbs: after `be` (`are all`). Compare "We all agree" — before a plain
   verb.*
8. **I often check my email before the first meeting of the day.** *(everyday)*
   *— plain verb (`check`) → `often` **before** it. "I check often my email" is the interference error.*

### 8. 🗣️ Para levar para a aula

- Ask your tutor to run a **"three slots" drill**: they give you an adverb (*always, never, usually, probably,
  already, also*) plus a sentence, and you place it correctly — deciding on the fly whether the verb is **plain**
  (adverb before: *"I **always** present"*), **be** (adverb after: *"she **is** always ready"*), or **auxiliary
  + main** (adverb between: *"we **have** already approved"*). Go fast and back to back until the slot is a
  reflex, so *"I go always"* and *"she always is"* feel physically wrong.
- Then a **merge-with-yesterday round**: the tutor hands you a verb + object + a frequency adverb + a manner
  adverb (*review / the budget / always / carefully*; *brief / the board / usually / clearly*) and you build the
  full sentence with each adverb in its own home — *"We **always** review the budget **carefully**."* This trains
  the middle-vs-end split that keeps your executive English sounding native, not translated.

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 088):**
1. ✅ *"We reviewed **the budget** carefully before the board saw it."* The manner adverb *carefully* was wedged
   between the verb and its object; **keep verb + object glued** (*reviewed the budget*), then the adverb at the
   end.
2. ✅ *"I like **this AI strategy** very much."* The object must sit next to the verb; *very much* (a degree
   phrase) waits until **after the object** — *"I like very much this…"* follows Portuguese order (*gosto muito
   de…*) and is the classic slip.
3. **in Berlin, last month** — *"We opened a new office **in Berlin last month**."* **Place before time**:
   *where* (in Berlin) first, *when* (last month) last.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 082 (*so / such*):** **such** — *"It's **such a** rare window that we have to move now."* The noun
   *window* (signalled by the article *a*) is what decides it: a noun after → *such*; an adjective alone would
   take *so*.
- **Unit 061 (reflexives & *each other*):** ✅ *"The CEO and CFO don't always agree with **each other** on pace,
   and I **feel** confident we'll align."* (1) The action is **reciprocal**, A ↔ B → *each other*, not
   *themselves* (which would mean each agrees only with their own self). (2) *sentir-se* is reflexive in
   Portuguese but English *feel* takes **no** reflexive — *"I feel myself confident"* is the interference error.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **before "check"** — *"I **always** check the numbers before a board call."* A single plain verb takes the
   frequency adverb **before** it; *"check always"* is the Portuguese-ordered error.
2. **(b)** *"We **always** review the forecast carefully."* Frequency adverb → **middle** (before the main verb);
   the manner adverb *carefully* stays at the **END**. (a) wrongly sends *always* to the end slot that belongs to
   *carefully* — two different homes.
3. **is always** — *"She **is always** prepared for tough questions."* With *be*, the adverb goes **after** it,
   not before; *be* is the one verb that flips the slot (*always is* is wrong).
4. ✅ *"We **have already** approved the AI budget, and I **also** want a scenario view."* Two fixes: *already*
   slots **between** the auxiliary and main verb (*have already approved*, not *approved already*), and *also*
   goes in the **middle** (*I also want*, not *want also* — or switch to *"a scenario view **too**"* at the end).

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Esta unidade trata da **posição dos advérbios do "meio"** — *always, never, usually, often, probably, also,
already, still, just* (e *all / both*). Diferente dos advérbios de modo de ontem (*carefully, well*), que vão no
**fim** da frase, estes ficam **no meio, colados ao verbo**. A regra é um teste de quantos verbos existem: (1)
**um verbo simples** → advérbio **antes** dele (*"I **always** go"*); (2) o verbo **be** (*is/are/was*) →
advérbio **depois** dele (*"she **is always** ready"*); (3) **auxiliar + verbo principal** → advérbio **entre os
dois**, depois do auxiliar (*"we **have already** approved,"* *"it **will probably** pass"*). O contraste com
ontem é o coração da lição: *"We **always** review the budget **carefully**"* — frequência no meio, modo no fim,
cada um na sua casa.

As armadilhas vêm da flexibilidade do português. *Sempre/nunca*: em português tanto faz (*vou sempre / sempre
vou*), mas em inglês com verbo simples é sempre **antes** — *"I always go,"* nunca *"I go always."* Com *be*, é
**depois** — *"she is always,"* nunca *"she always is."* *Também*: o português joga no fim (*"quero isso
também"*), mas o inglês usa *also* **no meio** (*"I also want that"*) ou troca por *too* no fim (*"I want that
too"*) — jamais *"I want also that."* E *já* vira *already* **depois do auxiliar**: *"we **have already**
approved."* Decore o gatilho: um verbo → advérbio antes; *be* ou auxiliar → advérbio depois (do primeiro verbo).
