### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** The vendor situation — remind me where we landed.
> **You:** The firm **we finally signed with** is the one **our architects pushed for**. The two we passed on — **neither of which** could guarantee uptime — are off the table.
> **CFO:** And the fund side?
> **You:** The €40M AI fund, **most of which** is already committed, covers the first two bets. The third is the pilot **I told you about** last week.
> **CEO:** The one the board was nervous about?
> **You:** That one. The directors, **all of whom** have now seen the revised model, are far more comfortable.
> **CFO:** Good. And the people **we'll depend on** to run it?
> **You:** Hired. The lead is the data scientist **(whom) we interviewed in September**.

Listen to where the little words land. *The firm **we signed with*** — the preposition *with* sits at the **end**, and the pronoun *that/who* has vanished. *The pilot **I told you about*** — same shape, *about* at the end. That's how real spoken English attaches a preposition to a relative clause. Then a second move: *the fund, **most of which** is committed* · *the directors, **all of whom** have seen it* — a quantifier (*most, all, none, some*) **plus** *of which / of whom* folds a whole extra fact into the noun. Today is those two moves — the everyday one and the boardroom one.

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

**Move 1 — a preposition inside the relative clause.** The verb or phrase needs a preposition (*sign **with**, invest **in**, depend **on**, talk **about**, pay **for**, deal **with**, report **to**, look **for**). You have two positions for it:

| Register | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural / spoken / most writing** | pronoun **dropped**, preposition at the **end** | the vendor **we signed with** · the fund **we invested in** · the issue **I told you about** |
| **Formal / written / careful** | **preposition + whom/which** at the front, pronoun kept | the vendor **with whom we signed** · the fund **in which we invested** |

- With a **person** → *whom* in the formal version (*with whom, to whom, for whom*). With a **thing** → *which* (*in which, on which, about which*). **Never** *with who*, **never** *with that* in the fronted pattern.
- The relaxed pattern (preposition at the end) is **not** an error — it is the default in modern English, spoken and written. The fronted pattern is the formal alternative, not the "correct" one.

**Move 2 — a quantifier + *of which / of whom*.** This only works in **non-defining** (extra-information) clauses — the kind with **commas** (Unit 098). It lets you say *how much / how many* of the noun something is true of:

| Quantifier | People → *of whom* | Things → *of which* |
|---|---|---|
| all / most / many / some / several / none / both / neither | the directors, **all of whom** approved | the funds, **most of which** are committed |

- Shape: **comma + quantifier + of + whom/which + verb**. *The board, **none of whom** objected, signed off.*
- Verb agreement follows the quantifier: *most of which **is/are**…*, *none of whom **has/have**…* (treat as you would *most of / none of* from Units 068–069).

**Fast test for Move 1:** say the clause as a full sentence and find the preposition's partner verb. *We signed **with** the vendor* → the vendor **we signed with**. If a verb drags a preposition behind it, that preposition belongs in the relative clause.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why do these structures exist?**
Both solve a problem relative clauses create. *Move 1*: many verbs you use all day are glued to a preposition — *depend on, invest in, deal with, report to, sign with*. When the noun you're describing is really the **object of that preposition**, English must park the preposition somewhere. The natural answer — leave it at the end — keeps the sentence fast and conversational. *Move 2* solves a different problem: you want to add a fact about **part** of a group without starting a new sentence. *"We shortlisted three vendors. None of them met the bar."* becomes one tight clause: *"the three vendors, **none of which** met the bar."* It's the executive's way of folding a verdict into the noun.

**Decision rule 1 — preposition at the end (default) vs fronted (formal):**
Ask: *who is listening, and how formal is this?*
- **Speaking, email, most writing → preposition at the end, drop the pronoun.** *the client **we're waiting on***, *the deal **they pulled out of***. This is the native default — use it by instinct.
- **Formal document, legal/board text, deliberate register → preposition + whom/which.** *the terms **under which** we invested*, *the colleagues **to whom** this reports*. Reserve it for weight; overused, it sounds stiff.

Minimal pair — same meaning, two registers:
- *the fund **we invested in*** (natural, 95% of the time)
- *the fund **in which we invested*** (formal, a board memo)
Both correct. What's **wrong** is mixing them: ❌ *the fund **in which we invested in*** (preposition used twice) and ❌ *the fund **in which we invested in it*** (resumptive pronoun).

**Decision rule 2 — *of which / of whom* vs just splitting the sentence:**
- Use **quantifier + of which/whom** when you want **one** smooth sentence and the fact is about **part** of the group: *the directors, **three of whom** are new, met on Friday.*
- If it feels heavy, **two sentences are always fine**: *The directors met on Friday. Three of them are new.* Never force the fancy version if clarity suffers — but in a written board recap, the folded version reads sharp.

**PT→EN mapping:**
- **Move 1, the end-position preposition has no Portuguese twin.** Portuguese cannot strand a preposition: *"o fundo **em que** investimos"* / *"o fundo **no qual** investimos"* — the preposition **must** come before the pronoun. So the natural English *"the fund we invested in"* feels wrong to a Brazilian ear, and Levi's instinct is to produce only the formal *"the fund in which we invested."* That's grammatical but sounds like a contract in casual talk. **Train the end-position version — it's the one that sounds native.**
- **Move 2 maps to *"dos quais / das quais / nenhum dos quais"***: *"os diretores, **todos os quais** aprovaram"* → *the directors, **all of whom** approved*; *"os fundos, **a maioria dos quais** está comprometida"* → *the funds, **most of which** are committed.* The structure exists in PT, so trust the mapping — just fix *which* (things) vs *whom* (people), a distinction Portuguese's single *os quais* erases.

**Top typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**

1. **Doubling the preposition** (keeping it fronted *and* at the end, or leaving a pronoun behind):
   - ❌ *the vendor **with whom** we signed **with*** · ❌ *the fund **which** we invested **in it***
   - ✅ *the vendor **we signed with*** (or formal: *the vendor **with whom we signed***) · ✅ *the fund **we invested in*** *(one preposition, no leftover pronoun)*
2. ***with who / to who* instead of *whom*** in the fronted pattern:
   - ❌ *the CFO **to who** I report* · ❌ *the partner **with who** we negotiated*
   - ✅ *the CFO **to whom** I report* · ✅ *the partner **with whom** we negotiated* *(preposition + person → always* whom*, never* who*,* that*, or* which*)*
3. **Using *of which / of whom* in a defining clause (no commas), or swapping which/whom:**
   - ❌ *three vendors **none of who** met the bar* · ❌ *the directors, **all of which** approved*
   - ✅ *three vendors, **none of whom** met the bar* (people → *whom*) · ✅ *the funds, **most of which** are committed* (things → *which*) *(and it needs the comma — it's extra information, Unit 098)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. The firm **we finally signed with** is the one our architects pushed for.
   *— Move 1, natural register: "sign with" → preposition at the end, pronoun dropped. This is how you'd actually say it in a meeting.*
2. The €40M AI fund, **most of which** is already committed, covers the first two bets.
   *— Move 2: quantifier "most" + "of which" (fund = thing). Commas → non-defining. Verb "is" agrees with "most of [singular fund]."*
3. The directors, **all of whom** have now seen the revised model, are far more comfortable.
   *— Move 2 with people → "of whom." Folds "all the directors have seen it" into the noun without a second sentence.*
4. The two vendors we passed on, **neither of which** could guarantee uptime, are off the table.
   *— Move 2: "neither of which" because they're things and there are exactly two. Note the already-negative "neither" → verb stays positive ("could").*
5. The CFO **to whom** this initiative reports wants a monthly readout.
   *— Move 1, formal register: fronted "to whom" because it's a person and the document is formal. In speech you'd say "the CFO this reports to."*
6. The pilot **I told you about** last week is finally funded.
   *— Move 1, the everyday default: "tell you about" → "about" at the end, "that" dropped. Trying to say "about which I told you" would sound oddly stiff here.*
7. The platform **we've built the whole strategy on** can't fail now.
   *— Move 1, everyday: "build on" → "on" stranded at the end. The formal twin "on which we've built the whole strategy" is correct but heavier.*
8. The neighbours **I share the lift with** every morning turned out to run a fintech.
   *— Everyday life, Move 1: "share with" → "with" at the end, pronoun dropped. Same mechanics as the boardroom version — it's one grammar, two worlds.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 100 — *-ing* / *-ed* clauses):**
1. ***approved*** — *the budget **approved** by the board.* Ask: *does the noun do the action or receive it?* The budget **receives** the approval (the board is the actor) → passive → *-ed*. *Approving* would wrongly mean the budget does the approving.
2. ***chose* → *chosen*** — *the strategy **chosen** last year.* An *-ed* clause needs the **past participle**, not the simple past. For irregular verbs the participle differs from the past: *chosen* (not *chose*), *taken* (not *took*), *shown* (not *showed*).
3. **No — you can't.** You may reduce only when there was a *who/that/which* **+ be** to delete. *"the deal that we closed"* is a plain one-time past action with no *be*, so it stays a full relative clause (you can drop *that*, giving *"the deal we closed"*, but not reduce further). *"the deal closing last week"* wrongly suggests it's **still in the act of closing** — wrong meaning.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 094:** ***guarantees*** — *"We won't commit the full budget **if** the vendor **doesn't guarantee** uptime."* The *if*-part stays in the **present** (here, present simple negative *doesn't guarantee*); *will* lives only in the result half (*we won't commit*). **Never** put *will* after *if* in a real condition — the Portuguese future subjunctive (*"se o fornecedor não **garantir**"*) is exactly what tempts you toward *if … will*, but that tense doesn't exist in English.
- **Unit 069:** ***none of them*** — *"I checked **three** shortlisted tools and **none of them** met the bar."* *Neither* is only for **exactly two**; with **three** (or more) you must use *none*. The number is the whole decision: two → *neither*, three-plus → *none*.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **❌ doubled preposition.** *with whom we negotiated **with*** uses *with* twice. Fix either way: formal *"The partner **with whom** we negotiated left the firm"* or natural *"The partner **we negotiated with** left the firm."* One *with*, never two.
2. ***none of whom*** — *The three directors, **none of whom** voted no, asked for a second review.* Directors are **people** → *whom* (not *which*); and the commas make it non-defining, which is exactly where *quantifier + of whom* belongs.
3. **(b) *the vendor we depend on* fits a strategy sync.** Both are grammatical, but the fronted *"on whom we depend"* is formal/written register and sounds stiff in speech. In a live meeting the natural, native default is the end-position preposition: *"the vendor we depend on most."* (Save *on whom* for the board memo.)
4. Two fixes: **(a) verb agreement** — *all of which* refers to the **three tools** (plural) → *were* rejected, not *was*; **(b)** the clause is fine as non-defining with the comma. Corrected: *"We shortlisted three tools, **all of which were** rejected by security."* (The core error is *was* → *were*; *all of which* itself is correct for three things.)

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Quando a oração relativa tem um verbo que pede preposição (*sign **with**, invest **in**, depend **on**, report **to**, talk **about***), o inglês natural deixa a preposição **no fim** e apaga o pronome: *the fund **we invested in*** , *the CFO **I report to***, *the pilot **I told you about***. Essa posição final **não existe em português** (*"o fundo **em que** investimos"* / *"no qual"* exige a preposição antes), então seu instinto é produzir só a versão formal *"the fund **in which** we invested"* — que está correta, mas soa como contrato na fala. **Treine a versão com preposição no fim: é a que soa nativa.** A versão *preposição + whom/which* (pessoa → *whom*, coisa → *which*) existe, mas reserve-a para textos formais e documentos de board.

O segundo movimento é *quantifier + of which / of whom*: para adicionar um fato sobre **parte** de um grupo numa só frase, sempre com **vírgulas** (oração não-definidora, Unit 098): *the directors, **all of whom** approved* · *the funds, **most of which** are committed* · *the two bids, **neither of which** met the bar.* Mapeia direto do português *"dos quais / nenhum dos quais"* — basta lembrar *which* para coisas e *whom* para pessoas, distinção que o *os quais* do português apaga. Cuidado com três armadilhas: **não dobre a preposição** (*with whom we signed ~~with~~*), **não deixe pronome sobrando** (*which we invested in ~~it~~*), e **nunca** use *with who / to who* — na frente, pessoa é sempre *whom*.
