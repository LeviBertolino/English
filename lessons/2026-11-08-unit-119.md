### 2. 🧠 Mini-context

> **CEO:** Who's presenting the AI roadmap to the board?
> **Levi:** Marta, **who** now runs strategy, will take the first half. The deck, **which** we rebuilt last week, leads with the three bets.
> **CFO:** And the budget line we argued about?
> **Levi:** Still there. The CEO approved it on Friday, **which** honestly surprised me.
> **CEO:** Remind me where we're piloting first.
> **Levi:** São Paulo, **where** we already have the data team, then we expand north.
> **CFO:** Good. One client I'd flag is Vextra, **whose** renewal is up in March.
> **Levi:** Noted — and they delayed their own rollout last quarter, **which** tells us something about their appetite.

Notice the **commas**. Every relative clause here is wrapped in commas (or sits after one), and every one is **extra information** — you could delete it and the main sentence still stands: *Marta will take the first half. The deck leads with the three bets.* The commas are doing real work: they say "this noun is already identified; here's a bonus fact about it." That's the whole difference from yesterday and the day before. And watch the last kind: *which honestly surprised me*, *which tells us something* — that *which* doesn't point at a noun at all. It points back at the **entire previous idea** (the CEO approving it; them delaying the rollout). Portuguese reaches for *o que* there — and that's exactly where the English *which* has to stand alone, after a comma, never *that*.

### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

A relative clause comes in **two flavours**. Units 117–118 were the first; today is the second.

| | **Defining** (Units 117–118) | **Non-defining** (today) |
|---|---|---|
| **Commas?** | No | **Yes** — always |
| **Job** | tells you *which one* (essential) | adds *extra info* (removable) |
| **Can use *that*?** | Yes | **No** — never |
| **Can drop the pronoun?** | Yes, if it's the object | **No** — never |
| **Pronouns** | who / which / that / whose / where | who / which / whose / where / whom |
| **Example** | *The client **who** complained left.* | *Vextra, **who** complained, left.* |

**Key facts:**
- **The comma changes the meaning, not just the punctuation.** *My colleague who speaks Mandarin will join* (defining — I have several colleagues; the one who speaks Mandarin) vs *My colleague, who speaks Mandarin, will join* (non-defining — I have one colleague in mind; by the way, she speaks Mandarin).
- **Non-defining clauses never take *that*.** ❌ *Marta, **that** runs strategy, …* → ✅ *Marta, **who** runs strategy, …*. Use *who* for people, *which* for things.
- **You can never drop the pronoun** in a non-defining clause, even when it's the object: *The deck, **which** we rebuilt, …* — the *which* must stay.
- **Sentence *which*** = *which* right after a comma, referring to the **whole clause before it**: *They delayed the launch, **which** nobody expected.* Here *which* ≠ the launch; it = "the fact that they delayed the launch." This *which* is only ever non-defining.
- **In speech, the comma is a tiny pause / pitch drop.** You can hear it: "Marta — who runs strategy — will present." If you can lift the clause out and the sentence still means the same person/thing, it's non-defining → commas.

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (THE core section)

**Why does this structure exist?** English has no other way to tag a **bonus fact** onto a noun that's *already* pinned down. Once everyone knows *which* thing you mean (a name, "the CEO", "our only data team"), any further description is extra — so English brackets it off with commas and forbids *that*. The commas are the signal: "don't use this to pick out the noun; the noun is already clear." Dropping them turns a comment into a filter and silently changes what you said.

**Decision rule vs the rival (defining, Units 117–118).** Ask: *does this clause tell the listener WHICH one, or just add a fact about one already known?*

| Sentence | Reading |
|---|---|
| *The engineers **who** quit took the IP.* (no commas) | **only** the engineers who quit — defines a subset |
| *The engineers, **who** quit, took the IP.* (commas) | **all** the engineers quit — and they took the IP |
| *Our vendor **that** missed the SLA is on notice.* | we have several vendors; *this* one |
| *Our vendor, **which** missed the SLA, is on notice.* | we have one vendor; by the way it missed the SLA |

Same words, opposite facts. With a **proper name** (Marta, Vextra, São Paulo) it's almost always non-defining — a name already identifies, so what follows can only be extra: *Marta, who runs strategy, …*

**PT→EN mapping.** Portuguese marks this too — *O relatório**,** que reescrevemos**,** foi aprovado* (commas) vs *O relatório que reescrevemos foi aprovado* (no commas). The trap is that Portuguese uses **the same word *que*** in both, and Brazilian writing is far more relaxed about commas, so you (a) carry *que* straight over as English *that* in the comma version (❌ *The report, that we rewrote, …*) and (b) drop the commas that English treats as meaning-bearing. The second trap is **sentence-*which***: Portuguese says *o que* (*Ele aprovou a verba, **o que** me surpreendeu*). You translate *o que* as *what* (❌ *…, **what** surprised me*) — but English uses a bare *which* here.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *The board, **that** met on Friday, approved it.* → ✅ *The board, **which** met on Friday, approved it.* — *that* is banned after a comma; use *which / who*.
- ❌ *He approved the budget, **what** surprised everyone.* → ✅ *He approved the budget, **which** surprised everyone.* — sentence-*which* is *which*, never *what* (and never *that*).
- ❌ *My manager who is based in Lisbon wants the deck* (meaning: I have one manager) → ✅ *My manager, who is based in Lisbon, wants the deck.* — missing commas turn "my (only) manager" into "the one of my managers who…". Add the commas or you've implied several managers.

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. **Marta, who now runs strategy, will present the roadmap.**
   *— non-defining: the name "Marta" already identifies her, so "who now runs strategy" can only be extra → commas, and `who` (never `that`).*
2. **The deck, which we rebuilt last week, leads with the three bets.**
   *— object pronoun inside a comma clause, so it **cannot** be dropped: `which we rebuilt`, not "we rebuilt". Defining would allow the drop; non-defining never does.*
3. **The CEO approved the budget on Friday, which honestly surprised me.**
   *— sentence-`which`: it refers to the whole fact (the approval), not to a noun. This is where Portuguese "o que" lives; English uses a bare `which` after the comma.*
4. **Vextra, whose renewal is up in March, is our top retention risk.**
   *— `whose` works in non-defining clauses too; the name "Vextra" is already specific, so the possession fact is bonus info → commas.*
5. **We're piloting in São Paulo, where we already have a data team.**
   *— `where` for a place, non-defining: the city is named, so "where we already have a data team" just explains why — extra, comma'd, keep `where`.*
6. **They delayed their own rollout, which tells us a lot about their appetite.**
   *— another sentence-`which`: "which" = "the fact that they delayed it." Compare #4's `which`, which pointed at a thing; this one points at an event.*
7. **My sister, who lives in Porto, is visiting next week.** *(everyday)*
   *— you have one sister, so her location is extra → commas + `who`. Drop the commas and you'd absurdly imply you're picking out one sister among many.*
8. **The restaurant, which only opened in May, is already fully booked.** *(everyday)*
   *— one specific restaurant in mind; "which only opened in May" is colour, not a filter → commas, `which` not `that`, pronoun kept.*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 118):**
1. *whose* — *"The client **whose** contract we lost…"*. *whose* replaces *who his*, and a **noun** (the thing owned: *contract*) comes directly after it — no *the* in between.
2. *"the advisor **we report to**"* — the preposition *to* moves to the **end** (stranded), and *whom* **disappears** entirely. This is how it's actually said in speech; *to whom* is the formal written form.
3. *when* — *"2026 was the year **when** we pivoted."* English uses *when* for a **time**; *where* is only for a **place**. Portuguese *onde* stretches over times, which is why *where* feels tempting.

**Spaced review:**
- *Unit 112:* *"The team isn't **big enough**…"* — *enough* goes **after** an adjective (*big enough*, not *enough big*). *not … enough* signals **less than needed** — the team is below the size required to integrate the product.
- *Unit 090:* *"Has the CFO signed off on the spend **yet**?"* — *yet* at the **end** of a neutral status question. *already* would change the meaning to surprise ("signed off *so soon*?!"), which isn't what a neutral "is it done?" asks.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **(b)** — commas mark *who sits on the board* as **extra** info about your one-and-only CFO. Version (a), with no commas, implies you have several CFOs and you're picking the one on the board.
2. *"The partnership, **which** we signed in Q1, is already paying off."* — *that* is banned after a comma; a non-defining clause uses *which* (things) or *who* (people).
3. **which** — *"…, **which** gave the team breathing room."* This is sentence-*which*: it refers to the whole act of pushing the launch, not to a noun. *that* can't start a comma clause, and *what* is never used for this (that's the Portuguese *o que* interference).
4. **Commas are missing** — *"The engineers**,** who quit**,** took the source code."* As written (no commas), it's defining and implies **only some** engineers quit (and those are the ones who took the code). To mean **all** of them quit, you must add the commas.

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Toda oração relativa vem em **dois sabores**. O que você já viu (Units 117–118) é a **defining** (sem vírgula): ela diz *qual* coisa e é essencial — *The client who complained* = justamente aquele que reclamou. Hoje é a **non-defining** (com vírgula): o substantivo **já está identificado** (um nome próprio, "o CEO", "nosso único time de dados") e a oração só **acrescenta uma informação extra** que você poderia apagar sem quebrar a frase — *Marta, who runs strategy, …*. A vírgula não é enfeite: ela **muda o sentido**. E existe um *which* especial que, depois de vírgula, se refere à **ideia inteira** anterior, não a um substantivo — *He approved it, which surprised me* (o fato de ele aprovar).

As armadilhas são todas de interferência do português. Primeiro: em oração com vírgula, **nunca use *that*** — é *who* (pessoas) ou *which* (coisas); seu instinto de traduzir *que* como *that* te trai aqui. Segundo: o *which* que remete à frase toda é o lugar do *o que* português, mas em inglês é um *which* sozinho — **nunca *what*, nunca *that***. Terceiro: não **esqueça as vírgulas** — "My manager, who lives in Lisbon" (tenho um só gestor) vira "My manager who lives in Lisbon" (implica vários gestores, e você fala de um). Em inglês a vírgula faz diferença de significado; no português escrito você é mais solto com ela, e é aí que escorrega. Reler: Unit 112 (*enough/too*) e Unit 090 (*still/yet/already*).
