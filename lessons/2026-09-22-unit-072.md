### 3. 📐 A regra (form)

A **relative clause** adds information right after a noun to say *which* person or thing you mean. It
starts with a **relative pronoun** (*who / that / which*) that stands in for the noun and does a job
inside the clause. In this unit the relative pronoun is the **subject** of its own verb.

| Points back to | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a **person** | **who** (or *that*) | the leader **who** decides |
| a **thing / idea** | **which** (or *that*) | the report **which / that** convinced them |
| either, in a defining clause | **that** | the person / report **that**… |

**Two non-negotiables here:**
- The relative pronoun comes **right after** the noun it describes: *the leader **who** decides* (not
  *the leader decides **who***).
- When the pronoun is the **subject** (it does the verb of the clause), you **cannot** drop it:
  *the report ~~convinced~~ **that** convinced them* — the word *that/who/which* must stay.

---

### 4. 🎯 Quando usar — e quando não (a seção central)

**Why does this structure exist?** It lets you glue a describing idea onto a noun without stopping to
start a second sentence. Compare: *"We backed a vendor. The vendor scored highest on security."* →
*"We backed the vendor **that** scored highest on security."* One breath, and the clause **identifies
which** vendor — that's a **defining** relative clause (no commas, essential information).

**Decision rule vs the rivals:**

- **who vs which — animacy decides.** A **person** takes **who**; a **thing/idea** takes **which**.
  Getting this wrong is the #1 tell of a non-native speaker.
  - *the strategist **who** framed the thesis* — a person → **who**.
  - *the strategy **which** framed our year* — a thing → **which**.
- **that vs who/which — register, not grammar.** In a **defining** clause, **that** is correct for
  **both** people and things and is the most natural in speech: *the vendor **that** scored highest*.
  Prefer **who** when you want to foreground the person, and **which** when the tone is formal (a board
  memo). All three are fine; **that** is the safe default.
- **subject (this unit) vs object (next unit).** Here the pronoun **does the verb** → keep it. Next
  unit covers cases where the pronoun is the *object* and can vanish (*the vendor we chose*) — don't
  drop anything yet.

**PT→EN mapping:** Portuguese collapses everything into **que** — *a pessoa **que** decide*, *o
relatório **que** convenceu*, *tudo **que** importa*. English **splits** that one word three ways:
**who** (people), **which** (things), **that** (both, defining). The direct translation *que → that*
often works, **but** two habits betray Levi:
- reaching for **which** on a person (*"the vendor which…"*) because *which* "feels" like *que*;
- using **what** for *que* after words like *everything / all / the thing* (*"everything **what**
  I said"*) — English never uses *what* as a relative pronoun after a noun.

**Top 3 typical Brazilian-speaker errors:**
- ❌ *"The vendor **which** presented lost the deal."* → ✅ *"The vendor **who / that** presented lost
  the deal."* *(a vendor is people → who/that, never which.)*
- ❌ *"Everything **what** we promised the board is on track."* → ✅ *"Everything **that** we promised
  the board is on track."* *("tudo que" → everything **that**, never what.)*
- ❌ *"The CFO **who she** owns the numbers disagreed."* → ✅ *"The CFO **who** owns the numbers
  disagreed."* *(don't add "she" — "who" is already the subject; the doubling comes from "o CFO que
  ela…".)*

### 5. ✏️ 8 annotated examples

1. *"She's the strategist **who** framed our AI-first thesis."*
   *— a person as subject → **who**; the clause tells the board **which** strategist you mean.*
2. *"The report **that** changed the board's mind ran only six pages."*
   *— a thing as subject → **that** (or which). It's the subject of "changed", so it can't be dropped.*
3. *"We backed the vendor **which** scored highest on our security review."*
   *— thing → **which**; the formal register suits a written recommendation to the CFO.*
4. *"A leader **who** can't explain the trade-offs won't get the budget."*
   *— person → **who**; defining (it says which kind of leader), so no commas.*
5. *"The pilot **that** missed its KPI is the one we're shutting down."*
   *— **that** singles out which pilot among several; drop it and "missed its KPI" loses its subject.*
6. *"Everything **that** matters to the CFO is in the one-page appendix."*
   *— after everything/all/the thing → **that**, never "what"; this is the classic "tudo que" trap.*
7. *"My neighbour **who** works in fintech gave me the tip."*
   *— everyday life: a person as subject → **who**; identifies which neighbour.*
8. *"This is the app **that** crashes every time I open it."*
   *— everyday: a thing as subject → **that**; keep it, because it does the verb "crashes".*

### 9. ✅ Gabarito

**Warm-up (Unit 070):**
1. **all** — *data* is **uncountable**, and *every* needs a **singular countable** noun; so *"all the
   data points…"* (mass noun, singular verb). *"Every data"* is impossible.
2. Both **"the whole quarter"** and **"all the quarter" / "all quarter"** work — one entire single
   quarter. *"Whole quarters"* turns it plural (several entire quarters, a recurring pattern), which
   changes the meaning from *this one quarter*.
3. **"Every one of them"** cleared the check (two words = each individual member) vs **"Everyone"**
   agreed (one word = everybody). Different meanings, different spellings.

**Spaced review:**
- **Unit 065:** *"Would you like **some** feedback?"* — **some**. Even though it's a question, an
  **offer / request** assumes the thing exists, so the some/any rule flips to *some*.
- **Unit 044:** *"I need **you to lead** the roadmap."* — English uses **need + object + to**;
  *"preciso que você…"* must **never** become *"I need that you lead"*.

**Recognition drill:**
1. **who** — a *consultant* is a **person**; *which* is only for things.
2. **"Everything that we committed to is still on track."** — after *everything*, use **that**, never
   *what* (the *"tudo que"* interference).
3. **that** — a relative pronoun can follow the noun *strategy*; **what** cannot ever follow a noun as
   a relative pronoun (*"the strategy what"* is impossible).
4. Delete **she**: *"The CFO **who** owns the numbers pushed back hard."* — *who* is already the
   subject; adding *she* double-marks it (the *"que ela"* habit).

### 10. 🇧🇷 Resumo em português

Uma **relative clause** (oração relativa) gruda uma informação num substantivo para dizer **qual**
pessoa ou coisa você quer, sem começar outra frase: *"the vendor **that** scored highest"*. A regra de
ouro é a **animacidade**: pessoa → **who**, coisa/ideia → **which**, e **that** serve para os dois em
orações **defining** (sem vírgula, informação essencial) — na fala, *that* é a escolha mais natural.
Neste ponto o pronome é o **sujeito** do verbo da oração, então ele **não pode ser omitido**: *"the
report **that** convinced them"*, nunca *"the report convinced them"* no meio da frase.

A armadilha central para brasileiros é o **que**, que em português vira *who*, *which* e *that* ao mesmo
tempo. Evite três deslizes: (1) usar **which para gente** (*"the vendor which…"* → use *who/that*);
(2) usar **what depois de um substantivo** (*"everything what…"* → sempre *that*); e (3) **repetir o
sujeito** (*"the CFO who **she** owns…"* → só *who*, porque ele já é o sujeito). Traduzir *que → that*
costuma funcionar, mas na dúvida entre pessoa e coisa, deixe o *who* para as pessoas e o *which/that*
para as coisas.
